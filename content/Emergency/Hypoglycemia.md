# 🩸 Hypoglycemia (ภาวะน้ำตาลในเลือดต่ำ)

> "Hypoglycemia is a metabolic emergency defined by a clinical state of abnormally low plasma glucose that exposes the subject to potential harm. It is characterized by autonomic and neuroglycopenic symptoms that resolve rapidly upon glucose administration."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Glucose is the primary obligate fuel source for the brain. The central nervous system cannot synthesize or store glucose. Severe hypoglycemia causes rapid neuroglycopenic brain injury, leading to seizures, status epilepticus, irreversible cognitive impairment, focal stroke-like deficits, coma, and brain death. Autonomic activation can precipitate myocardial infarction or fatal cardiac arrhythmias (QT prolongation).

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess patency. A comatose or actively convulsing hypoglycemic patient cannot protect their airway.
* Secure the airway if the patient is deeply comatose (GCS $<8$) and does not respond rapidly to IV glucose.

### Breathing
* Look for normal respiratory expansion. Bradypnea may occur in profound coma.
* Measure $SpO_2$.

### Circulation
* **Assess Vitals**: Look for tachycardia, hypertension (sympathetic surge), or bradycardia (severe).
* **Identify Autonomic Signs**: Profuse **diaphoresis** (sweating), cool pale skin, and visible [[Tremor]].
* **Fingerstick Blood Glucose (FSBS)**: Perform a bedside fingerstick glucose test immediately on any patient presenting with altered consciousness, confusion, seizure, or focal neurological deficits. **Do not wait for formal lab glucose results.**
* **Establish IV Access**: Insert a peripheral line immediately.

---

## 🔍 Recognition (Whipple's Triad & Symptoms)

Hypoglycemia diagnosis is based on the classic **Whipple's Triad**:
1. **Symptoms** consistent with hypoglycemia.
2. **Documented low plasma glucose** concentration (typically $< 70$ mg/dL for clinical concern; severe neuroglycopenia occurs $< 54$ mg/dL).
3. **Resolution of symptoms** once glucose levels are restored.

```
Hypoglycemia Symptom Categories
└── 1. Autonomic (Adrenergic / Cholinergic) - Occurs first (Glucose < 55-60 mg/dL)
    ├── Palpitations, Tachycardia, Anxiety, Tremulousness
    └── Diaphoresis (Sweating), Hunger, Paresthesia
└── 2. Neuroglycopenic (Brain sugar starvation) - Occurs later (Glucose < 50 mg/dL)
    ├── Confusion, Aberrant behavior, Irritability
    ├── Visual disturbances (diplopia), Dizziness
    └── Seizures, Focal deficits (stroke mimic), Coma
```

---

## 🦠 Causes

```
Hypoglycemia Etiologies
└── 1. Medication-Induced (Most common in diabetics)
    ├── Insulin overdose or mismatch with meals
    └── Sulfonylureas (Glipizide, Glimepiride, Glibenclamide)
└── 2. Organ Failure
    ├── Severe Hepatic Failure (impaired gluconeogenesis/glycogenolysis)
    ├── Renal Failure (decreased clearance of insulin/sulfonylureas)
    └── Sepsis (hypermetabolic state, cytokine-induced inhibition)
└── 3. Endocrine Deficiencies
    ├── Adrenal Insufficiency (Cortisol deficiency)
    └── Hypopituitarism
└── 4. Endogenous Hyperinsulinism
    └── Insulinoma (Beta-cell tumor) or post-bariatric reactive hypoglycemia
└── 5. Toxin / Other
    └── Severe ethanol binge without food (blocks gluconeogenesis)
```

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Clinical Features | Key Investigations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Hypoglycemia** | Diaphoresis, tremor, altered consciousness, rapid resolution with glucose. | Fingerstick glucose $<70$ mg/dL. |
| **Acute Ischemic Stroke** | Focal deficits (hemiparesis, aphasia). **Does not resolve with IV glucose.** | Normal glucose, Brain CT. |
| **[[Seizure]] / Postictal State** | History of tonic-clonic movements, urinary incontinence. Normal glucose. | Normal glucose, EEG. |
| **Panic Attack** | Palpitations, sweating, anxiety. Patient is alert and oriented. | Normal glucose, normal ECG. |

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Fingerstick Capillary Blood Glucose**: Bedside diagnostic standard.
* **Venous Blood Glucose**: Confirmatory laboratory test (draw tube *before* administering IV glucose, but do not wait for results to treat).
* **The Diagnostic Critical Sample** (if evaluating unexplained hypoglycemia in non-diabetics):
  * Draw blood during the hypoglycemic episode ($glucose < 55$ mg/dL) for: **Insulin, C-peptide, Proinsulin, and Sulfonylurea screen**.
    * *Insulin high, C-peptide low* $\rightarrow$ Exogenous insulin administration (factitious).
    * *Insulin high, C-peptide high* $\rightarrow$ Sulfonylurea ingestion or Insulinoma.
* **Renal and Hepatic Panels**: Check Creatinine, AST, ALT.
* **Adrenal Panel**: Check morning Cortisol (rule out adrenal crisis).

## 🏥 Immediate Management

Dosed based on the patient's level of consciousness:

### 1. Conscious Patient (Able to swallow safely):
* **Apply the "Rule of 15"**:
  1. Give **15 grams of fast-acting, simple carbohydrates** orally:
     - $150$ mL of fruit juice or regular soda (non-diet) OR
     - $3 - 4$ glucose tablets or 3 teaspoons of sugar dissolved in water.
  2. Wait **15 minutes** and recheck fingerstick glucose.
  3. If glucose remains $< 70$ mg/dL, give another 15g of simple carbs and recheck in 15 minutes.
  4. Once glucose is $> 70$ mg/dL, have the patient eat a meal containing complex carbohydrates and protein (e.g., rice, sandwich) to prevent recurrent drop.

### 2. Unconscious Patient / Unable to swallow safely:
* **Establish IV Access**:
  * **Option A (Standard)**: Administer **50 - 100 mL of 10% Dextrose (D10W)** as an IV bolus (or **25 - 50 mL of 50% Dextrose (D50W)**).
    * *Safety Note*: D50W is highly hypertonic and can cause severe tissue necrosis and thrombophlebitis if extravasation occurs. D10W is preferred, especially in peripheral lines.
* **No IV Access (Pre-hospital or ward delay)**:
  * Administer **Glucagon 1 mg IM or SC** (adult).
    * *Mechanism*: Mobilizes hepatic glycogen stores.
    * *Limitation*: Ineffective in malnourished patients, alcoholics, or patients with liver cirrhosis due to depleted glycogen stores.

---

## 🏥 Definitive & Sulfonylurea Management

* **Sulfonylurea-Induced Hypoglycemia**:
  * Sulfonylureas have long half-lives and active metabolites that continually stimulate beta-cells to secrete insulin. Hypoglycemia will recur.
  * **Admit the patient** to the hospital. Start a continuous infusion of **10% Dextrose (D10W)** at $50-100$ mL/h.
  * **Octreotide Therapy**:
    * If hypoglycemia recurs despite D10W infusion, administer **Octreotide 50 - 100 $\mu$g SC (or IV) every 6 to 8 hours**.
    * *Mechanism*: Somatostatin analog that directly blocks calcium channels in pancreatic beta-cells, suppressing insulin release.

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Blood Glucose**: Recheck every 15-30 minutes after bolus therapy until stable ($> 100$ mg/dL), then check hourly.
* **Mental Status**: Ensure GCS recovers to 15.
* **Electrolytes**: Monitor potassium (glucose infusion shifts potassium intracellularly).

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Octreotide for Sulfonylureas**: Giving repeated boluses of D50W to a patient with sulfonylurea toxicity actually triggers a paradox: the sudden surge in glucose stimulates the hyper-sensitized beta-cells to secrete even more insulin, causing severe rebound hypoglycemia. Use a continuous D10W infusion and **Octreotide** to turn off insulin secretion.
> **Beta-Blocker Hypoglycemia Unawareness**: Beta-blockers block the beta-1 and beta-2 adrenergic receptors. If a patient is on a beta-blocker, they will not experience the warning autonomic signs of hypoglycemia (palpitations, tremor, anxiety). The only sign they may display is **sweating** (which is mediated by cholinergic pathways, not adrenergic).
