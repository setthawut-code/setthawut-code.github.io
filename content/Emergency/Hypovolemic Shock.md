# 🩸 Hypovolemic Shock (ช็อกจากการขาดน้ำหรือเสียเลือด)

> "Hypovolemic shock is a life-threatening emergency caused by a critical decrease in intravascular volume. This leads to reduced venous return (preload), decreased cardiac output, and systemic tissue hypoperfusion. It is divided into hemorrhagic and non-hemorrhagic etiologies."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Severe hypovolemia triggers intense compensatory vasoconstriction to maintain perfusion to the brain and heart. However, prolonged splanchnic and renal vasoconstriction leads to ischemic gut injury and renal failure. If the volume deficit is not rapidly corrected, progressive cardiovascular collapse and death occur.

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess and secure airway. If the patient has altered consciousness due to severe hemorrhagic shock, protect the airway from aspiration.

### Breathing
* Look for tachypnea (respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis).
* Provide high-flow oxygen via non-rebreather mask to maximize hemoglobin oxygen saturation.

### Circulation
* **Assess Vitals**: Look for hypotension (systolic BP $< 90$ mmHg, MAP $< 65$ mmHg) and tachycardia (earliest compensatory sign).
* **Assess Skin**: Cool, pale, clammy skin with delayed capillary refill ($>3$ seconds) and cold extremities.
* **Assess Jugular Veins**: Flat neck veins (JVP is not visible, even when supine).
* **Obtain Large-Bore IV Access**: Insert two **14G or 16G peripheral IV lines** in the antecubital fossae immediately. If peripheral access is impossible, establish an intraosseous (IO) line or a central venous line (using a large-bore cordis catheter).

---

## 🔍 Recognition & ATLS Hemorrhagic Shock Classification

Hemorrhagic shock severity is categorized into four classes based on the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) framework:

| Feature | Class I | Class II | Class III | Class IV |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blood Loss (mL)** | $< 750$ mL | $750 - 1500$ mL | $1500 - 2000$ mL | $> 2000$ mL |
| **Blood Loss (%)** | $< 15\%$ | $15 - 30\%$ | $30 - 40\%$ | $> 40\%$ |
| **Heart Rate** | $< 100$ bpm | $> 100$ bpm | **$> 120$ bpm** | **$> 140$ bpm** |
| **Blood Pressure** | Normal | Normal | **Decreased** | **Decreased** |
| **Pulse Pressure** | Normal | **Decreased** | Decreased | Decreased |
| **Respiratory Rate** | $14-20$ breaths/min| $20-30$ breaths/min| $30-40$ breaths/min| $> 35$ breaths/min |
| **Mental Status** | Slightly anxious | Mildly anxious | Anxious, confused | Lethargic, obtundation|
| **Fluid of Choice** | Crystalloid | Crystalloid | **Crystalloid + Blood** | **Massive Transfusion** |

*Clinical Pearl: Blood pressure remains normal in Class I and II shock due to compensatory vasoconstriction. A drop in blood pressure indicates Class III shock (loss of $>30\%$ intravascular volume).*

## 🦠 Causes

```
Hypovolemic Shock Etiology
└── 1. Hemorrhagic (Blood Loss)
    ├── Trauma (Internal or external bleeding)
    ├── Gastrointestinal: Variceal hemorrhage, bleeding peptic ulcer
    ├── Obstetric: Ruptured ectopic pregnancy, postpartum hemorrhage
    └── Vascular: Ruptured Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)
└── 2. Non-Hemorrhagic (Fluid Loss)
    ├── Gastrointestinal: Severe vomiting, diarrhea (cholera)
    ├── Renal: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (osmotic diuresis), adrenal crisis
    ├── Skin: Severe burns, heat stroke
    └── Third-Spacing: Pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, peritonitis
```

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

* **Hemorrhagic Shock**: Evident bleeding, trauma history, drop in hematocrit.
* **Non-Hemorrhagic Shock**: Dehydration signs, history of fluid loss, high hematocrit/hemoglobin (due to hemoconcentration).
* **[[Septic Shock]]**: Warm extremities (early), fever, source of infection (pneumonia, UTI), low systemic vascular resistance.
* **[[Cardiogenic Shock]]**: Elevated JVP, bilateral crackles, history of cardiac disease.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Immediate Labs**:
  * **Type and Crossmatch**: The most critical test in hemorrhagic shock. Order type O-negative blood immediately if crossmatched blood is unavailable.
  * **Hematocrit and Hemoglobin**: *Caution: Initial Hgb/Hct may be normal in acute hemorrhage before hemodilution occurs.*
  * **Coagulation Profile (PT, INR, PTT, Fibrinogen)**.
  * **Serum Lactate & ABG**: Monitor tissue hypoperfusion and acidosis.
  * **Renal Function & Electrolytes**: Look for AKI or electrolyte abnormalities (e.g., hyperkalemia in tissue necrosis, hypokalemia in GI losses).
* **Bedside Imaging**:
  * **eFAST Scan** (Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma): Rapidly rule out hemoperitoneum, hemothorax, and pneumothorax in trauma.
  * **Bedside Chest and Pelvis X-ray**: Identify sources of massive internal bleeding (e.g., hemothorax, unstable pelvic fractures).

## 🏥 Immediate Management

```
Hypovolemic Resuscitation Protocol
├── 1. Non-Hemorrhagic (Dehydration/Burns)
│   └── Rapid infusion of isotonic crystalloid (Ringer's Lactate preferred over Normal Saline)
└── 2. Hemorrhagic (Active Bleeding)
    ├── A. Stop the Bleeding (Direct pressure, tourniquets, pelvic binder, surgery)
    ├── B. Permissive Hypotension (Target MAP ~60-65 mmHg before bleeding is controlled)
    ├── C. Avoid Crystalloid Overload (Limit to 1-2L; excess crystalloid causes coagulopathy)
    └── D. Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)
        └── Transfuse PRBCs, FFP, and Platelets in a 1:1:1 balanced ratio
```

---

## 🏥 Definitive Management
* **Bleeding Control**: Emergent surgical intervention (laparotomy, thoracotomy), endoscopic intervention (variceal banding, epinephrine injection for ulcers), or angiographic embolization.
* **Rehydration**: Direct management of underlying causes (e.g., antiemetics, antibiotics for infectious diarrhea, insulin for DKA).

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)**: Target **$\ge 65$ mmHg** (or **$60-65$ mmHg** in active hemorrhage under permissive hypotension).
* **Urine Output**: Target **$>0.5$ mL/kg/h** via Foley catheter.
* **Lactate & Base Deficit**: Serial measurements every 2 hours to verify resolution of shock.
* **The Trauma Lethal Triad Prevention**: Keep patient warm to avoid **Hypothermia**, treat **Acidosis** by correcting perfusion, and avoid **Coagulopathy** by transfusing FFP/platelets rather than crystalloid.
* **Ionized Calcium**: Monitor during massive transfusion (citrate preservative in blood products binds calcium $\rightarrow$ hypocalcemia). Replace with Calcium Gluconate.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **The Lethal Triad of Trauma**: Resuscitation failure in hemorrhagic shock is often driven by the Lethal Triad: **Hypothermia, Acidosis, and Coagulopathy**. Hypothermia impairs coagulation enzymes; acidosis impairs clotting factor activity. Together, they exacerbate active bleeding. Resuscitation must include warming the patient and active replacement of clotting factors (FFP) rather than cold Normal Saline.
> **Narrow Pulse Pressure is Early**: Before systolic blood pressure drops, a narrowing of the pulse pressure (difference between systolic and diastolic BP) is an early, sensitive indicator of Class II hypovolemic shock, caused by diastolic rise from compensatory vasoconstriction.
