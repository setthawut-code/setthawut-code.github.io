# 🩸 Hyperglycemic Crises: DKA & HHS (ภาวะฉุกเฉินน้ำตาลในเลือดสูง)

> "Hyperglycemic crises represent acute, life-threatening metabolic emergencies of diabetes mellitus. They include Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) and Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS), characterized by extreme insulin deficiency, hyperglycemia, profound osmotic diuresis, dehydration, and electrolyte depletion."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Severe osmotic diuresis leads to massive intravascular volume depletion ($5-10$ L deficit). DKA causes severe metabolic acidosis ($pH < 7.3$, elevated anion gap) that impairs cardiac contractility and enzyme function. HHS causes extreme serum hyperosmolality ($>320$ mOsm/kg), leading to intracellular brain dehydration, coma, seizures, and thrombosis.

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess patency. Patients presenting with HHS or deep DKA coma may have altered consciousness, compromising the airway.
* Prepare for intubation if GCS $< 8$.

### Breathing
* Look for **Kussmaul Respiration** (deep, rapid, sighing breaths) in DKA. This is a vital respiratory compensation to wash out carbon dioxide and raise pH.
* Check for "fruity" odor on breath (acetone).
* Measure $SpO_2$.

### Circulation
* **Assess Dehydration**: Look for hypotension, tachycardia, dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, and delayed capillary refill.
* **Determine Fluid Deficit**: Typically $3 - 6$ liters in DKA ($100$ mL/kg) and $6 - 10$ liters in HHS ($100 - 220$ mL/kg).
* **IV Access**: Establish two large-bore peripheral IV lines immediately.

---

## 🔍 DKA vs. HHS Diagnostic Criteria

Differentiating DKA from HHS is critical, as fluid and insulin titration rates differ:

| Feature | Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) | Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blood Glucose** | Typically $> 250$ mg/dL (can be lower) | **Markedly elevated ($> 600$ mg/dL)** |
| **Arterial / Venous pH** | **Acidosis ($< 7.30$)** | Normal ($> 7.30$) |
| **Serum Bicarbonate** | **Low ($< 18$ mEq/L)** | Normal or near-normal ($> 18$ mEq/L) |
| **Ketones (Urine/Serum)**| **Positive** (Beta-hydroxybutyrate $>3$ mmol/L) | Minimal or negative |
| **Serum Osmolality** | Variable | **Markedly elevated ($> 320$ mOsm/kg)** |
| **Anion Gap** | **Elevated ($> 12$)** | Normal |
| **Mental Status** | Alert to stupor | **Stupor or Coma** |
| **Fluid Deficit** | $\sim 3 - 6$ Liters ($\sim 10\%$ body weight) | **$\sim 6 - 10$ Liters ($\sim 15\%$ body weight)** |

$$\text{Serum Osmolality} = 2[\text{Na}^+] + \frac{\text{Glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8}$$
$$\text{Anion Gap} = [\text{Na}^+] - ([\text{Cl}^-] + [\text{HCO}_3^-])$$

---

## 🦠 Causes

Classified by the "5 I's" precipitating factors:
* **Infection** (most common: Pneumonia, Urinary Tract Infection, Sepsis).
* **Infarction** (Myocardial Infarction, Stroke, Mesenteric Ischemia).
* **Insulin Non-adherence** (missed doses, pump failure).
* **Initial Presentation** (undiagnosed new-onset Diabetes Mellitus).
* **Iatrogenic** (Corticosteroids, SGLT2 inhibitors, Thiazides).

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

* **DKA / HHS**: Hyperglycemia, ketones (DKA), hyperosmolality (HHS).
* **Hypoglycemia**: Altered consciousness, diaphoresis. *Blood glucose $<70$ mg/dL*.
* **Other High Anion Gap Acidoses**: Uremia (CKD/AKI), lactic acidosis, toxic alcohol ingestion (methanol, ethylene glycol), salicylate toxicity. (Urine ketones are negative).

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Arterial or Venous Blood Gas (ABG/VBG)**: Confirm pH and bicarbonate levels.
* **Electrolytes (with corrected Sodium)**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **Corrected Sodium Calculation**: Hyperglycemia draws water out of cells, diluting serum sodium. For every $100$ mg/dL increase in glucose above $100$ mg/dL, add $1.6$ mEq/L to the measured sodium (or $2.4$ mEq/L if glucose is $>400$ mg/dL).
    $$\text{Corrected }\text{Na}^+ = \text{Measured }\text{Na}^+ + 1.6 \times \left( \frac{\text{Glucose} - 100}{100} \right)$$
* **Beta-hydroxybutyrate (Serum Ketones)**: More accurate and sensitive than urine acetoacetate testing.
* **Renal Function (BUN, Creatinine)**: Assess renal impairment from dehydration.
* **Urinalysis**: Check for glucosuria and ketonuria.

---

## 🏥 Immediate Management: The Three Pillars

Resuscitation focuses on **Fluids, Potassium, and Insulin** in a strict order:

```
Hyperglycemic Crisis Management Flow
├── PILLAR 1: Fluid Resuscitation (FIRST)
│   ├── Hour 1: Give 0.9% NaCl (1 - 1.5 L or 15-20 mL/kg)
│   └── Hour 2+: Assess Corrected Sodium:
│       ├── If Na+ is High/Normal: Switch to 0.45% NaCl (250-500 mL/h)
│       └── If Na+ is Low: Continue 0.9% NaCl (250-500 mL/h)
│   └── Switch to D5 in 0.45% NaCl: When glucose reaches 200 mg/dL (DKA) or 300 mg/dL (HHS)
├── PILLAR 2: Potassium Repletion (MANDATORY BEFORE INSULIN)
│   ├── If K+ < 3.3 mEq/L: HOLD insulin. Give 20-30 mEq/h KCl IV until K+ > 3.3 mEq/L
│   ├── If K+ 3.3 - 5.2 mEq/L: Give 20-30 mEq/L in fluids. Start Insulin. Maintain K+ 4.0-5.0 mEq/L
│   └── If K+ > 5.2 mEq/L: Do NOT give potassium. Start Insulin. Check K+ every 2 hours
└── PILLAR 3: Insulin Therapy (Start only if K+ > 3.3 mEq/L)
    └── Regular Insulin IV: Infuse at 0.1 U/kg/h (or 0.1 U/kg bolus + 0.1 U/kg/h)
    └── Target: Reduce blood glucose by 50 - 75 mg/dL per hour
```

*Note on Bicarbonate*: Do not routinely give bicarbonate. Only administer **Sodium Bicarbonate** (100 mmol in 400 mL sterile water with 20 mEq KCl over 2 hours) if **$pH < 6.9$**.

---

## 🏥 Definitive Management (Transition to Subcutaneous Insulin)

1. **Resolution Criteria**:
   - **DKA**: Blood glucose $< 200$ mg/dL AND two of: Venous $pH > 7.30$, Serum Bicarbonate $\ge 15$ mEq/L, or Anion Gap closed ($\le 12$).
   - **HHS**: Serum osmolality $< 310$ mOsm/kg, normal mental status, and patient is eating.
2. **Transition Protocol**:
   - Initiate subcutaneous (SC) basal insulin (e.g., Glargine).
   - **Overlap**: **Continue IV insulin infusion for 1 to 2 hours after the first SC insulin dose** is administered to prevent immediate rebound ketosis.

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Blood Glucose**: Monitor **hourly** at the bedside.
* **Electrolytes & Venous pH**: Monitor every **2 to 4 hours** (specifically Potassium, Sodium, and Bicarbonate/Anion Gap).
* **Fluid Balance**: Strict intake and output charting.
* **Neurological Checks**: Monitor every 1-2 hours. Look for signs of **Cerebral Edema** (headache, bradycardia, pupillary changes, altered mental status), especially in children and young adults if glucose/osmolality drops too rapidly ($>100$ mg/dL/h or $>3$ mOsm/kg/h).

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Euglycemic DKA Warning**: SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., Empagliflozin, Dapagliflozin) can cause DKA with **normal or near-normal blood glucose ($< 250$ mg/dL)**. This occurs because SGLT2i promote urinary glucose excretion, lowering blood glucose while glucagon levels remain high and insulin low, stimulating ketogenesis. Always check serum ketones/pH in a sick patient taking SGLT2i, regardless of blood sugar levels.
> **The Potassium Trap**: Insulin pushes potassium into cells. In addition, correction of acidosis shifts potassium intracellularly. Even if the initial potassium is normal (e.g., $4.2$ mEq/L), it will drop rapidly upon starting insulin. Always add potassium to IV fluids once $K^+ < 5.2$ mEq/L.
