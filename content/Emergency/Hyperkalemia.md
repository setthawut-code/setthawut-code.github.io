# ⚡ Hyperkalemia (ภาวะโพแทสเซียมในเลือดสูง)

> "Hyperkalemia is a critical electrolyte emergency defined as a serum potassium concentration $> 5.5$ mEq/L (severe if $\ge 6.5$ mEq/L). It alters the resting membrane potential of cardiac myocytes, leading to conduction blocks, lethal ventricular arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac arrest."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Potassium is the primary determinant of the resting membrane potential. Hyperkalemia decreases the electrical gradient across the cell membrane, reducing myocardial excitability and conduction velocity. This can lead to rapid, unpredictable progression from mild ECG changes to a **sine wave pattern**, ventricular fibrillation, or asystole.

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess patency. (Only compromised if cardiac arrest or severe respiratory muscle paralysis occurs).

### Breathing
* Look for normal respiration. Severe hyperkalemia can cause ascending muscle weakness, leading to diaphragmatic paralysis and respiratory arrest.
* Measure $SpO_2$.

### Circulation
* **Obtain and Interpret a 12-lead ECG Immediately**: Any patient with a suspected potassium $>6.0$ mEq/L or history of renal failure with missed dialysis must have an immediate ECG.
* **Assess Pulse**: Look for bradycardia, irregular rhythm, or a thready pulse.
* **Establish IV Access**: Secure reliable access immediately (calcium gluconate requires a patent vein).

---

## 🔍 Recognition (Serum Levels & ECG Progression)

Hyperkalemia severity is classified by serum potassium levels and the presence of ECG abnormalities:
* **Mild**: $5.5 - 5.9$ mEq/L.
* **Moderate**: $6.0 - 6.4$ mEq/L.
* **Severe**: $\ge 6.5$ mEq/L (or any level with ECG changes).

### 📈 Classic ECG Changes (In Order of Progression):
1. **Peaked T Waves**: Narrow, symmetrical, tall, "tent-like" T waves (earliest sign, best seen in precordial leads V2-V4).
2. **PR Prolongation & P Wave Flattening**: Conduction slows through the atrium.
3. **QRS Widening**: Conduction slows through the ventricle.
4. **Sine Wave Pattern**: QRS complex merges with the T wave (pre-terminal sign; indicates impending ventricular fibrillation or asystole).

```
ECG Hyperkalemia Progression
└── 1. Normal ECG
└── 2. Peaked T Waves (Tall, narrow, tented)
└── 3. PR Prolongation / Flat P Waves (Atrial standstill)
└── 4. QRS Widening (Mersed with T wave)
└── 5. Sine Wave Pattern (Impending cardiac arrest)
```

## 🦠 Causes

```
Hyperkalemia Pathophysiology
└── 1. Decreased Renal Excretion (Most Common)
    ├── Acute Kidney Injury or Chronic Kidney Disease
    ├── Hypoaldosteronism (Adrenal insufficiency)
    └── Meds: ACEi/ARBs, Spironolactone, NSAIDs, Trimethoprim
└── 2. Extracellular Shift (Acidosis / Cell lysis)
    ├── Metabolic Acidosis (Hydrogen ions shift in, potassium shifts out)
    ├── Rhabdomyolysis (Severe muscle breakdown)
    ├── Tumor Lysis Syndrome
    └── Acute Hemolytic Crisis
└── 3. Excessive Intake
    └── Potassium supplements or blood transfusions (stored red cells leak potassium)
```

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

* **True Hyperkalemia**: High serum potassium with or without ECG changes.
* **Pseudohyperkalemia**: Falsely elevated potassium due to **in vitro hemolysis** during blood draw (e.g., tight tourniquet, fist clenching, or small needle). Also occurs in severe thrombocytosis ($platelets > 1,000,000$) or leukocytosis ($WBC > 100,000$) as cells leak potassium during clotting. (Confirmed by repeating the draw and checking ECG; *if ECG is normal and patient has no risk factors, suspect pseudohyperkalemia*).

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **12-lead ECG**: The single most important immediate test.
* **Serum Potassium**: Confirm with a repeat venous blood draw if pseudohyperkalemia is suspected and the ECG is completely normal.
* **BUN and Creatinine**: Assess for underlying renal failure.
* **Venous / Arterial Blood Gas (VBG/ABG)**: Check pH and bicarbonate (metabolic acidosis shifts potassium out of cells).
* **Serum Calcium**: Hypocalcemia synergistically worsens the cardiotoxicity of hyperkalemia.
* **Creatine Kinase (CK)**: If rhabdomyolysis is suspected.

---

## 🏥 Immediate Management: The Three-Pillar Protocol

Resuscitate using the classic hyperkalemia treatment pillars:

```
Hyperkalemia Resuscitation Protocol
├── PILLAR 1: Cardiac Membrane Stabilization (PROTECT)
│   ├── Drug: 10% CALCIUM GLUCONATE: 10 mL IV infused over 5 - 10 minutes
│   ├── Target: Give immediately if ECG changes are present or K+ >= 6.5 mEq/L
│   ├── Onset/Duration: Works in 1-3 minutes; lasts 30-60 minutes. Repeat dose if ECG changes persist
│   └── Note: Calcium stabilizes the membrane potential; it does NOT lower potassium levels
├── PILLAR 2: Intracellular Shift (TEMPORARY HIDE)
│   ├── A. Insulin & Dextrose: Regular Insulin 10 Units IV bolus + 50 mL of D50W (drives K+ into cells)
│   ├── B. Nebulized Beta-2 Agonist: Albuterol/Salbutamol 10 - 20 mg nebulized (nebulize over 15 min)
│   └── C. IV Sodium Bicarbonate: 50-100 mEq IV over 15 min (indicated ONLY if metabolic acidosis is present)
└── PILLAR 3: Potassium Elimination (REMOVE FROM BODY)
    ├── A. Loop Diuretics: Furosemide 40-80 mg IV (if patient has intact renal function)
    ├── B. GI Cation Exchangers: Lokelma (SZC) 10 g PO or Kayexalate (SPS) (avoid SPS in bowel obstruction)
    └── C. Hemodialysis: The most effective and definitive method (indicated in renal failure/anuria)
```

---

## 🏥 Definitive Management
* Perform **Emergency Hemodialysis** for patients with anuric renal failure, severe tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis), or hyperkalemia refractory to medical therapy.
* Discontinue all offending drugs (ACEi/ARBs, NSAIDs, potassium-sparing diuretics).

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Continuous Cardiac Telemetry**: Mandatory for any patient receiving calcium or with potassium $>6.0$ mEq/L.
* **Serial Serum Potassium**: Check 1 and 2 hours after initiating shifting therapy, then every 2-4 hours until $<5.0$ mEq/L.
* **Hourly Blood Glucose**: Monitor for **hypoglycemia** (a common side effect of IV insulin administration, peaking at 1-2 hours post-infusion).

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Hypoglycemia Risk with Insulin**: Up to 10-15% of patients treated with IV insulin/dextrose for hyperkalemia develop hypoglycemia. Patients with underlying renal impairment are at highest risk because insulin clearance is delayed. Always monitor blood glucose hourly for at least 4 hours after giving insulin.
> **Calcium Chloride vs. Gluconate**: IV Calcium Gluconate is preferred for peripheral lines because it is less irritating. However, if the patient has a central line or is in active cardiac arrest, **IV Calcium Chloride (10% 10 mL)** is preferred because it contains 3 times more elemental calcium and does not require hepatic metabolism to become active.
