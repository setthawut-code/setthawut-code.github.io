# ⚡ Malignant Hypertension & Hypertensive Emergencies (ภาวะความดันโลหิตสูงวิกฤต)

> "A hypertensive emergency is characterized by severe elevation of blood pressure (typically systolic $BP > 180$ mmHg or diastolic $BP > 120$ mmHg) associated with acute, progressive target organ damage. Malignant hypertension is a subset characterized by encephalopathy or acute retinopathy (papilledema, hemorrhages, exudates)."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Severe, rapid rise in blood pressure overwhelms cerebral and systemic autoregulation. This causes endothelial dysfunction, fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and catastrophic organ failures: stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, or pulmonary edema.

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess and secure airway. Patients presenting with hypertensive encephalopathy, acute hemorrhagic stroke, or seizures may have compromised airway reflexes.

### Breathing
* Look for signs of pulmonary congestion (respiratory distress, accessory muscle use, and bilateral crackles).
* Measure $SpO_2$. If hypoxemic, provide oxygen or initiate non-invasive ventilation (NIV) to counter acute pulmonary edema.

### Circulation
* **Measure Blood Pressure in Both Arms**: A significant discrepancy ($>20$ mmHg) between arms suggests **[[Aortic Dissection]]**.
* **Establish Continuous Monitoring**: Insert an arterial line for real-time blood pressure tracking.
* **Auscultate Heart**: Check for new murmurs (aortic regurgitation in dissection) or S3/S4 gallops.
* Assess peripheral pulses (check for asymmetric or absent pulses).

---

## 🔍 Recognition
* **Blood Pressure**: Typically $>180/120$ mmHg.
* **Target Organ Damage Indicators**:
  * *Neurological*: Severe headache, altered consciousness (**Hypertensive Encephalopathy**), visual deficits, seizures, or focal neurological deficits (Stroke/ICH).
  * *Cardiovascular*: Crushing chest pain (ACS, Aortic Dissection), severe dyspnea ([[Acute Pulmonary Edema]]), or tearing back pain.
  * *Renal*: Oliguria, hematuria, or rapidly rising creatinine.
  * *Ophthalmic*: Papilledema, flame-shaped hemorrhages, or cotton-wool spots on fundoscopy.

## 🦠 Causes

```
Hypertensive Emergency Etiologies
└── 1. Essential Hypertension Adherence Failure
    └── Abrupt cessation of chronic antihypertensive drugs
└── 2. Secondary Renal Causes
    ├── Renal Artery Stenosis (Renovascular)
    └── Acute Glomerulonephritis
└── 3. Endocrine / Catecholamine Excess
    ├── Pheochromocytoma
    └── Clonidine withdrawal syndrome
└── 4. Drug-Induced
    └── Cocaine, Amphetamines, Sympathomimetics
└── 5. Pregnancy-Related
    └── Preeclampsia / Eclampsia
```

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

The key clinical distinction is between **Hypertensive Emergency** and **Hypertensive Urgency**:

| Feature | Hypertensive Emergency | Hypertensive Urgency |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blood Pressure** | Typically $>180/120$ mmHg | Typically $>180/120$ mmHg |
| **Target Organ Damage**| **Present** (encephalopathy, ACS, stroke, AKI, dissection). | **Absent**. |
| **Management Site** | **Intensive Care Unit (ICU)** | Emergency Dept / Outpatient Clinic |
| **Therapy Route** | **Intravenous** antihypertensives | **Oral** antihypertensives |
| **BP Reduction Goal** | Controlled, gradual reduction (MAP down 20-25% in 1st hour). | Gradual reduction over 24-48 hours. |
| **Risks of Rapid Drop**| Required to limit organ damage, but *excessive* drop causes stroke/ischemia. | Highly dangerous; rapid drop causes cerebral/myocardial hypoperfusion. |

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Immediate Labs**:
  * **Renal Function (BUN, Creatinine) & Electrolytes**: Evaluate for acute kidney injury.
  * **[[Urinalysis]]**: Look for proteinuria, microscopic hematuria, or red cell casts (suggests glomerulonephritis or nephrosclerosis).
  * **Cardiac Enzymes (Troponin)**: Evaluate for myocardial necrosis.
  * **CBC and Peripheral Blood Smear**: Look for schistocytes (indicates microangiopathic hemolytic anemia).
* **Imaging and Diagnostics**:
  * **12-lead ECG**: Look for signs of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) or acute myocardial ischemia/infarction.
  * **Chest X-ray**: Check for cardiomegaly, pulmonary congestion, or widened mediastinum (aortic dissection).
  * **Non-Contrast Head CT**: Indicated if the patient has altered consciousness, severe headache, or focal neurologic deficits.
  * **CT Angiogram (CTA) of Chest and Abdomen**: Indicated immediately if aortic dissection is suspected.

---

## 🏥 Immediate Management

### The 20-25% MAP Rule (General Rule)
In most hypertensive emergencies, **do not lower Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) by more than 20% to 25% within the first hour**. Then, lower BP to approximately $160/100 - 110$ mmHg over the next 2 to 6 hours. Lowering BP too rapidly can cause cerebral or coronary ischemia due to shifted autoregulation curves.

$$\text{MAP} = \text{Diastolic BP} + \frac{\text{Systolic BP} - \text{Diastolic BP}}{3}$$

### Critical Exceptions to the Rule
1. **[[Aortic Dissection]]**: Reduce Systolic BP **rapidly to $<120$ mmHg** and heart rate to $<60$ bpm within 20 minutes. Use IV Esmolol first (to prevent reflex tachycardia) followed by Nicardipine or Nitroprusside.
2. **Acute Ischemic Stroke**: Do not lower BP unless it is **$> 220/120$ mmHg** (lower by 15% over 24h) OR **$> 185/110$ mmHg** if the patient is a candidate for IV thrombolysis (tPA).
3. **Preeclampsia/Eclampsia**: Lower systolic BP to $<160$ mmHg and diastolic BP to $<110$ mmHg. Give IV Magnesium Sulfate for seizure prophylaxis.

### First-line Intravenous Medications
* **Nicardipine**: Calcium channel blocker. Easy to titrate. *Dose*: Start 5 mg/h IV, increase by 2.5 mg/h every 5-15 min to max 15 mg/h.
* **Labetalol**: Combined alpha-1 and beta blocker. Excellent for aortic dissection, stroke, and pregnancy. *Dose*: 20 mg IV bolus over 2 min, then double dose every 10 min (40, 80, 80 mg) to max 300 mg, or start infusion. *Contraindicated in asthma/COPD or severe bradycardia.*
* **Nitroglycerin**: Primary venous vasodilator. Preferred in acute coronary syndrome or pulmonary edema.
* **Hydralazine**: Direct vasodilator. Preferred in pregnancy. Avoid in ACS/dissection due to reflex tachycardia.

---

## 🏥 Definitive Management

* Transition to oral antihypertensive medications once the patient is hemodynamically stable for 24-48 hours.
* Investigate for secondary causes of hypertension (e.g., Renal artery duplex, plasma aldosterone/renin ratio, sleep study for OSA, or 24-hour urine metanephrines for pheochromocytoma).

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Intra-arterial Blood Pressure (Arterial Line)**: Mandatory for patients receiving continuous IV antihypertensive infusions.
* **Continuous ECG Telemetry**.
* **Neurologic Checks**: Perform every 15-30 minutes during initial BP reduction. If neurologic deficits worsen, hold or reduce the rate of antihypertensive infusion.
* **Hourly Urine Output**: Maintain target $>0.5$ mL/kg/h.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Avoid Sublingual Nifedipine**: The use of sublingual nifedipine to rapidly lower BP in hypertensive urgency/emergency is **strictly contraindicated**. It causes unpredictable, abrupt hypotension that precipitates stroke, myocardial infarction, and death.
> **Beta-Block Before Vasodilator in Dissection**: In acute aortic dissection, never administer a vasodilator (like Nicardipine or Nitroprusside) before achieving beta-blockade (with Esmolol or Labetalol). Vasodilators cause reflex sympathetic activation, increasing heart rate and shear stress ($dP/dt$), which propagates the dissection tear.
