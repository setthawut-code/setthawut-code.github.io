# 🫁 Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (กลุ่มอาการอุดกั้นหลอดเลือดดำใหญ่ส่วนบน)

> "Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Syndrome is an oncological emergency caused by the gradual or acute obstruction of blood flow through the SVC. This leads to severe venous congestion, hypertension in the upper body, and potential life-threatening laryngeal or cerebral edema."

---

## 🚨 Why It Is An Emergency
Severe SVC obstruction elevates venous pressure in the head, neck, and upper extremities. This can precipitate **laryngeal edema** (causing immediate airway occlusion) or **cerebral edema** (causing headache, confusion, seizures, and coma). Tracheal compression by a large mediastinal mass can also cause rapid respiratory failure.

---

## 🩺 Immediate Assessment

### Airway
* Assess for signs of upper airway edema: **stridor**, hoarseness, globus sensation, or difficulty breathing.
* If stridor or signs of airway compromise are present, secure the airway. *Caution: Intubation can be extremely difficult due to laryngeal swelling and mucosal friability. Have a senior anesthesiologist/ENT specialist present and prepare for a surgical airway.*

### Breathing
* Look for tachypnea, orthopnea, and accessory muscle use.
* Auscultate for unilateral decreased breath sounds (often associated with pleural effusion) or stridor.
* Measure $SpO_2$. Maintain target $>92\%$.

### Circulation
* **Assess Venous Congestion**: Look for **facial plethora** (redness and swelling), periorbital edema, swelling of the neck and arms, and **dilated venous collateral pathways** on the chest wall and abdomen.
* Measure blood pressure. (Note: BP measured in the arms may be falsely elevated due to venous congestion; if possible, measure in the lower extremities).
* **Positioning**: **Elevate the head of the bed to $> 45$ degrees** immediately. This utilizes gravity to decrease hydrostatic pressure in the head and neck, reducing edema.

---

## 🔍 Recognition
* **Facial Plethora & Swelling**: A classic "puffiness" of the face, eyelids, and neck (Pemberton's sign: facial flushing and distress when both arms are raised above the head).
* **Venous Distension**: Engorged jugular veins (without respiratory variation) and prominent chest wall veins.
* **Cerebral Symptoms**: Dull, throbbing headache, dizziness, visual disturbances, confusion, or somnolence (signs of increased intracranial pressure).

---

## 🦠 Causes

Classified into Malignant (most common, $>90\%$) and Non-Malignant:

```
SVC Syndrome Causes
└── 1. Malignant Intrusion (Extrinsic compression or invasion)
    ├── Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC) (Most common)
    ├── Small Cell Lung Cancer (Highly chemosensitive)
    ├── Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)
    └── Mediastinal Germ Cell Tumors
└── 2. Non-Malignant (Thrombosis / Intraluminal obstruction)
    └── Catheter-associated thrombosis (PICC lines, pacemakers)
```

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Clinical Features | Key Investigations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **SVC Syndrome** | Facial swelling, upper body plethora, dilated chest wall veins. Normal heart sounds. | Contrast Chest CT. |
| **Right Heart Failure** | Bilateral lower extremity edema, hepatomegaly, ascites, JVP elevation with respiratory variation. | Echocardiography. |
| **[[Anaphylactic Shock]]** | Acute onset, urticaria, wheezing, hypotension. No chest wall collateral veins. | Clinical diagnosis. |
| **[[Cardiac Tamponade]]** | Hypotension, muffled heart sounds, clear lungs. No facial plethora or chest collaterals. | Bedside Echocardiography. |

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Gold Standard Diagnostic**:
  * **CT Scan of the Chest with IV Contrast (CT Venography)**: The primary diagnostic tool. Confirms SVC obstruction, identifies the compressing mass, visualizes thrombi, and maps out collateral venous pathways.
* **Supportive Diagnostics**:
  * **Chest X-ray**: May show a widened mediastinum ($60\%$ of cases), right-sided pleural effusion, or a hilar mass.
* **Histopathological Diagnosis (Crucial)**:
  * Unless there is immediate airway compromise, **obtain a tissue diagnosis before starting definitive therapy**. Options include sputum cytology, thoracentesis (pleural fluid cytology), bronchoscopy with biopsy, or lymph node biopsy (mediastinoscopy).

---

## 🏥 Immediate Management

Prioritize symptom relief and reduction of venous hydrostatic pressure:

```
SVC Syndrome Emergency Protocol
├── 1. Elevate Head of Bed (Keep HOB > 45 degrees or sit upright)
├── 2. Supplemental Oxygen (Provide oxygen to maintain SpO2 > 92%)
├── 3. Glucocorticoids (For suspected lymphoma, thymoma, or severe edema)
│   ├── DEXAMETHASONE: 4 - 10 mg IV every 6 hours
│   └── Action: Reduces tumor-associated inflammation and edema
├── 4. Loop Diuretics (Use with caution)
│   ├── FUROSEMIDE: 20 - 40 mg IV
│   └── Action: Decreases venous return; avoid systemic dehydration
└── 5. Anticoagulation (If thrombus is present)
    └── Initiate Unfractionated Heparin (UFH) or LMWH (Enoxaparin)
```

---

## 🏥 Definitive Management

Definitive therapy depends on the tumor type and severity of symptoms:

1. **Endovascular SVC Stenting**:
   - The treatment of choice for **severe, life-threatening symptoms** (airway/cerebral edema). Provides rapid symptomatic relief within 24-72 hours by restoring SVC patency.
2. **Radiation Therapy**:
   - Preferred first-line therapy for patients with non-small cell lung cancer or other radioresistant tumors who present with severe symptoms but no tissue diagnosis.
3. **Chemotherapy**:
   - Preferred first-line therapy for highly chemosensitive tumors (**Small Cell Lung Cancer, Lymphoma, Germ Cell Tumors**). Responds rapidly, making radiation or stenting unnecessary in stable patients.

---

## 📊 Monitoring

* **Airway Patency**: Check frequently for stridor or worsening hoarseness.
* **Neurological Status**: Assess for headache progression, confusion, or visual changes.
* **Urine Output & Hydration**: Ensure diuretics do not cause volume depletion.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Pemberton's Sign**: A bedside physical maneuver. Ask the patient to elevate both arms above their head for 1 minute. A positive test is characterized by progressive facial plethora, cyanosis, jugular venous distension, and respiratory distress, indicating subclinical SVC compression.
> **Avoid Upper Extremity IV Access**: In patients with suspected SVC syndrome, **never insert peripheral IV lines or PICC lines in the upper extremities**. The high venous pressure will impede fluid infusion and increase the risk of local thrombosis. Use lower extremity veins or a femoral central line.
