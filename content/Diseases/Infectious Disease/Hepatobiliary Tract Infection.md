# 🦠 Hepatobiliary Tract Infection (การติดเชื้อทางเดินน้ำดีและตับ)

> **Definition**: Bacterial infections affecting the gallbladder (**Acute Cholecystitis**), biliary tree (**Acute Cholangitis**), or liver parenchyma (**Pyogenic Liver Abscess**), often secondary to biliary obstruction and stasis.

---

## 🦠 1. Acute Cholecystitis

* **Definition**: Acute inflammation of the gallbladder wall.
* **Pathophysiology**:
  * **Calculous Cholecystitis** ($90-95\%$): Cystic duct obstruction by gallstone $\rightarrow$ bile stasis $\rightarrow$ chemical irritation of mucosa $\rightarrow$ secondary bacterial infection (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococcus*).
  * **Acalculous Cholecystitis** ($5-10\%$): Gallbladder ischemia and stasis in critically ill, septic, or severely burned patients on TPN. High risk of gangrene/perforation.
* **Clinical Manifestations**:
  * Persistent, severe RUQ pain (may radiate to right shoulder/scapula - **Boas' sign**).
  * Fever, nausea, vomiting.
  * **Murphy's Sign**: Involuntary arrest of inspiration during deep palpation of the RUQ.
* **Investigations**:
  * **RUQ Ultrasound (First-line)**: Shows gallstones, **gallbladder wall thickening ($>4$ mm)**, pericholecystic fluid, and **sonographic Murphy's sign** (exquisite pain when transducer presses gallbladder).
  * **HIDA Scan (Cholescintigraphy)**: Most sensitive/specific. Non-visualization of gallbladder after 4 hours confirms cystic duct obstruction.
* **Management**:
  * NPO, IV hydration, analgesics (avoid Morphine due to potential spasm of sphincter of Oddi; use NSAIDs/meperidine).
  * Antibiotics: **Ceftriaxone** 1-2 g IV qd + **Metronidazole** 500 mg IV q8h.
  * **Definitive**: **Early Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy** (preferably within 72 hours of symptom onset).

---

## 🦠 2. Acute Cholangitis (Emergency)

* **Definition**: Life-threatening bacterial infection of the biliary tree.
* **Pathophysiology**: Obstruction of the common bile duct (CBD) (due to choledocholithiasis, tumor, or stricture) $\rightarrow$ biliary stasis $\rightarrow$ rapid bacterial proliferation $\rightarrow$ increased biliary pressure $\rightarrow$ ascending bacterial translocation into systemic circulation (cholangiovenous reflux $\rightarrow$ severe bacteremia).
* **Common Pathogens**: *E. coli*, *Klebsiella* spp., *Enterococcus* spp., *Bacteroides fragilis* (anaerobe).
* **Clinical Manifestations**:
  * **Charcot's Triad**: **Fever** (often with rigors), **RUQ pain**, and **Jaundice**.
  * **Reynolds' Pentad** (indicates severe toxic cholangitis/septic shock): Charcot's triad + **Hypotension** + **Altered Mental Status**.
* **Investigations**:
  * **Labs**: Neutrophilic leukocytosis, marked cholestasis (**markedly elevated bilirubin and Alkaline Phosphatase**), mild transaminitis. Positive blood cultures.
  * **Imaging**: RUQ Ultrasound or abdominal CT shows **dilated common bile duct ($>6$ mm)**, stones, or biliary obstruction.
* **Management**:
  * **Step 1: Resuscitation & IV Antibiotics**: IV fluids, and broad-spectrum antibiotics (**Piperacillin-Tazobactam** 4.5 g IV q6h OR Ceftriaxone + Metronidazole).
  * **Step 2: Urgent Biliary Decompression (Crucial)**:
    * **First-line**: **ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography)** with sphincterotomy and biliary stenting.
    * **Alternative** (if ERCP fails/unavailable): **PTBD (Percutaneous Transhepatic Biliary Drainage)** or surgical decompression.

## 🦠 3. Pyogenic Liver Abscess

* **Definition**: Localized collection of pus within the liver parenchyma.
* **Routes of Infection**:
  * Ascending biliary tract infection (cholangitis; most common).
  * Portal vein bacteremia (pylephlebitis from appendicitis or diverticulitis).
  * Hematogenous spread (systemic bacteremia via hepatic artery).
  * Direct extension (cholecystitis, peptic ulcer perforation).
* **Key Pathogens**:
  * *E. coli*, *Enterococcus* spp.
  * ***Klebsiella pneumoniae***: Highly virulent. Classic in **diabetic patients**. Associated with **metastatic infections** (spontaneous endophthalmitis, brain abscesses).
* **Clinical Manifestations**: Fever, chills, RUQ pain, tender hepatomegaly, weight loss, night sweats. Jaundice is mild or absent unless biliary tract is obstructed.
* **Investigations**:
  * **Abdominal Ultrasound / CT**: Shows single or multiple round hypoechoic/low-density lesions with a peripheral enhancing rim.
  * **Blood Cultures**: Positive in $50\%$ of cases.
* **Management**:
  * **Antibiotic Therapy**: **Ceftriaxone** 2 g IV qd + **Metronidazole** 500 mg IV q8h (or Piperacillin-Tazobactam) for **4–6 weeks** (first 2-3 weeks IV, then switch to oral).
  * **Drainage (Essential)**:
    * **Percutaneous Catheter Drainage (Pigtail insertion)**: First-line for abscesses $>3-5$ cm.
    * Needle aspiration: For abscesses $<3$ cm or multiple small abscesses.
    * Surgical drainage: Reserved for rupture, multiloculated abscesses failing percutaneous drainage.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > ***Klebsiella* Liver Abscess Syndrome**: A diabetic patient presenting with fever, liver abscess, and sudden unilateral vision loss or headache has *Klebsiella pneumoniae* liver abscess with septic embolization to the eye (**Endophthalmitis**) or brain (**Meningitis**). Urgent ophthalmology consult and systemic antibiotics required.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **ERCP Safety**: Perform ERCP decompression only after stabilizing the patient with IV fluids and starting antibiotics. However, if the patient has Reynolds' Pentad (toxic cholangitis), emerge ERCP is life-saving and should not be delayed.
* > [!TIP]
  > **Amebic vs. Pyogenic Liver Abscess**: Amebic liver abscess (caused by *Entamoeba histolytica*) is usually single, in the right lobe, in young males, and presents with chocolate-brown ("anchovy paste") fluid. It **rarely requires drainage** and responds to Metronidazole alone. Pyogenic abscess is usually multiple, in older/diabetic patients, and **always requires drainage plus antibiotics**.
