## type: disease

# Definition
Cerebral Palsy (CP) is a group of permanent, non-progressive disorders of movement and posture development that cause activity limitation. It is attributed to non-progressive disturbances or injuries that occurred in the developing fetal or infant brain (usually prior to age 2 to 3 years).

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Approximately 1.5 to 4 per 1,000 live births, making it the most common cause of motor disability in childhood.
* **Risk Factors**: Strongly associated with **prematurity** (gestational age $<32$ weeks) and **very low birth weight** ($<1,500$ g). The prevalence is substantially higher in these subgroups.

# Etiology
Cerebral palsy is multifactorial, with insults occurring prenatally, perinatally, or postnatally:

1. **Prenatal ($75-80\%$)**:
   * Intrauterine infections (e.g., TORCH infections: Toxoplasmosis, Rubella, CMV, Herpes).
   * Fetal stroke, maternal preeclampsia, placental pathology, or genetic syndromes.
2. **Perinatal ($10-15\%$)**:
   * Birth asphyxia / hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) (contrary to historical belief, birth asphyxia alone causes $<10\%$ of CP cases).
   * **Severe Neonatal Jaundice (Kernicterus)**.
   * Prematurity complications: **Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH)** and **Periventricular Leukomalacia (PVL)**.
3. **Postnatal ($5\%$, up to age 2)**:
   * Bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis, head trauma (accidental or non-accidental), or near-drowning.

# Pathophysiology
* **Non-Progressive Lesion**: The primary brain injury (e.g., hypoxia, hemorrhage, focal stroke) is **static** and does not worsen over time. However, the clinical manifestations (spasticity, deformities) evolve as the child grows.
* **Periventricular Leukomalacia (PVL)**:
  * Characterized by ischemic necrosis of oligodendrocytes in the white matter adjacent to the lateral ventricles, most commonly seen in premature infants.
  * The descending corticospinal tract fibers controlling the lower extremities pass closest to the ventricles; hence, PVL classically produces **Spastic Diplegia** (paralysis of both legs).
* **Basal Ganglia Injury**:
  * Hypoxia-ischemia of the basal ganglia/thalamus or toxic deposition of unconjugated bilirubin in the globus pallidus (kernicterus) disrupts motor planning pathways, leading to **Dyskinetic CP**.
* **Cerebellar Injury**:
  * Damage to the developing cerebellum results in coordination failure and **Ataxic CP**.

# Clinical Subtypes & Manifestations

## Spastic Cerebral Palsy (70 - 80% of cases)
Characterized by upper motor neuron (UMN) signs: spastic hypertonia, hyperreflexia, and positive Babinski signs. It is subclassified anatomically:
* **Spastic Diplegia**:
  * Bilateral lower extremity involvement is much more severe than upper extremity involvement.
  * Classically associated with prematurity and PVL. On walking, children display a **scissoring gait** (adductor muscle spasm causes legs to cross).
* **Spastic Hemiplegia**:
  * Unilateral motor impairment (arm is usually more affected than the leg). Hand dominance is established abnormally early (before age 1).
* **Spastic Quadriplegia**:
  * Severe involvement of all four limbs, often accompanied by bulbar palsy, severe cognitive impairment, and a high incidence of seizures.

## Dyskinetic (Athetoid) Cerebral Palsy (10 - 15% of cases)
Characterized by involuntary, uncontrolled, recurring movements:
* **Athetosis**: Slow, writhing, purposeless movements, particularly of distal extremities.
* **Chorea**: Rapid, random, jerky movements.
* **Dystonia**: Sustained, twisting muscle contractions.
* Muscle tone fluctuates from hypotonic to hypertonic. Reflected by basal ganglia damage (e.g., post-kernicterus).

## Ataxic Cerebral Palsy (5% of cases)
Characterized by impaired balance, coordination, and hypotonia. Children present with a wide-based, unstable gait, intention tremor, and dysmetria.

## Associated Comorbidities (Non-Motor)
* **Intellectual Disability** (present in $50\%$ of patients, especially spastic quadriplegia).
* **[[Epilepsy]] / Seizure Disorder** ($30-50\%$).
* **Speech and Language Deficits**: Dysarthria due to pseudobulbar palsy.
* **Visual & Hearing Impairments**: Strabismus, cortical visual impairment, or sensorineural hearing loss.
* **Orthopedic Complications**: Joint contractures, scoliosis, and progressive **hip subluxation/dislocation** due to muscle imbalances.

## Physical Examination
* **Motor Assessment**: Delayed developmental motor milestones (e.g., not sitting by 8 months, not walking by 18 months).
* **Muscle Tone**: Hypertonia (spasticity) or fluctuating tone.
* **Reflexes**: Hyperreflexia, clonus, persistent primitive reflexes (e.g., Moro, tonic neck reflexes persisting beyond 6 months), and bilateral positive Babinski signs.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Progressive Neurodegenerative Disorders** (e.g., Metachromatic Leukodystrophy, Rett Syndrome): Present with a progressive loss of developmental milestones, unlike CP where milestones are delayed but not lost.
* **Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia**: Slowly progressive spastic paraplegia with a positive family history.
* **Dopa-Responsive Dystonia (Segawa Disease)**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
    > **Segawa Disease Diagnostic Pitfall**: A rare hereditary metabolic disorder that mimics spastic diplegia/dystonic CP. Unlike CP, it is **highly treatable with low-dose L-Dopa**, which completely reverses symptoms. A trial of L-dopa is warranted in children presenting with progressive dystonia/spasticity without clear perinatal risk factors.

# Investigations

## Imaging
* **[[MRI Brain]] (Gold Standard)**:
  * Recommended for all children with suspected CP to identify the underlying structural lesion and rule out progressive disorders.
  * Common findings include **periventricular leukomalacia (PVL)**, focal cerebral infarctions, brain malformations, or bilateral globus pallidus hyperintensities (kernicterus).

## Special Tests
* **Electroencephalogram ([[EEG]])**: Indicated if seizures are suspected.
* **Genetic and Metabolic Screens**: Indicated if MRI is normal or there is clinical progression.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Diagnosis is clinical, based on a delay in motor milestones, static (non-progressive) postural/movement abnormalities, persistence of primitive reflexes, and neuroimaging confirmation.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Multidisciplinary Team**: Program requires coordination between pediatric neurologists, physical and occupational therapists, orthopedists, speech therapists, and developmental specialists.
* **Early Intervention Programs**: Optimize motor development, positioning, and nutrition.

## Definitive Management
* **Spasticity Pharmacotherapy**:
  * **Oral Antispasticity Meds**: **Baclofen**, Diazepam, Tizanidine.
  * **Botulinum Toxin (Botox) Injections**: Injected directly into spastic muscle groups (e.g., gastrocnemius to treat toe-walking) to improve gait and prevent contractures. (Repeated every 3-6 months).
  * **Intrathecal Baclofen (ITB) Pump**: Surgical implantation of a pump that delivers baclofen directly into the CSF, highly effective for severe generalized spasticity.
* **Surgical Interventions**:
  * **Orthopedic Surgery**: Muscle tendon lengthening (e.g., heel cord lengthening) and osteotomies to correct hip dislocation or scoliosis.
  * **Selective Dorsal Rhizotomy (SDR)**: A neurosurgical procedure where specific sensory (dorsal) nerve rootlets in the lumbar spine are sectioned to cut the spastic reflex loop, permanently reducing lower limb spasticity in select ambulatory children.
* **Comorbidity Management**: Control seizures with AEDs; correct strabismus; support nutrition (G-tube if severe dysphagia).

---

# Complications
* **Musculoskeletal Deformities**: Hip dislocations, fixed joint contractures, windblown hip deformity, and severe scoliosis.
* **Malnutrition & Aspiration Pneumonia**: Due to bulbar coordination failure.
* **Osteoporosis & Pathological Fractures**: Secondary to non-weight-bearing immobility.

# Prognosis
* Cerebral palsy is a lifelong, static condition.
* The majority of children with mild spastic hemiplegia or diplegia achieve independent walking. Spastic quadriplegia carries a poor functional prognosis.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Early Hand Dominance Red Flag**: Infants should not show a preference for one hand over the other before the age of 12 months. If an infant under 12 months consistently uses only their right hand and keeps their left fist clenched, suspect contralateral **spastic hemiplegic cerebral palsy**.
* > [!TIP]
  > **Scissoring Gait**: In spastic diplegia, the hypertonicity of the adductor muscles of the thigh causes the legs to cross over one another like scissors when the child is held upright or attempts to walk.
