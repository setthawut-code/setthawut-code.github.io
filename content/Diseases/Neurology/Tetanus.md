## type: disease

# Definition
Tetanus is an acute, spastic neuromuscular disease caused by **tetanospasmin**, a potent neurotoxin produced by *Clostridium tetani*. It is clinically characterized by severe, generalized muscle rigidity and painful muscle spasms, classically presenting as lockjaw (trismus).

# Epidemiology
* Rare in developed nations due to widespread childhood vaccination programs (DTaP/Tdap).
* Remains endemic in developing nations, particularly in agricultural areas. **Neonatal Tetanus** is a major cause of infant mortality in developing countries, arising from unsterile cutting of the umbilical cord in non-immunized mothers.

# Etiology
* **Clostridium tetani**: An obligate anaerobic, motile, spore-forming, Gram-positive rod. The terminal spore gives the vegetative cell a characteristic **"drumstick" or "tennis racket"** microscopic appearance.
* Spores are highly resilient and ubiquitously present in soil, animal feces, and house dust.
* **Transmission**: Spores enter the body through contaminated wounds (deep puncture wounds, crush injuries, animal bites, compound fractures, or contaminated needles).

# Pathophysiology
1. **Germination**: In an anaerobic environment (necrotic tissue, foreign bodies), spores germinate into vegetative cells and replicate.
2. **Toxin Synthesis**: Vegetative cells release **tetanospasmin** (a zinc metalloprotease toxin).
3. **Retrograde Axonal Transport**: The toxin binds to peripheral nerve terminals at the NMJ and travels retrogradely via fast axonal transport along motor neurons into the spinal cord and brainstem.
4. **Synaptic Blockade**:
   * The toxin leaves the motor neuron and enters adjacent inhibitory interneurons (**Renshaw cells**).
   * Here, the toxin's light chain cleaves **synaptobrevin** (VAMP), a SNARE protein essential for vesicle fusion.
   * This **blocks the exocytosis of inhibitory neurotransmitters (GABA and Glycine)** into the synaptic cleft.
5. **Hyper-excitation**: Loss of GABA and glycine inhibition removes the "off-switch" from motor neurons. The alpha-motor neurons fire continuously and uncontrollably, leading to sustained muscle rigidity and severe spastic contractions. The autonomic nervous system is similarly affected, leading to sympathetic storm.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Incubation Period**: Typically **3 to 21 days** (range 1 day to months; a shorter incubation period correlates with wound proximity to the CNS and predicts a worse prognosis).
* **Trismus (Lockjaw)**: The presenting symptom in $>80\%$ of cases (due to spasm of the masseter muscles, which are highly sensitive to the toxin).
* **Risus Sardonicus**: A fixed, sardonic grin caused by spasm of the facial muscles (orbicularis oris, zygomaticus).
* **Opisthotonos**: Severe, painful spasms of the paraspinal and neck muscles, causing the patient's spine to arch backward.
* **Painful Generalized Spasms**: Triggered by minimal sensory stimuli (light, sound, touch, drafts of air). The patient remains **fully conscious** during these agonizing spasms.
* **Autonomic Storm**: Severe hypertension, tachycardia alternating with bradycardia, hyperthermia, and profuse sweating.

## Physical Examination
* **Trismus**: Inability of the patient to open the mouth.
* **Opisthotonos Posture**: Extensor rigidity.
* **The Spatula Test**:
  * *How to perform*: Touch the posterior pharyngeal wall with a sterile tongue spatula.
  * *Negative (Normal)*: Elicits a gag reflex; the patient attempts to spit out the spatula.
  * *Positive (Tetanus)*: Elicits a reflex spasm of the masseter muscles, causing the patient to **involuntarily bite down on the spatula** (highly sensitive ($94\%$) and specific ($100\%$) for tetanus; no false-positives).

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Strychnine Poisoning**: Competitively blocks glycine receptors. Presents identically to tetanus, but features rapid onset ($<2$ hours) after ingestion and lacks early localized lockjaw/trismus.
* **[[Rabies]]**: Presents with violent spasms, but these are localized to the pharyngeal muscles (hydrophobia) rather than the masseters (trismus), and are accompanied by severe confusion/hallucinations (tetanus patients are fully alert).
* **Acute Dystonic Reaction**: Induced by dopamine antagonists (Metoclopramide, Haloperidol). Presents with oculogyric crisis (upward gaze deviation) and torticollis. Responds rapidly to IV Diphenhydramine or Benztropine.
* **Hypocalcemia (Tetany)**: Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs are positive. Corrected by calcium replacement.

---

# Investigations
* **Clinical Diagnosis**: Tetanus is a clinical diagnosis.
* **Wound Culture**: Gram stain showing drumstick rods is positive in only $30\%$ of cases. A negative culture does not rule out tetanus.
* **Serum Antitoxin Level**: A level $>0.1$ IU/mL is protective and makes tetanus highly unlikely (but rarely available in acute settings).
* **Electromyography (EMG)**: Shows continuous high-frequency motor unit discharges.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by the clinical presentation of trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonos, or generalized muscle spasms, particularly in the setting of an dirty wound and a positive spatula test.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Airway Protection**: Secure the airway early. Laryngospasm can cause asphyxiation. Perform endotracheal intubation or early tracheostomy under deep sedation.
* **Neutralize Toxin**:
  * **Human Tetanus Immunoglobulin (HTIG)**: Administer **3,000 - 6,000 units IM** immediately. (Neutralizes circulating toxin, preventing further binding to motor terminals).
* **Wound Debridement**:
  * Perform thorough debridement to eliminate the anaerobic focus and stop toxin production.
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **Debridement Timing**: Always administer HTIG *before* performing wound debridement. Manipulating the wound mechanically releases unbound toxin into the bloodstream, which must be neutralized by circulating antibodies first.
* **Eradicate Bacteria**:
  * **Metronidazole 500 mg IV every 6 hours** for 7-10 days (antibiotic of choice; suppresses vegetative *C. tetani*).
  * > [!WARNING]
  * **Avoid Penicillin G**: Historically used, Penicillin G is now avoided because it is a competitive GABA antagonist and can act synergistically with tetanospasmin to worsen muscle spasms.

## Definitive Management
* **Control Spasms**:
  * **Benzodiazepines**: High-dose **IV Diazepam** (continuous infusion or $5-10$ mg boluses) or Lorazepam. Potentiates GABA transmission, reversing the toxin's effect.
  * *Refractory*: If spasms compromise respiration despite high-dose benzos, paralyze the patient with a continuous neuromuscular blocker (e.g., Vecuronium) and maintain mechanical ventilation.
* **Autonomic Control**:
  * **IV Magnesium Sulfate**: Excellent afterload reducer and sympathetic blocker. Administer $2-4$ g IV loading dose, then $1-2$ g/h infusion. Keeps epinephrine levels stable and controls spasms.
* **Active Immunization (Mandatory)**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **Active Vaccination Rule**: Tetanus infection does *not* confer immunity, because the lethal dose of tetanospasmin is too small to stimulate an antibody response. All patients must receive a complete course of **Tetanus Toxoid (Td/Tdap)** vaccine (dose on Day 0, 1 month, and 6 months) at a separate site from HTIG.

# Complications
* Laryngospasm and asphyxiation.
* Vertebral compression fractures (from violent muscle spasms).
* Autonomic dysfunction (cardiac arrest, severe hypertension).
* Rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury.

# Prognosis
* Mild cases have $<10\%$ mortality.
* Severe or neonatal tetanus has $>50\%$ mortality, particularly without ICU care.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Bite Test**: The spatula test is a simple, high-yield bedside test. A normal patient will gag and push the tongue depressor out. A tetanus patient will reflexively clamp their jaw shut (bite down) due to masseter spasm.
* **Quiet Environment**: Tetanus patients must be managed in a dark, quiet ICU room with minimal auditory and tactile stimuli. Even the sound of a closing door or a light touch can trigger a violent, generalized muscle spasm.
