## type: disease

# Definition
Hyperkinetic movement disorders are a group of neurological conditions characterized by excessive, involuntary, purposeless movements that intrude into normal motor activity. They arise from dysfunction within the basal ganglia pathways and are contrasted with hypokinetic disorders (such as **[[Parkinsonism]]**).

# Key Hyperkinetic Movement Types

| Movement Disorder | Clinical Definition | Key Etiologies | Clinical Features & Treatment |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Essential Tremor** | Postural and action tremor ($8-12$ Hz) affecting the hands, head, or voice. | Familial (autosomal dominant in $50\%$) | **Worsened by posture/action**; **relieved by alcohol**. <br>First-line: **Propranolol** or **Primidone**. |
| **Chorea** | Rapid, irregular, flowing, non-stereotyped involuntary movements. | **Huntington's Disease**, Sydenham's chorea (rheumatic fever), SLE | "Fidgety" movements, milkmaid's grip. <br>Treatment: **Tetrabenazine** (VMAT2 inhibitor). |
| **Dystonia** | Sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing abnormal, repetitive twisting movements/postures. | Idiopathic, drug-induced (antipsychotics), **Wilson's Disease** | Cervical dystonia (torticollis), blepharospasm. <br>Treatment: **Botulinum Toxin Injections** (First-line). |
| **Hemiballismus** | Violent, wild, large-amplitude, flinging movements of unilateral proximal limbs. | Contralateral **Subthalamic Nucleus (STN)** stroke | Sudden onset in elderly. Self-limiting. <br>Treatment: Atypical antipsychotics or tetrabenazine. |
| **Tardive Dyskinesia** | Delayed-onset choreoathetoid movements, classically involving the face, mouth, and tongue. | Chronic use of **dopamine receptor antagonists** (antipsychotics, metoclopramide) | Grimacing, lip-smacking, tongue protrusion. <br>Treatment: Discontinue offending drug; Valbenazine. |
| **Myoclonus** | Sudden, brief, shock-like muscle contractions (positive) or lapses in tone (negative). | Post-anoxic brain injury (Lance-Adams), epilepsy, uremia | Asterixis ("flapping tremor" in hepatic/renal failure is negative myoclonus). <br>Treatment: Clonazepam or Valproate. |

# Pathophysiology
The basal ganglia regulate motor output via two parallel pathways projecting to the thalamus, which in turn excites the motor cortex:
1. **The Direct Pathway**: Excites motor output (pro-kinetic).
2. **The Indirect Pathway**: Inhibits motor output (anti-kinetic).

In hyperkinetic disorders, the balance shifts in favor of the **direct pathway** or there is a **loss of the indirect pathway's inhibitory control** (e.g., degeneration of GABAergic striatal projection neurons in the indirect pathway in Huntington's disease, or injury to the subthalamic nucleus in hemiballismus). This leads to a loss of normal thalamic inhibition, resulting in excessive, uncontrolled motor cortical excitation.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Tremor**: Rhythmic oscillation of limbs. For Essential Tremor, it is absent at rest, appearing during arm extension (postural) or writing/pouring water (action). (Contrast with Parkinson's resting tremor).
* **Chorea**: Flowing, dance-like movements that the patient may try to mask by turning them into purposeful gestures (parakinesia).
* **Dystonia**: Painful, involuntary twisting of the neck (torticollis) or repetitive eyelid squeezing (blepharospasm).
* **Psychiatric & Cognitive Symptoms** (In Huntington's): Severe depression, apathy, impulsivity, leading to progressive dementia.

## Physical Examination
* **Essential Tremor Exam**:
  * Ask the patient to hold their arms extended (postural tremor visible) and perform a finger-to-nose test (terminal kinetic tremor visible).
  * Check **spiral drawing** (shows jagged, irregular lines).
  * General neurological exam is **normal** (no rigidity, no bradykinesia).
* **Huntington's Chorea Exam**:
  * Rapid alternating movements are impaired.
  * **Milkmaid's Grip**: Fluctuating grip pressure when holding the clinician's fingers.
  * **Motor Impersistence**: Inability to keep the tongue protruded for more than a few seconds.
  * Atrophy of the caudate nucleus leads to cognitive decline.
* **Tardive Dyskinesia**:
  * Assess using the **AIMS Scale** (Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale), documenting mouth puckering, chewing movements, and tongue movements inside/outside the mouth.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Parkinsonism]]**: Characterized by a **resting tremor ("pill-rolling" $4-6$ Hz)** that is **asymmetric** and accompanied by **rigidity** (cogwheel), **bradykinesia** (slowness), and postural instability. (Essential tremor is symmetric, action-induced, and has no rigidity/bradykinesia).
* **Wilson's Disease**: Autoimmune/genetic copper accumulation.
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
    > **Wilson's Disease Rule**: Always screen for **Wilson's Disease** (check ceruloplasmin and 24-hour urine copper) in any patient **younger than 40 years** presenting with a new movement disorder (tremor, dystonia, or parkinsonism). It is a highly treatable, reversible cause; missing it leads to liver failure and permanent brain damage.
* **Drug-Induced Akathisia**: Inner restlessness prompting constant movement (pacing, shifting weight), occurring shortly after starting dopamine antagonists.

---

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Genetic Testing (Huntington's)**: Confirms **CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion** in the *HTT* gene on chromosome 4. ($\ge 40$ repeats is fully penetrant).
* **Wilson's Disease Screen**: Low serum **ceruloplasmin**, elevated **24-hour urinary copper**, and a slit-lamp eye exam showing **Kayser-Fleischer rings** in the cornea.
* **Thyroid Panel**: Rule out hyperthyroidism (cause of enhanced physiological tremor).

## Imaging
* **[[MRI Brain]]**:
  * *Huntington's*: Shows bilateral **atrophy of the caudate head**, leading to compensatory enlargement of the lateral ventricles ("box-car ventricles").
  * *Hemiballismus*: Shows a focal ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in the contralateral **subthalamic nucleus**.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Diagnosis is clinical, based on the phenomenology of the involuntary movement (tremor vs. chorea vs. dystonia), and confirmed by genetic testing (Huntington's), metabolic panels (Wilson's), or MRI (hemiballismus).

---

# Management

## Essential Tremor
* **First-line Pharmacotherapy**:
  * **Propranolol**: Start $40$ mg PO daily/bid, titrate to $160 - 320$ mg/day. (Contraindicated in asthma, bradycardia, or heart block).
  * **Primidone** (Anticonvulsant): Start $12.5 - 25$ mg at bedtime, slowly titrate to $100 - 250$ mg/day. (Sedating).
* **Refractory Cases**:
  * **Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)** targeting the **Ventral Intermediate (Vim) nucleus of the thalamus** provides excellent tremor reduction.

## Huntington's Chorea
* **Dopamine Depleting Agents**:
  * **Tetrabenazine** or Deutetrabenazine: Blocks vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), depleting presynaptic dopamine. (Monitor for depression and suicidality).
* **Atypical Antipsychotics**: Low-dose Risperidone or Olanzapine (dopamine receptor blockers).

## Dystonia
* **Focal Dystonia** (e.g., Blepharospasm, Cervical Dystonia):
  * **Intramuscular Botulinum Toxin Injections** directly into hyperactive muscles. Highly effective; repeated every 3 months.
* **Generalized Dystonia**: Oral anticholinergics (Trihexyphenidyl) or Baclofen.

## Tardive Dyskinesia
* Preventative: Regularly review indications for antipsychotics and metoclopramide.
* Treatment: Taper and discontinue the offending agent. If an antipsychotic is still required, switch the patient to **Clozapine** (lowest risk of TD). Treat with **Valbenazine** or Deutetrabenazine (VMAT2 inhibitors).

---

# Complications
* **Social Isolation & Depression**: Severe in patients with disfiguring chorea, tardive dyskinesia, or severe essential tremor (inability to eat/drink in public).
* **Trauma / Falls**: Hemiballismus can cause severe injury from flinging limbs.
* **Aspiration Pneumonia**: In advanced Huntington's due to chorea of bulbar/swallowing muscles.

# Prognosis
* Essential tremor is progressive but does not affect lifespan.
* Huntington's disease is progressive and universally fatal, with death occurring 15-20 years after symptom onset.
* Hemiballismus has a good prognosis, often resolving spontaneously within weeks to months.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!TIP]
  > **Alcohol Diagnostic Test**: A dramatic reduction in tremor intensity after a small amount of alcohol (e.g., a glass of wine) is highly characteristic of **Essential Tremor** and is rarely seen in Parkinsonian or cerebellar tremors.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Tardive Dyskinesia Irreversibility**: Tardive dyskinesia can become **permanent** and irreversible even after the causative antipsychotic is stopped. Screen all patients on chronic neuroleptics using the AIMS scale every 6 months.
