## type: disease

# Definition
An encephalocele is a rare congenital neural tube defect (NTD) characterized by a sac-like protrusion of the brain parenchyma and/or meninges through a herniated opening in the skull. It is caused by an incomplete closure of the cranial vault during embryogenesis.

# Epidemiology
* Rare condition, occurring in approximately 1 in 5,000 to 10,000 live births.
* **Geographical Variation**:
  * *Occipital (Posterior) Encephaloceles*: Most common in Western countries ($75-80\%$ of cases).
  * *Syncipital (Anterior/Frontonasal) Encephaloceles*: Most common in Southeast Asia (specifically Thailand, Myanmar, and Malaysia).

# Etiology
* **Folate Deficiency**: Maternal deficiency in folic acid during the first 28 days post-conception is a major risk factor (impairs neural tube closure).
* **Genetic Factors**: Autosomal recessive inheritance as part of genetic syndromes, most notably **Meckel-Gruber Syndrome** (characterized by occipital encephalocele, microcephaly, polycystic kidneys, and polydactyly).
* **Maternal Risk Factors**: Maternal hyperthermia, uncontrolled diabetes, or exposure to teratogens (valproic acid) during early pregnancy.

# Pathophysiology
1. **Neurulation Failure**: Failure of the cranial neural tube to close during primary neurulation (around day 24-26 of embryogenesis).
2. **Bone Defect (Cranioschisis)**: The overlying mesoderm fails to form the cranial bone (flat bones of skull), leaving a bony defect.
3. **Herniation**: Increased hydrostatic pressure within the developing ventricles pushes the meninges alone (**Meningocele**) or meninges plus neural tissue (**Meningoencephalocele**) through the bony defect, creating a skin-covered herniation sac.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Visible Mass**: A soft, fluctuant, skin-covered pulsatile mass protruding from the midline skull (most commonly at the occiput, or at the glabella, nose bridge, or forehead).
* **Neurological Delays**: Severe developmental delay, intellectual disability, and spastic motor impairment.
* **[[Seizure]]**: Occurs due to cortical dysgenesis or structural disruption of herniated tissue.
* **Vision Changes**: Strabismus, optic nerve hypoplasia, or cortical blindness (especially in occipital cases).

## Physical Examination
* **Head Circumference**: Look for microcephaly (due to herniation of significant brain volume) or macrocephaly (if secondary hydrocephalus is present).
* **Mass Characteristics**:
  * *Pulsatile*: The mass often pulsates in synchrony with the arterial pulse or CSF pressure waves.
  * *Transillumination*: Meningoceles transilluminate brightly, while meningoencephaloceles containing solid brain tissue do not.
  * *Valsalva increase*: The mass increases in size or tension when the infant cries or coughs.
* **Facial Dysmorphism**: Hypertelorism (widely spaced eyes) or broad nasal bridge (classic in anterior frontonasal encephaloceles).

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Nasal Dermoid Cyst**: A non-pulsatile midline nasal mass. Does not communicate with the intracranial space. Normal MRI.
* **Nasal Glioma**: Ectopic glial tissue. May look identical to anterior encephalocele, but has no active fluid communication with the subarachnoid space (no "cough impulse").
* **Dacryocystocele**: Swelling over the lacrimal sac (lateral to nasal bridge). No skull defect on CT.

---

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Maternal Serum Alpha-Fetoprotein (MSAFP)**: Elevated in maternal blood during the second trimester (if the encephalocele is open or poorly epithelialized).

## Imaging
* **Fetal Ultrasonography**: Prenatal screening tool (detects cranial bony defect and fluid-filled sac).
* **Cranial CT (with 3D Reconstruction)**: Excellent to delineate the exact size and location of the bony skull defect.
* **[[Brain MRI]] (Fetal or Postnatal) - Gold Standard**:
  * Crucial to determine the contents of the herniated sac (meninges vs. functional brain parenchyma).
  * Evaluates venous sinus anatomy (magnetic resonance venography [MRV] is needed to ensure major dural venous sinuses are not displaced into the sac, which is vital for surgical planning).

## Special Tests
* **CSF leak test**: If the sac is ruptured, check fluid for **Beta-2 Transferrin** (highly specific marker for CSF).

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Typically diagnosed prenatally via screening ultrasound or postnatally by physical examination showing a midline pulsatile cranial mass, confirmed by CT showing a skull defect and MRI showing intracranial tissue protrusion.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Sac Protection**: If the encephalocele is ruptured or has a thin, translucent membrane, protect it with sterile, warm, saline-moistened non-adherent dressings. Avoid pressure on the sac.
* **Infection Prevention**: Prophylactic IV antibiotics if there is a CSF leak or ruptured membrane to prevent neonatal [[Meningitis]].

## Definitive Management
* **Surgical Reconstruction**:
  * *Timing*: Elective repair in the first few months of life if the sac is fully epithelialized and stable. Emergent surgery is required if there is active CSF leakage or skin breakdown.
  * *Procedure*:
    1. Excision of the herniated sac. Non-functional, gliotic herniated brain tissue is resected.
    2. Water-tight dural closure (frequently utilizing a pericranial graft).
    3. Bony skull reconstruction (autologous bone graft or cranioplasty).
* **Treatment of Hydrocephalus**:
  * Placement of a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt if hydrocephalus develops post-operatively (occurs in up to $50\%$ of posterior cases).

---

# Complications
* **Meningitis / Ventriculitis**: High risk if the sac ruptures.
* **Post-operative Hydrocephalus**: Due to altered CSF hemodynamics after sac closure.
* **Severe Intellectual Disability** & Motor Impairment.

# Prognosis
* *Anterior (Frontonasal) Encephaloceles*: Favorable prognosis ($>80\%$ have normal or near-normal intelligence after successful surgical repair, as they contain little functional brain tissue).
* *Posterior (Occipital) Encephaloceles*: Guarded; up to $50-60\%$ have severe cognitive delay and motor deficits, especially if the ventricles or microcephaly are present.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Geography Clue**: If a child from Southeast Asia presents with a nasal bridge mass and hypertelorism, always suspect an anterior frontonasal encephalocele. Never biopsy or needle-aspirate a midline nasal mass without obtaining a CT/MRI first to rule out intracranial communication, as aspiration can precipitate fatal meningitis.
* **Folic Acid Prevention**: Up to $70\%$ of neural tube defects (including encephaloceles and spina bifida) can be prevented by maternal ingestion of **Folic Acid (400 mcg daily)** starting 1 month before conception and throughout the first trimester.
