## type: disease

# Definition
Parkinsonism is a clinical syndrome characterized by a combination of bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural instability. Parkinson's Disease (PD) is the primary idiopathic neurodegenerative disorder that presents with this syndrome.

# Epidemiology
* Parkinson's Disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer's.
* Affects $1 - 2\%$ of the population older than 60, rising to $4\%$ in those over 80.
* Slightly more common in men than women.

# Etiology
* **Idiopathic (Parkinson's Disease)**: Multifactorial (genetic predisposition + environmental exposure). Genetic mutations (e.g., *LRRK2*, *SNCA*, *GBA*) are present in $10\%$ of cases.
* **Secondary Parkinsonism**:
  * **Drug-Induced Parkinsonism (DIP)**: Blockade of dopamine D2 receptors. Caused by typical antipsychotics (**Haloperidol**), atypical antipsychotics (Risperidone), and antiemetics (**Metoclopramide**, Prochlorperazine).
  * **Vascular Parkinsonism**: Multiple small vessel subcortical infarctions ("lower body parkinsonism").
  * *Toxins*: Carbon monoxide, manganese, MPTP exposure.
* **Atypical Parkinsonism ("Parkinson-Plus" Syndromes)**:
  * **Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP)**
  * **Multiple System Atrophy (MSA)**
  * **Corticobasal Degeneration (CBD)**

# Pathophysiology
1. **Dopaminergic Loss**: Progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the **substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc)** that project to the striatum (caudate and putamen).
2. **Lewy Bodies**: The neuropathological hallmark of PD is the presence of intraneuronal eosinophilic inclusions called **Lewy Bodies**, composed of misfolded and aggregated **alpha-synuclein** protein.
3. **Basal Ganglia Imbalance**: Dopamine loss leads to overactivity of the indirect pathway (inhibiting movement) and underactivity of the direct pathway (promoting movement). The net result is increased inhibitory output from the basal ganglia to the thalamus, which decreases excitatory drive to the motor cortex, presenting as **bradykinesia**.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Asymmetric Motor Symptoms**: Motor signs typically begin unilaterally.
* **Non-Motor Symptoms** (Often precede motor signs by years):
  * **Anosmia** (loss of smell).
  * **REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD)**: Acting out dreams during sleep due to loss of motor muscle atonia.
  * Severe **Constipation**.
  * **Orthostatic Hypotension** and depression.

## Physical Examination
To diagnose Parkinsonism, **Bradykinesia must be present**, plus at least one of the remaining three cardinal signs:

1. **Bradykinesia**: Slowness of movement. Tested by rapid repetitive finger tapping, hand opening/closing, and toe tapping. Look for **decremental amplitude** (movements become progressively smaller and slower). Other signs: masked facies (**hypomimia**), decreased blink rate, soft monotonic voice (hypophonia), and micrographia (small handwriting).
2. **Resting Tremor**: A $4 - 6$ Hz, slow **"pill-rolling"** tremor. Unilateral at onset, present at rest, and disappears during purposeful movement or sleep.
3. **Rigidity**: Increased resistance to passive joint movement.
   * *Cogwheel Rigidity*: A ratchet-like, clicking resistance felt when passive movement is superimposed on a resting tremor.
   * *Lead-pipe Rigidity*: Smooth, uniform resistance throughout the entire range of motion.
4. **Postural Instability**: Tested by the **Pull Test** (standing behind the patient and pulling back on shoulders; taking $>2$ corrective steps is abnormal). Typically a *late* feature in idiopathic PD; early occurrence suggests an atypical parkinsonism.
5. **Gait abnormalities**: **Shuffling gait** (short, hurried steps), decreased arm swing (asymmetric), and **freezing of gait** (hesitancy when turning or walking through doorways).

# Differential Diagnosis

### 📊 Idiopathic PD vs. Atypical "Parkinson-Plus" Syndromes

| Disease / Syndrome | Key Distinguishing Features | Pathology / Biomarkers | Response to Levodopa |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Parkinson's Disease (PD)** | **Asymmetric** motor onset, resting tremor, anosmia, no early falls, no early severe autonomic failure. | **Alpha-synuclein** (Lewy bodies). | **Excellent** response. |
| **Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP)** | **Early postural instability (frequent backward falls)**, **vertical supranuclear gaze palsy** (difficulty looking down), symmetrical rigidity. | **Tau pathology** (neurofibrillary tangles). | Poor / Transient. |
| **Multiple System Atrophy (MSA)** | **Early severe autonomic failure** (orthostatic collapse, urinary incontinence) plus cerebellar signs (MSA-C) or parkinsonism (MSA-P). | **Alpha-synuclein** (Glial cytoplasmic inclusions). | Poor. |
| **Corticobasal Degeneration (CBD)** | **Marked asymmetry**, cortical sensory loss, apraxia, **alien limb phenomenon** (limb moves purposelessly without control). | **Tau pathology**. | Poor. |
| **Drug-Induced Parkinsonism (DIP)** | **Symmetric** onset, history of taking dopamine D2 blockers (Metoclopramide, Haloperidol). | Normal dopamine transporter (DaT) scan. | None. Resolves on drug discontinuation. |

# Investigations
* **Clinical Diagnosis**: Primary diagnostic method.
* **Brain MRI**: Typically normal in idiopathic PD. Essential to rule out secondary causes (vascular stroke, normal pressure hydrocephalus).
  * *PSP Sign*: Midbrain atrophy on sagittal view ("hummingbird" or "penguin" sign).
  * *MSA Sign*: Pons degeneration showing the "hot cross bun" sign on T2-weighted axial view.
* **Levodopa Challenge Test**: A significant improvement in motor symptoms ($>30\%$ reduction in UPDRS motor score) after a single dose of Levodopa/Carbidopa supports idiopathic PD.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria (MDS Clinical Diagnostic Criteria)
Diagnosis of clinically established Parkinson's disease requires:
1. Presence of Parkinsonism (Bradykinesia + at least one of: resting tremor, rigidity).
2. Absence of absolute exclusion criteria (e.g., vertical gaze palsy, severe early autonomic failure, treatment with D2 blockers).
3. At least two supportive criteria (e.g., marked levodopa response, levodopa-induced dyskinesias, rest tremor, olfactory loss).

# Management

## Initial Management
The choice of initial medication depends on age, cognitive status, and severity of symptoms:

* **Patients $< 60$ years (with mild-moderate symptoms)**:
  * Initiate **Dopamine Agonists** (**Pramipexole**, Ropinirole) or MAO-B Inhibitors (Rasagiline) as monotherapy. This delays the initiation of Levodopa and postpones the onset of levodopa-induced motor complications.
  * *Anticholinergics (Trihexyphenidyl)*: Effective for severe tremor in young patients; avoid in elderly due to side effects.
* **Patients $\ge 60$ years or with severe cognitive/motor impairment**:
  * Initiate **Levodopa / Carbidopa (Sinemet)** immediately.

## Definitive Management
* **Levodopa / Carbidopa Titration**:
  * **Levodopa** is the direct precursor to dopamine.
  * **Carbidopa** is a peripheral dopa-decarboxylase inhibitor. It does not cross the BBB. It prevents the peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine, increasing its brain bioavailability and eliminating peripheral side effects (nausea, vomiting, orthostatic hypotension).
  * *Dosing*: Start 100/25 mg PO tid with meals.
* **COMT Inhibitors (Entacapone)**:
  * Prevents breakdown of levodopa. Co-administered with Levodopa to reduce **"wearing-off"** symptoms (end-of-dose motor deterioration).
* **Amantadine**:
  * NMDA receptor antagonist; primarily used to treat **Levodopa-Induced Dyskinesias** (involuntary writhing/choreic movements that occur at peak drug levels).
* **Neurosurgical (Deep Brain Stimulation - DBS)**:
  * Indicated for patients with advanced PD who have severe motor fluctuations ("on-off" phenomena) or dyskinesias, but have *no cognitive impairment*. Targets: Subthalamic Nucleus (STN) or Globus Pallidus Internus (GPi).

# Complications
* **Motor Fluctuations**: "Wearing-off" (symptoms return before the next dose) and "On-Off" phenomenon (unpredictable, abrupt transitions from mobile ["on"] to completely immobile ["off"]).
* **Aspiration Pneumonia**: Due to progressive dysphagia and impaired cough reflex.
* **Falls and Fractures**: Due to postural instability.
* **Psychiatric**: Dopamine agonists can trigger **Impulse Control Disorders** (pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive buying). Haloperidol cannot be used to treat hallucinations in PD (worsens parkinsonism); use **Quetiapine** or Clozapine.

# Prognosis
Progressive, chronic disease. Survival is relatively unchanged with modern therapy, but quality of life deteriorates over $10-15$ years, with high rates of dementia in the late stages (Parkinson's Disease Dementia).

# Clinical Pearls
* **Metoclopramide (Plasil) Warning**: A very common cause of drug-induced parkinsonism in hospital wards. Elderly patients treated with Metoclopramide for nausea can develop severe bradykinesia and tremor. Always review medications in any patient with new-onset parkinsonism.
* **Trihexyphenidyl in Elderly**: Never prescribe Trihexyphenidyl (Artane) to an elderly parkinsonian patient. It has strong anticholinergic properties that precipitate acute delirium, visual hallucinations, urinary retention, and worsen glaucoma Beers list medication.
