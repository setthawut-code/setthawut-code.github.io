## type: disease

# Definition
Migraine is a chronic, episodic neurovascular disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of moderate-to-severe, throbbing, typically unilateral headache. It is frequently accompanied by autonomic symptoms (nausea, vomiting) and sensory hypersensitivity (photophobia, phonophobia), and can occur with or without a preceding transient neurological aura.

# Epidemiology
* Affects approximately $12\%$ of the global population, peaking in young adulthood ($30-40$ years).
* Three times more common in post-pubertal women than men, largely driven by estrogen fluctuations.

# Etiology
* **Genetic Predisposition**: Strong polygenic inheritance. Rare monogenic forms include *Familial Hemiplegic Migraine* (mutations in *CACNA1A*, *ATP1A2*, or *SCN1A* genes affecting calcium, sodium, or potassium channels).
* **Environmental Triggers**: Stress, sleep deprivation, hormonal fluctuations (drop in estrogen prior to menstruation), weather changes, sensory stimuli (bright lights, strong odors), and dietary factors (red wine, aged cheeses containing tyramine, nitrates, artificial sweeteners).

# Pathophysiology
Migraine is not a simple vascular headache; it is a complex CNS disorder:
1. **Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD)**: A self-propagating wave of neuronal and glial depolarization followed by hyperpolarization that spreads slowly ($2-6$ mm/min) across the cerebral cortex (typically originating in the visual occipital cortex). CSD is the neurophysiological correlate of the **migraine aura** and is believed to activate trigeminal nociceptors.
2. **Trigeminovascular Activation**: Nociceptive afferents from the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (innervating meningeal vessels and dura) release vasoactive neuropeptides: **Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP)**, Substance P, and Neurokinin A.
3. **Neurogenic Inflammation**: These neuropeptides trigger vasodilation of dural blood vessels, mast cell degranulation, and plasma extravasation, creating sterile neurogenic inflammation.
4. **Sensitization**:
  * *Peripheral Sensitization*: Causes the throbbing quality of the headache, exacerbated by physical activity (pulsing of vessels stimulates sensitized nerve endings).
  * *Central Sensitization*: Leads to **allodynia** (pain from normally non-painful stimuli, e.g., brushing hair).

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Classic Migraine Attack Phases**:
  1. *Prodrome (Hours to days before)*: Irritability, food cravings, fatigue, yawning, or neck stiffness.
  2. *Aura (Minutes before)*: Transient neurological symptoms (see below).
  3. *Headache (4 - 72 hours)*: Unilateral, pulsating pain.
  4. *Postdrome (Days after)*: "Migraine hangover" - fatigue, difficulty concentrating.
* **The POUND Mnemonic** (for clinical recognition of migraine):
  * **P** - **P**ulsating / Throbbing quality.
  * **O** - **O**nset unilateral (affects one side of head).
  * **U** - **U**ncomfortable nausea and/or vomiting.
  * **N** - **N**euro-sensory hypersensitivity: **photophobia** and **phonophobia**.
  * **D** - **D**uration of **4 to 72 hours** (untreated or unsuccessfully treated).

## Physical Examination
* **Neurological Exam**: Typically normal in idiopathic migraine.
* **Cutaneous Allodynia**: Pain felt when lightly touching the scalp or face during an attack.
* **Trigger Point Tenderness**: Tenderness in the temporalis, masseter, or suboccipital muscles.

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Tension Headache**: Bilateral, non-pulsating ("band-like" pressure), mild-to-moderate intensity, not aggravated by physical activity, no nausea/vomiting, and either photophobia or phonophobia is absent.
* **Cluster Headache**: Severe, strictly unilateral, periorbital pain lasting $15-180$ minutes, accompanied by ipsilateral autonomic signs (lacrimation, rhinorrhea, miosis, ptosis).
* **Secondary Headaches (Life-threatening)**: Subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, giant cell arteritis, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, or brain tumor.

---

# Investigations

## Clinical Diagnosis & SNOOP4 Red Flags
Migraine is a clinical diagnosis. Neuroimaging is not indicated unless the patient presents with **SNOOP4 red flag features** suggesting a secondary headache:

* **S** - **Systemic symptoms** (fever, weight loss) or secondary risk factors (history of cancer, HIV).
* **N** - **Neurologic deficits** or abnormal examination findings (confusion, hemiparesis).
* **O** - **Onset sudden** ("thunderclap" headache, reaching peak in $<1$ minute $\rightarrow$ suspect SAH).
* **O** - **Older age** of onset ($> 50$ years old $\rightarrow$ suspect Giant Cell Arteritis or tumor).
* **P** - **Pattern change** (progressive headache, new type, or loss of previous headache control).
* **P** - **Precipitated by Valsalva** maneuver, coughing, or bending over (indicates raised ICP).
* **P** - **Papilledema** on fundoscopy.
* **P** - **Pregnancy** or postpartum state.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria (ICHD-3 criteria for Migraine without Aura)
* At least **5 attacks** fulfilling the following:
  1. Headache attacks lasting **4 to 72 hours** (untreated or unsuccessfully treated).
  2. Headache has at least two of: unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate/severe intensity, aggravation by routine physical activity.
  3. During headache, at least one of: nausea and/or vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia.
  4. Not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis.

---

# Management

## Initial Management (Acute Abortive Therapy)
Administer therapy early in the attack. Limit use to **$< 10$ days per month** to prevent Medication Overuse Headache (rebound headache).

* **Mild-to-Moderate Attacks**:
  * **NSAIDs**: Naproxen ($500 - 1000$ mg), Ibuprofen ($400 - 800$ mg), or Acetaminophen.
  * **Antiemetics**: IV/PO **Metoclopramide** 10 mg or Prochlorperazine (treats nausea and enhances gastric emptying/drug absorption).
* **Moderate-to-Severe Attacks**:
  * **Triptans (5-HT 1B/1D Receptor Agonists)**:
    * *Medications*: **Sumatriptan** (50-100 mg PO, 6 mg SC, or 20 mg intranasal), Rizatriptan, Zolmitriptan.
    * *Mechanism*: Bind to presynaptic 5-HT 1B/1D receptors on trigeminal nerves, preventing CGRP release and causing cranial vasoconstriction.
> [!WARNING]
Triptan Contraindications: Do not prescribe triptans to patients with coronary artery disease, history of stroke/TIA, uncontrolled hypertension, peripheral vascular disease, or hemiplegic migraine, due to the risk of arterial vasoconstriction.
  * **Newer Agents** (No vasoconstrictive properties):
    * *Gepants (CGRP Antagonists)*: Rimegepant, Ubrogepant.
    * *Ditans (5-HT 1F Agonist)*: Lasmiditan.

## Definitive Management (Prophylactic Therapy)
Indicated if the patient has $\ge 3 - 4$ debilitating attacks per month, fails acute therapy, or has hemiplegic migraine.

* **First-line Oral Prophylaxis**:
  1. **Beta-blockers**: **Propranolol** ($40 - 240$ mg/day), Metoprolol. *Contraindicated in asthma, severe bradycardia, or depression.*
  2. **Anticonvulsants**:
     - **Topiramate**: First-line. *Side effects: paresthesias, weight loss, nephrolithiasis (kidney stones), cognitive slowing ("dope-amax").*
     - **Sodium Valproate**: Highly effective. *Contraindicated in pregnancy.*
  3. **Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)**:
     - **Amitriptyline** ($10 - 75$ mg at bedtime). Preferred for patients with concurrent insomnia or tension-type headache. *Side effects: sedation, dry mouth, constipation, weight gain.*
* **CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies** (Injected monthly):
  * Erenumab (targets CGRP receptor), Galcanezumab, Fremanezumab (target CGRP ligand). Highly effective with minimal side effects.

---

# Complications
* **Status Migrainosus**: A debilitating migraine attack lasting $> 72$ hours. Treated with IV fluids, IV antiemetics, IV dexamethasone, and IV valproate or dihydroergotamine (DHE).
* **Medication Overuse Headache**: Chronic daily headache caused by frequent use of abortive medications ($>10$ days/month for triptans/opioids; $>15$ days/month for NSAIDs). Requires complete withdrawal of offending drugs.
* **Migrainous Infarction**: Ischemic stroke occurring during a typical migraine with aura.

# Prognosis
Fluctuating course; often improves spontaneously post-menopause. Highly treatable with appropriate prophylactic adjustments.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Aura duration**: A true migraine aura always develops gradually over $>5$ minutes and resolves completely within $60$ minutes. If neurological symptoms occur abruptly ("like a stroke") or last $>60$ minutes, obtain neuroimaging to rule out TIA or ischemic stroke.
* **Estrogen Contraception Warning**: Women who have **migraine with aura** have a 2- to 4-fold increased baseline risk of ischemic stroke. The use of **combined oral contraceptive pills (estrogen-containing)** is contraindicated in these patients, as estrogen further increases the risk of stroke. Progestin-only contraceptives are safe.
