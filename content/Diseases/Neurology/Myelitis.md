## type: disease

# Definition
Myelitis is an inflammatory disorder of the spinal cord parenchyma, resulting in axonal injury and demyelination. When the inflammation extends across the entire cross-section of the spinal cord, it is termed **Transverse Myelitis (TM)**, which causes bilateral motor, sensory, and autonomic deficits below the level of the lesion.

# Epidemiology
* Rare condition, with an annual incidence of $1 - 4$ cases per million.
* Bimodal age distribution: peaks between $10 - 19$ years and $30 - 39$ years. No gender predisposition.

# Etiology
Myelitis can be idiopathic, post-infectious, or secondary to systemic autoimmune and demyelinating diseases:
* **Demyelinating Diseases**:
  * **Multiple Sclerosis (MS)**: Typically causes partial, asymmetric transverse myelitis (lesion $<2$ vertebral segments in length).
  * **Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder (NMOSD)**: Mediated by **Anti-Aquaporin-4 (AQP4) Antibodies**. Characteristically causes **Longitudinally Extensive Transverse Myelitis (LETM)** (lesion $\ge 3$ vertebral segments).
  * **MOG Antibody-Associated Disease (MOGAD)**: Mediated by **Anti-Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein (MOG) Antibodies**.
* **Systemic Autoimmune Diseases**:
  * Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) (Lupus myelitis).
  * Sjögren's Syndrome, Sarcoidosis.
* **Infectious Myelitis (Direct Invasion)**:
  * *Viral*: Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1/2), West Nile Virus, Enteroviruses, HIV, HTLV-1 (tropical spastic paraparesis).
  * *Bacterial/Spirochetal*: Neurosyphilis (*Treponema pallidum*), Lyme disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*), Tuberculosis (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*).
* **Idiopathic Transverse Myelitis**: Diagnosis of exclusion; often post-vaccination or post-infectious molecular mimicry.

# Pathophysiology
1. **Inflammatory Infiltration**: Focal infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, macrophages, and B cells into the spinal cord parenchyma.
2. **Demyelination & Axonal Injury**: The inflammatory cascade triggers astrocyte and oligodendrocyte damage, leading to demyelination and axonal degeneration.
3. **Pathway Interruption**: This interrupts the descending motor tracts (corticospinal), ascending sensory tracts (spinothalamic and dorsal columns), and autonomic pathways, presenting as a complete functional transection of the spinal cord.
4. **Spinal Shock**: During the acute phase of a sudden spinal cord lesion, there is temporary physiological depression of all reflex activity below the lesion, presenting as flaccid paralysis (resembling GBS). Over weeks, as spinal shock resolves, hyperreflexia and spasticity develop.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Sensory Level**: A rapid-onset, bilateral loss of sensation that ascends or localizes to a **sharp horizontal boundary (sensory level)** on the trunk or limbs.
* **Motor Weakness**: Rapidly progressive bilateral leg weakness (paraparesis) or all four limbs (quadriparesis, if cervical lesion).
* **Autonomic Dysfunction**: Urinary urgency, urinary retention (inability to void), fecal incontinence, or constipation.
* **Radicular Pain**: Band-like thoracic or abdominal pain corresponding to the level of inflammation.

## Physical Examination
* **Sensory Level Determination**: Pinprick or temperature testing reveals a distinct demarcation line below which sensation is absent (e.g., T4 at the nipple line, T10 at the umbilicus).
* **Motor Deficits**:
  * *Acute Phase (Spinal Shock)*: Flaccid paralysis, **hyporeflexia or areflexia** below the lesion, and hypotonia.
  * *Chronic Phase (Upper Motor Neuron Signs)*: Develops weeks later. **Spasticity**, hyperactive deep tendon reflexes (hyperreflexia), clonus, and a **positive Babinski sign** (extensor plantar response).
* **Autonomic Signs**:
  * Palpable distended bladder (due to urinary retention).
  * **Autonomic Dysreflexia**: Occurs in lesions above **T6**. Noxious stimuli below the lesion (e.g., full bladder or fecal impaction) trigger uncontrolled sympathetic activation, causing life-threatening hypertension, bradycardia (reflex), headache, and sweating above the lesion.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Acute Spinal Cord Compression**: Epidural abscess, hematoma, or metastatic spinal tumor. **Mandatory to rule out first** via emergency MRI, as compression requires urgent surgical decompression.
* **[[Guillain-Barre Syndrome]] (GBS)**: Presents with ascending flaccid paralysis and areflexia, but has **no sensory level**, features early sensory paresthesias rather than dense loss, and has prominent cranial nerve involvement.
* **Spinal Cord Infarction (Anterior Spinal Artery Syndrome)**: Sudden vascular occlusion. Spares the dorsal columns (vibration and proprioception are normal), whereas transverse myelitis involves all sensory modalities.

---

# Investigations

## Imaging
* **Spinal Cord MRI (with gadolinium contrast) - Diagnostic Gold Standard**:
  * Mandatory to rule out mechanical cord compression.
  * Demonstrates **T2-weighted hyperintensity** within the spinal cord parenchyma, with variable gadolinium enhancement.
  * *LETM (Longitudinally Extensive Transverse Myelitis)*: Hyperintensity spanning **$\ge 3$ contiguous vertebral segments** on sagittal view (highly suggestive of **NMOSD**).
  * *Short-segment TM*: Hyperintensity spanning **$< 3$ segments** (suggests **Multiple Sclerosis**).

## Laboratory & Serology
* **Neuromyelitis Optica IgG (Anti-AQP4 Antibody)**: Checked in all patients with LETM. High specificity ($>99\%$).
* **Anti-MOG Antibody**: If AQP4 is negative.
* **Autoimmune Panel**: ANA, anti-dsDNA, ENA (lupus myelitis screening).
* **CSF Analysis**:
  * Shows lymphocytic pleocytosis ($10-100$ cells/$\mu$L) and moderately elevated protein.
  * **Oligoclonal Bands (OCBs)**: Positive in up to $90\%$ of MS-related myelitis cases; typically negative in idiopathic TM or NMOSD.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Requires bilateral (though not necessarily symmetric) sensory, motor, or autonomic dysfunction localized to the spinal cord + a clear sensory level + evidence of spinal cord inflammation (CSF pleocytosis/IgG index or MRI gadolinium enhancement) + exclusion of extra-medullary compression.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **High-Dose Corticosteroid Pulse Therapy**:
  * Initiate immediately on suspicion of transverse myelitis (after ruling out compression via MRI).
  * **Methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3 to 5 days** (reduces spinal cord edema and halts inflammation).
* **Plasma Exchange (Plasmapheresis)**:
  * Indicated for patients with severe myelitis who show **no clinical improvement after 3-5 days of high-dose steroids** (especially in NMOSD). Perform 5 to 7 exchanges.
* **Bladder Management**:
  * Place a **Foley catheter** immediately for acute urinary retention.
* **DVT Prophylaxis**:
  * LMWH (Enoxaparin) + sequential compression devices due to acute paraplegia.

## Definitive Management
* **Neuropathic Pain Control**: Gabapentin, Pregabalin, or Duloxetine.
* **Spasticity Management**: Oral **Baclofen** or Tizanidine.
* **Long-Term Immunosuppression** (For NMOSD or Lupus Myelitis recurrence prevention):
  * **Rituximab** (monoclonal antibody targeting CD20+ B cells), Mycophenolate Mofetil, or Azathioprine.

---

# Complications
* **Autonomic Dysreflexia**: Life-threatening hypertensive crisis in lesions above T6. Treated by elevating head, removing the noxious stimulus (e.g., straight catheterization of bladder), and fast-acting antihypertensives (SL Nifedipine or IV Labetalol).
* **Neurogenic Bladder**: Leading to recurrent UTIs, urinary stones, and hydronephrosis.
* **Pressure Ulcers & DVT**.

# Prognosis
* *Rule of Thirds*:
  * $1/3$ of patients make a full recovery.
  * $1/3$ are left with moderate permanent disability (spastic gait, neurogenic bladder).
  * $1/3$ have severe permanent disability (completely wheelchair-dependent, quadriplegic).

# Clinical Pearls
* **Spinal Shock Pitfall**: Do not diagnose a peripheral nerve lesion (like GBS) just because the patient has flaccid paralysis and absent reflexes. If a **sharp sensory level** is present, or if the bladder is paralyzed, the lesion is in the spinal cord (spinal shock phase).
* **Anti-AQP4 Pathology**: Aquaporin-4 is a water channel located on the foot processes of **astrocytes**. NMOSD is therefore primarily an astrocytopathy rather than a myelinopathy (unlike Multiple Sclerosis).
