## type: disease

# Definition
Hemiplegia is the complete or severe loss of motor function (paralysis) on one side of the body. Hemiparesis refers to mild-to-moderate weakness on one side. Both represent clinical manifestations of a lesion in the contralateral upper motor neuron (UMN) pathway, extending from the motor cortex to the decussation in the medulla, or ipsilateral UMN pathway in the spinal cord.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Very common neurological finding, most frequently caused by **[[Cerebrovascular Disease]]** (Stroke).
* **Mortality/Morbidity**: The prognosis and impact on activities of daily living (ADLs) are highly dependent on the underlying etiology and the speed of acute intervention.

# Etiology
Hemiplegia can be caused by vascular, neoplastic, infectious, inflammatory, or traumatic processes:

1. **Vascular (Most Common)**:
   * Ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, subdural or epidural hematoma (detailed in **[[Intracranial Hemorrhage]]**).
2. **Neoplastic**:
   * Primary brain tumors (e.g., glioblastoma) or metastatic brain disease (e.g., lung, breast cancer) causing progressive mass effect.
3. **Infectious**:
   * **[[Brain Abscess]]**, viral encephalitis, neurosyphilis, or parasitic infections (e.g., neurocysticercosis).
4. **Inflammatory / Autoimmune**:
   * **[[Demyelinating Disease]]** (e.g., Multiple Sclerosis plaque, ADEM) or CNS vasculitis.
5. **Traumatic**:
   * Traumatic brain injury (TBI) causing axonal shearing or contusions.
6. **Developmental**:
   * **[[Cerebral Palsy]]** (congenital hemiplegic subtype).
7. **Transient Mimics**:
   * **Todd's Paralysis**: Post-ictal transient weakness following a focal seizure.
   * Hemiplegic migraine.

# Pathophysiology
The corticospinal (pyramidal) tract arises from the motor cortex, passes through the internal capsule, travels down the brainstem, decussates (crosses) in the lower medulla, and descends in the lateral funiculus of the spinal cord to synapse on lower motor neurons. 

Localization of the lesion determines the character of the hemiplegia:

```
                            ┌────────────────────────┐
                            │   LOCATION OF LESION   │
                            └───────────┬────────────┘
                                        │
             ┌──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┐
             ▼                          ▼                          ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐┌─────────────────────────┐┌─────────────────────────┐
│     CEREBRAL CORTEX     ││    INTERNAL CAPSULE     ││        BRAINSTEM        │
└────────────┬────────────┘└────────────┬────────────┘└────────────┬────────────┘
             │                          │                          │
   Contralateral weakness     Dense, proportional        Crossed Hemiplegia
   (Non-proportional:         weakness (Face, Arm,       (Ipsilateral CN palsy,
   Arm/Face > Leg) +          Leg equally affected due   contralateral limb
   Cortical signs (Neglect,   to packed fibers)          weakness)
   Aphasia)
```

* **Cerebral Cortex Lesions**: 
  * Cause contralateral hemiplegia that is **non-proportional** (e.g., face and arm weakness $>$ leg weakness in MCA stroke; leg $>$ arm in ACA stroke).
  * Associated with cortical signs: **aphasia** (if dominant hemisphere) or **hemispatial neglect** (if non-dominant hemisphere).
* **Internal Capsule Lesions**:
  * Cause **proportional hemiplegia** (face, arm, and leg are equally and severely weak) because descending fibers are tightly packed together in the posterior limb of the internal capsule.
* **Brainstem Lesions**:
  * Cause **Crossed Hemiplegia**: Ipsilateral cranial nerve deficits (e.g., CN III palsy in midbrain, CN VII palsy in pons) with contralateral hemiplegia of the limbs.
* **Spinal Cord Lesions (above C5)**:
  * Cause **ipsilateral hemiplegia** (sparing the face) because the lesion is below the decussation in the medulla. If half the cord is damaged, this presents as **Brown-Séquard Syndrome** (ipsilateral hemiplegia and loss of vibration/proprioception, with contralateral loss of pain/temperature).

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Onset**: Sudden (suggests ischemic or hemorrhagic vascular cause); subacute/progressive (suggests tumor, abscess, or demyelinating disease).
* **Motor Deficits**: Loss of fine motor coordination in the hand, dragging of the foot, slurred speech (dysarthria).
* **Sensory Deficits**: Co-existing numbness or paresthesias on the same side.

## Physical Examination
* **Acute Phase ("Cerebral Shock")**:
  * Immediately following an acute lesion (e.g., stroke), the affected side exhibits **flaccid tone and hyporeflexia**. This can be mistaken for a lower motor neuron lesion.
* **Chronic Phase (Spastic Hemiplegia)**:
  * Over days to weeks, spasticity and UMN signs develop:
    * **Hypertonia**: Spastic tone (velocity-dependent resistance to passive stretch, showing a "clasp-knife" phenomenon).
    * **Hyperreflexia**: Exaggerated deep tendon reflexes (3+ or 4+).
    * **Clonus**: Rhythmic, involuntary muscle contractions (especially ankle clonus).
    * **Positive Babinski Sign**: Extensor plantar response (dorsiflexion of the great toe and fanning of other toes).
    * **Pyramidal Weakness Pattern**: In the upper limbs, extensors are weaker than flexors (causing a flexed posture). In the lower limbs, flexors are weaker than extensors (causing an extended, circumducting gait).
* **Pronator Drift**:
  * The patient holds both arms extended with palms up and eyes closed. The weak arm will slowly pronate and drift downward (highly sensitive for UMN weakness).

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Functional (Conversion) Disorder**: Weakness is non-anatomical (e.g., normal muscle tone, active resistance, positive Hoover's sign—extension of the normal leg is felt when the patient is asked to flex the paretic leg against resistance).
* **Todd's Paralysis**: Resolves spontaneously within 24 hours. Normal imaging.
* **[[Hypoglycemia]]**: Mimics stroke; completely resolves with dextrose infusion.

---

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Fingerstick Glucose**: Rule out hypoglycemia mimic immediately.
* **Routine Panels**: CBC, electrolytes, renal function.

## Imaging
* **[[CT Brain]] (without contrast)**:
  * **Urgent first-line imaging** to rule out hemorrhagic stroke, subdural hematoma, or large mass lesions before any acute thrombolysis.
* **[[MRI Brain]] (with and without contrast)**:
  * **Gold standard**. Excellent for identifying early ischemic strokes (DWI sequence), demyelinating plaques, brainstem lesions, and differentiating brain tumors from abscesses.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Diagnosis is clinical, based on objective unilateral motor weakness. Localization (cortical, subcortical, brainstem, spinal) is determined by cranial nerve and sensory examination, and confirmed via neuroimaging (CT or MRI).

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **ABCs & Stabilization**: Ensure airway patency. Check blood glucose.
* **Acute Reperfusion**: If within 4.5 hours of ischemic stroke, evaluate for IV thrombolysis (tPA); if large vessel occlusion is present, prepare for mechanical thrombectomy (detailed in **[[Cerebrovascular Disease]]**).
* **Infection Control**: If brain abscess or meningitis is suspected, start empiric IV antibiotics and obtain neurosurgical consultation.

## Definitive Management
* **Rehabilitation (Cornerstone of Care)**:
  * **Physical Therapy (PT)**: Focuses on gait training, balance, and preventing joint contractures.
  * **Occupational Therapy (OT)**: Assists with regaining independence in ADLs, using adaptive equipment.
  * **Speech Therapy (ST)**: Evaluates and treats dysphagia (swallowing difficulties) and dysphasia/aphasia (speech impairments).
* **Spasticity Pharmacotherapy**:
  * **Baclofen** ($5 - 20$ mg PO tid): GABA-B agonist; reduces spastic muscle tone. (Monitor for sedation).
  * **Tizanidine**: $\alpha_2$-adrenergic agonist.
  * **Botulinum Toxin Injections**: Injected directly into spastic muscles (e.g., flexor carpi radialis) to temporarily paralyze them, allowing stretching and functional movement.
* **DVT Prophylaxis**: LMWH or sequential compression devices in the acute flaccid/immobile phase.

---

# Complications
* **Joint Contractures**: Permanent shortening of muscles/tendons due to untreated spasticity (prevented with splinting and passive stretching).
* **Shoulder Subluxation**: Painful downward displacement of the humeral head due to flaccidity of shoulder girdle muscles in the early phase.
* **Pressure Ulcers**: From immobility.
* **Aspiration Pneumonia**: Due to co-existing bulbar weakness and dysphagia.

# Prognosis
* Spontaneous recovery of motor function occurs primarily within the first 3-6 months.
* Poor prognostic factors include: complete flaccidity at 4 weeks, absence of early finger extension, and severe sensory or cognitive neglect.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Pronator Drift Specificity**: Pronator drift is one of the most sensitive signs for detecting subtle UMN weakness. If the patient's arm drifts down *without* pronation, suspect functional weakness or cerebellar disease (drift is usually outward/upward in cerebellar lesions).
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Hoover's Sign**: To distinguish conversion disorder from organic hemiplegia, perform the Hoover test. In organic hemiplegia, when the patient attempts to flex the normal leg against resistance, no downward pressure is felt under the paretic heel. In functional weakness, downward pressure under the paretic heel is felt (due to involuntary synkinesis).
