## type: disease

# Definition
Hydrocephalus is the abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the ventricular system of the brain. This leads to ventricular dilatation, compression of surrounding brain tissue, and, in many cases, elevated intracranial pressure ([[Acute Increased Intracranial Pressure]] / ICP).

# Epidemiology
* **Congenital**: Occurs in 1 to 2 per 1,000 live births.
* **Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)**: Primarily affects the elderly, with a prevalence of $1 - 2\%$ in those over 80 years. Often misdiagnosed as Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease.

# Classification & Etiology
Hydrocephalus is broadly classified based on the mechanism of CSF accumulation:

```
Hydrocephalus Classifications
└── 1. Non-Communicating (Obstructive)
    ├── Physical blockage within the ventricular system (before CSF exits lateral foramina)
    └── Causes: Aqueductal stenosis, colloid cyst of 3rd ventricle, Pineal tumor, Chiari malformation
└── 2. Communicating (Non-Obstructive)
    ├── Impaired CSF absorption at arachnoid granulations or CSF overproduction (rare)
    └── Causes: Post-meningitis adhesions, post-hemorrhagic (SAH clot blocking granulations)
└── 3. Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)
    ├── Form of communicating hydrocephalus in the elderly with normal/intermittent high pressure
    └── Causes: Idiopathic, or secondary to previous SAH/trauma
└── 4. Hydrocephalus Ex Vacuo (Pseudo-hydrocephalus)
    ├── Ventricular enlargement secondary to generalized brain atrophy (e.g., Alzheimer's)
    └── Pathophysiology: ICP is normal; ventricles expand to fill the void of lost brain tissue (no shunt needed!)
```

# Pathophysiology
1. **Normal CSF Circulation**: CSF is produced by the choroid plexus in the lateral ventricles $\rightarrow$ flows through the **Foramina of Monro** to the 3rd ventricle $\rightarrow$ passes through the narrow **Aqueduct of Sylvius** to the 4th ventricle $\rightarrow$ exits via the **Foramina of Luschka & Magendie** into the subarachnoid space $\rightarrow$ circulates over the cerebral hemispheres $\rightarrow$ absorbed by the **arachnoid granulations** into the superior sagittal dural venous sinus.
2. **Pathogenesis of Ventricular Expansion**: A mismatch between CSF production (rarely elevated, e.g., choroid plexus papilloma) and CSF absorption or flow leads to fluid accumulation. Ventriculomegaly stretches the overlying white matter fibers, particularly the **corona radiata** fibers surrounding the lateral ventricles (which carry motor fibers to the legs and sacral fibers controlling micturition).
3. **NPH Pathophysiology**: Chronic, slow ventricular dilation. Because the ventricles expand slowly, the CSF pressure is normalized or only intermittently elevated. However, the expanding ventricles still physically compress the adjacent periventricular white matter tracts, leading to the classic clinical triad.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Acute High ICP Symptoms** (typical in obstructive or acute communicating hydrocephalus): Progressive generalized **[[Headache]]**, projectile vomiting, irritability, somnolence, and diplopia.
* **Chronic / Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH) Triad**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **The "Wet, Wacky, and Wobbly" Mnemonic**:
    1. **Wobbly (Gait Disturbance)**: The **earliest and most prominent** feature. Characterized as a apraxic, "magnetic" gait (the patient's feet appear glued to the floor), short steps, wide base, and difficulty turning.
    2. **Wacky (Dementia)**: Cognitive decline featuring psychomotor slowing, apathy, loss of short-term memory, and executive dysfunction (subcortical dementia profile).
    3. **Wet (Urinary Incontinence)**: Due to compression of sacral motor fibers in the corona radiata. Presents as urgency, progressing to complete detrusor instability and involuntary voiding.

## Physical Examination
* **Infantile / Pediatric Signs**:
  * **Macrocephaly**: Rapidly increasing head circumference.
  * Bulging, tense anterior fontanelle with widened cranial sutures.
  * **"Setting-Sun" Sign (Sunset Eyes)**: Downward deviation of both eyes with sclera visible above the iris (caused by compression of the midbrain tectum / Parinaud's syndrome).
  * Prominent, engorged scalp veins.
* **Adult Signs**:
  * Papilledema on fundoscopy.
  * Hyperreflexia and spasticity in the lower extremities (due to corticospinal tract compression).

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Hydrocephalus Ex Vacuo**: Enlarged ventricles due to Alzheimer's or vascular dementia. Differentiated by **prominent cortical sulci** on CT/MRI matching the ventricular size, and absence of gait disturbance as the presenting symptom.
* **Parkinson's Disease**: Gait instability and bradykinesia. Differentiated by the presence of a resting tremor, rigidity, asymmetric onset, and absence of cognitive/urinary symptoms early on.
* **Vascular Dementia**: Stepwise cognitive decline with focal deficits on exam.

---

# Investigations

## Imaging
* **[[CT Brain]] or [[Brain MRI]]**:
  * Demonstrates **ventriculomegaly disproportionate to cortical sulcal thinning** (confirms true hydrocephalus rather than ex vacuo atrophy).
  * **Transependymal Flow of CSF**: Visualized as **periventricular lucency** on CT, or hyperintensity on MRI FLAIR/T2 surrounding the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles (represents high-pressure CSF leaking through the ependymal lining into the brain parenchyma).
  * Look for narrowing of the aqueduct of Sylvius (suggests stenosis) or mass lesions.

## Special Tests
* **Large-Volume CSF Tap Test (Diagnostic for NPH)**:
  1. Perform a baseline quantitative assessment of the patient's gait and cognitive function.
  2. Perform a **[[Lumbar Puncture]]** and remove **$30 - 50$ mL of CSF**.
  3. Re-assess gait and cognition $2 - 4$ hours post-tap.
  * *Interpretation*: A **significant, visible improvement in gait velocity or step height** confirms the diagnosis of NPH and predicts a $>90\%$ chance of successful response to permanent surgical shunting.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by neuroimaging showing ventriculomegaly disproportionate to atrophy, combined with appropriate clinical findings (e.g., sunset eyes in infants; wet/wacky/wobbly triad in NPH), and positive response to a CSF tap test (for NPH).

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Acute Obstructive Hydrocephalus**: Medical emergency.
  * Elevate HOB to 30 degrees, optimize ventilation.
  * Administer Mannitol or Hypertonic Saline temporarily if signs of herniation are present.
  * Consult neurosurgery immediately for emergent decompression.

## Definitive Management
* **Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) Shunt**:
  * The standard treatment for communicating hydrocephalus, NPH, and many obstructive cases. Diverts excess CSF from the lateral ventricles via a subcutaneous one-way valve catheter into the peritoneal cavity (where it is absorbed).
  * *Shunt settings*: Programmable valves are preferred, allowing non-invasive adjustment of CSF flow rates using a magnetic device.
* **Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV)**:
  * Preferred treatment for **non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus** (e.g., aqueductal stenosis).
  * An endoscope is passed into the third ventricle, and a small fenestration is made in its floor, allowing CSF to bypass the obstruction and drain directly into the prepontine basal cisterns, avoiding a permanent VP shunt implant.

---

# Complications
* **Shunt Infection**: Typically occurs within 6 months of insertion. Most common pathogen is **Staphylococcus epidermidis** or *Staphylococcus aureus*. Requires shunt removal, external ventricular drain (EVD) placement, IV antibiotics, and subsequent replacement of a new shunt.
* **Shunt Malfunction / Occlusion**: Presents with acute recurrence of high ICP or NPH symptoms.
* **Subdural Hematoma**: Occurs due to **over-drainage** of CSF. The rapid drop in ventricular size causes the brain to shrink away from the skull, tractioning and rupturing the bridging veins. Requires reprogramming the valve to a higher pressure.

# Prognosis
* *NPH*: Gait disturbances respond best to VP shunting ($80\%$ improvement); cognitive impairment responds least ($40-50\%$).
* *Congenital / Obstructive*: Highly curable with ETV or shunt; cognitive outcomes are favorable if treated early.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Magnetic Gait vs. Parkinson's**: NPH patients have a "magnetic" gait—their feet are glued to the floor and they have difficulty lifting them. However, when lying supine, they can simulate bicycling movements easily, confirming that it is a **gait apraxia** (motor planning issue) rather than true muscle weakness or rigidity.
* **The Sachet of Pus**: A cloudy CSF tap post-shunt insertion indicates shunt infection. The hardware must be completely removed; attempting to treat a shunt infection with antibiotics alone without removal will always fail due to bacterial biofilm formation on the plastic catheter.
