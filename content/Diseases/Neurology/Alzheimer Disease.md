## type: disease

# Definition
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative disorder that represents the most common cause of dementia in older adults. It is characterized clinically by a gradual decline in memory, cognition, reasoning, and behavior, ultimately leading to a complete loss of independent functioning.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: The most common cause of dementia, accounting for $60 - 80\%$ of all dementia cases. It affects approximately $10\%$ of individuals aged 65 and older, and up to $40 - 50\%$ of those over 85.
* **Economic & Social Burden**: A major source of healthcare expenditure and caregiver stress globally.

# Etiology
Alzheimer's disease is classified based on age of onset and genetic contributions:
1. **Sporadic Late-Onset AD ($>95\%$ of cases, onset $\ge 65$ years)**:
   * Multifactorial, resulting from a complex interaction of aging, lifestyle, and genetics.
   * The **Apolipoprotein E epsilon 4 (APOE $\epsilon4$)** allele is the strongest genetic risk factor; inheriting one copy increases risk 3-fold, and two copies increases risk 12-fold. APOE $\epsilon2$ is protective.
2. **Familial Early-Onset AD ($<5\%$ of cases, onset $<65$ years)**:
   * Inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.
   * Caused by mutations in three specific genes:
     * **APP** (Amyloid Precursor Protein) on chromosome 21.
     * **PSEN1** (Presenilin 1) on chromosome 14 (most common cause of familial AD).
     * **PSEN2** (Presenilin 2) on chromosome 1.
   * **Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21)**: Virtually all patients with Down syndrome develop AD pathology by age 40 due to gene dosage effects of having three copies of the *APP* gene.

# Pathophysiology
The neuropathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease are extracellular plaques and intracellular tangles, which propagate through a stereotypic neuroanatomical pathway (starting in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus before spreading diffusely to the neocortex):

1. **Extracellular Amyloid-Beta (A$\beta$) Plaques**:
   * Aberrant processing of Amyloid Precursor Protein (APP). Instead of the normal non-amyloidogenic pathway (cleaved by $\alpha$-secretase), APP undergoes sequential cleavage by **$\beta$-secretase (BACE1)** and **$\gamma$-secretase** (a protein complex containing presenilin).
   * This releases insoluble **A$\beta_{42}$ peptides**, which aggregate into oligomers, protofibrils, and eventually extracellular **senile plaques**. These plaques are neurotoxic and trigger local inflammatory/microglial activation.
2. **Intracellular Neurofibrillary Tangles (NFTs)**:
   * **Tau** is a normal microtubule-associated protein that stabilizes axonal microtubules.
   * Under the influence of A$\beta$ accumulation and cellular stress, tau undergoes hyperphosphorylation.
   * Hyperphosphorylated tau detaches from microtubules and aggregates to form intracellular **neurofibrillary tangles**, leading to cytoskeletal collapse and loss of axonal transport.
3. **Synaptic & Cholinergic Loss**:
   * Early and selective degeneration of cholinergic projection neurons in the **nucleus basalis of Meynert**, resulting in a profound deficiency of acetylcholine (ACh) in the neocortex and hippocampus.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
The onset of symptoms is insidious and slowly progressive. It is divided into clinical phases:
* **Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) due to AD**: Objective cognitive impairment in one or more domains (typically short-term memory) but with **preserved activities of daily living (ADLs)**.
* **Mild AD Dementia**:
  * **Amnestic Presentation (Classic)**: Impairment in learning and retaining new information. The patient repeats questions, misplaces items, and forgets recent events, while long-term remote memory is initially spared.
  * Executive dysfunction (difficulty managing finances, planning meals).
  * Anomia (word-finding difficulties) and mild visuospatial disorientation (getting lost in familiar areas).
* **Moderate AD Dementia**:
  * Progressive loss of independence; requires assistance with instrumental ADLs (IADLs: cooking, cleaning, shopping).
  * Significant language impairment (aphasia) and loss of comprehension.
  * **Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia (BPSD)**: Apathy (most common), depression, agitation, sleep disturbances, paranoia, and delusions (e.g., believing spouses are unfaithful or caretakers are stealing).
* **Severe AD Dementia**:
  * Loss of independence in basic ADLs (bathing, dressing, toileting, feeding).
  * Loss of verbal communication (mutism).
  * Motor impairment (rigidity, loss of ambulation, dysphagia).

## Physical Examination
* **Cognitive Assessment**:
  * Administer standardized screening tools: **MMSE** (Mini-Mental State Exam) or **MoCA** (Montreal Cognitive Assessment). In local contexts, the Thai Mental State Examination (TMSE) is used.
* **Neurological Examination**:
  * In mild-to-moderate stages, the neurological exam is **completely normal** (no motor, sensory, or cerebellar deficits).
  * In advanced stages, look for extrapyramidal signs (rigidity, bradykinesia), frontal release signs (grasp, snout, glabellar reflexes), and muscle wasting.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Vascular Dementia**: Characterized by a **stepwise cognitive decline** with sudden drops matching ischemic events, accompanied by focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, asymmetric reflexes) and prominent cardiovascular risk factors.
* **Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB)**: Distinguished by the early triad of: **detailed visual hallucinations**, **spontaneous parkinsonism** (occurring within 1 year of cognitive decline), and **fluctuating attention/alertness**, along with REM Sleep Behavior Disorder.
* **Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD)**: Characterized by early-onset ($40-60$ years) presenting with prominent **personality and behavioral changes** (disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy) or progressive aphasia, with relative preservation of memory early on.
* **Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus ([[Hydrocephalus]] / NPH)**: Triad of **"wet, wobbly, and wacky"** (urinary incontinence, gait ataxia, cognitive decline).
* **Depressive Pseudodementia**: Major depressive disorder in elderly patients presenting as cognitive loss. Differentiated by **intact effort on cognitive testing** (depressed patients often say "I don't know" to avoid trying, whereas AD patients make errors while trying to answer).

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Screening for Reversible Dementias (Mandatory)**:
  1. **Serum Vitamin B12 level**.
  2. **Thyroid Function Test (TSH, Free T4)**.
  3. **Syphilis Serology (RPR/VDRL)**.
  4. **Renal & Liver Function Tests** (to rule out chronic uremic or hepatic encephalopathies).
  5. **HIV antibody screen** (if clinically indicated).

## Imaging
* **[[MRI Brain]] (Preferred)**:
  * Shows **bilateral hippocampal atrophy** (especially prominent in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, which correlates with memory loss) and generalized cortical volume loss (widened sulci and enlarged ventricles).
* **FDG-PET Scan**:
  * Shows characteristic **temporoparietal hypometabolism** (reduced glucose utilization).

## Special Tests
* **CSF Biomarkers** (supportive, used in diagnostic ambiguity):
  * Typically shows **low levels of A$\beta_{42}$** (as it is sequestered in brain plaques) and **elevated total Tau and phosphorylated Tau (p-Tau)**.
* **Amyloid / Tau PET Imaging**:
  * Shows direct deposition of amyloid or tau in cortical regions.

# Diagnostic Criteria
According to DSM-5 criteria for Major Neurocognitive Disorder due to Alzheimer's disease:
1. Clear evidence of decline in memory and learning and at least one other cognitive domain.
2. Steadily progressive, gradual decline in cognition without extended plateaus.
3. No evidence of mixed etiology (e.g., active cerebrovascular disease, Parkinson's disease).
4. Cognitive deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities.

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Patient & Family Counseling**: Discuss safety, future planning, and end-of-life wishes while the patient has decision-making capacity.
* **Environmental Modifications**: Minimize fall risks, establish rigid daily routines, and install door alarms or GPS trackers for patients prone to wandering.
* **Hold Driving Privileges**: Assess driving safety; advise cessation if unsafe.

## Definitive Management
Pharmacotherapy does not cure AD but provides symptomatic benefit:
1. **Cholinesterase Inhibitors** (First-line for mild, moderate, or severe AD):
   * **Donepezil**: Start $5$ mg PO daily at bedtime, titrate to $10 - 23$ mg daily.
   * **Galantamine**: $8 - 24$ mg/day (extended-release daily).
   * **Rivastigmine**: Available as oral or transdermal patch (useful in patients with severe GI side effects).
   * *Mechanism*: Inhibits acetylcholinesterase, increasing acetylcholine availability at synapses.
   * *Side Effects*: Diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, muscle cramps, bradycardia, heart block, and vivid dreams.
2. **NMDA Receptor Antagonist** (Approved for moderate-to-severe AD):
   * **Memantine**: Start $5$ mg PO daily, titrate by 5 mg weekly to a target of $10$ mg PO bid (or $28$ mg extended-release daily).
   * *Mechanism*: Blocks pathological tonic activation of NMDA receptors by glutamate, preventing calcium-mediated excitotoxicity.
   * *Combination Therapy*: Often added to Donepezil for synergistic benefits in moderate-to-severe stages.
3. **Management of Behavioral & Psychological Symptoms (BPSD)**:
   * Non-pharmacological approaches are first-line (sensory stimulation, music therapy, validating feelings).
   * If agitation or psychosis is severe and poses a safety risk, use **low-dose atypical antipsychotics** (e.g., **Risperidone** $0.25 - 1$ mg/day or **Quetiapine**).
   * > [!CAUTION]
     > **Antipsychotic Black Box Warning**: Atypical antipsychotics carry a black box warning in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis due to an increased risk of stroke and all-cause mortality.

# Complications
* **Aspiration Pneumonia**: The most common terminal event, caused by progressive dysphagia and loss of bulbar control.
* **Falls and Hip Fractures**: Secondary to visuospatial deficits and gait instability.
* **Malnutrition & Dehydration**: Due to patient forgetting to eat/drink, or refusing food.
* **Pressure Ulcers**: From immobility in late-stage bedbound states.

# Prognosis
* AD is a progressive, terminal illness.
* The average survival from the time of diagnosis ranges from 3 to 10 years (typically shorter in patients diagnosed at older ages).

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Cholinesterase Inhibitor Bradycardia Rule**: Before starting Donepezil, always obtain an **ECG** to check for bradycardia or baseline AV block. Cholinesterase inhibitors enhance vagal tone and can cause severe bradycardia, leading to **[[Syncope]]** and pacemaker dependency.
* > [!TIP]
  > **Donepezil Timing**: Donepezil is classically given at bedtime to reduce the impact of gastrointestinal side effects (nausea/vomiting). However, if the patient develops severe insomnia or vivid nightmares, switch the dosing to the morning.
