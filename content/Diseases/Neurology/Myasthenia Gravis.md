## type: disease

# Definition
Myasthenia Gravis (MG) is an acquired autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) characterized by fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness and fatigability. The weakness typically worsens with repetitive muscle use or at the end of the day, and improves with rest.

# Epidemiology
* **Bimodal Distribution**: Primarily affects young women in their 20s and 30s, and older men in their 60s and 70s.
* Prevalence is approximately $14 - 20$ cases per 100,000 population.

# Etiology
* **Autoantibody-Mediated**:
  * **Anti-Acetylcholine Receptor (AChR) Antibodies**: Present in $85\%$ of patients with generalized MG and $50\%$ of patients with purely ocular MG.
  * **Anti-Muscle-Specific Receptor Tyrosine Kinase (MuSK) Antibodies**: Present in $5 - 10\%$ of patients (predominantly female, severe bulbar/respiratory weakness, less responsive to pyridostigmine).
  * *Anti-LRP4 Antibodies*: Present in a small subset of "double-seronegative" patients.
* **Thymic Association**:
  * Strongly associated with thymic abnormalities: **Thymic Hyperplasia** ($65\%$ of cases) or **Thymoma** ($15\%$ of cases).

# Pathophysiology
1. **Antibody Binding**: Autoantibodies (primarily IgG1 and IgG3) bind to the postsynaptic nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptors (AChR) at the neuromuscular junction.
2. **Complement Activation**: This triggers the complement cascade, causing structural simplification of the postsynaptic junctional folds and receptor endocytosis.
3. **Transmission Failure**: The reduced density of functional AChRs decreases the amplitude of the endplate potential (EPP). With repetitive stimulation, acetylcholine release depletes, and the EPP falls below the threshold required to trigger a muscle action potential, presenting clinically as muscle weakness and fatigue.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Fluctuating, Fatigable Weakness**: The hallmark of MG. Symptoms are usually minimal in the morning and progress throughout the day.
* **Ocular Manifestations** (Initial presentation in $50-60\%$, eventually present in $90\%$):
  * **Ptosis**: Drooping of the eyelids (often asymmetric, fluctuating).
  * **Diplopia**: Double vision (due to extraocular muscle weakness, does not follow a single cranial nerve distribution; pupils are always spared).
* **Bulbar Manifestations**: Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing, frequent choking), dysarthria (slurred, nasal speech that worsens with prolonged talking), and facial muscle weakness.
* **Proximal Limb Weakness**: "Asymmetric proximal" distribution. Difficulty climbing stairs, arising from a chair, or holding arms above the head (combing hair).
* **Respiratory Weakness**: Dyspnea on exertion or at rest (leads to Myasthenic Crisis).

## Physical Examination
* **Fatigable Ptosis Test**: Ask the patient to look upward continuously for 60 seconds. Gradual worsening of the ptosis is a positive sign of fatigability.
* **Cogan's Lid Twitch**: Ask the patient to look down, then rapidly return to primary gaze. A brief upward overshoot of the eyelid before settling into a ptotic position is positive.
* **"Snarl" Smile**: Weakness of the orbicularis oris muscle causes the patient's smile to look like a snarl (risus sardonicus).
* **Neck Muscle Weakness**: Neck flexor weakness is typically worse than extensor weakness (causing "dropped head syndrome").

# Differential Diagnosis

### 📊 Myasthenia Gravis vs. Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS)

| Feature | Myasthenia Gravis (MG) | Lambert-Eaton Syndrome (LEMS) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pathophysiology** | Autoantibodies against **postsynaptic AChR**. | Autoantibodies against **presynaptic voltage-gated Calcium channels (VGCC)**. |
| **Weakness Pattern** | **Worsens with exercise**; improves with rest. | **Improves with exercise (post-exercise facilitation)**. |
| **Deep Tendon Reflexes** | **Normal / Intact** | **Depressed or Absent** (increases post-exercise). |
| **Autonomic Symptoms** | Absent | Present (dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, constipation). |
| **Malignancy Association**| **Thymoma** ($15\%$) | **Small Cell Lung Cancer** ($50-60\%$). |
| **Repetitive Stimulation**| **Decremental response** ($>10\%$) | **Incremental response** ($>100\%$). |

---

* **Botulism**: Acute descending paralysis, early pupillary involvement (fixed dilated pupils), history of home-canned food ingestion.
* **Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)**: Acute, ascending symmetric paralysis with early loss of deep tendon reflexes, sensory symptoms (paresthesia), and autonomic instability.

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Serum Anti-AChR Antibody Assay**: High specificity ($>99\%$).
* **Serum Anti-MuSK Antibody**: If AChR antibody is negative.
* **Thyroid Function Tests & Autoantibodies**: MG is associated with other autoimmune diseases (Graves' disease).

## Imaging
* **Chest CT / MRI (with contrast)**: Mandatory to screen for **Thymoma** in the anterior mediastinum.

## Special Tests
* **Repetitive Nerve Stimulation (RNS)**: Repetitive electrical stimulation ($2-3$ Hz) of a motor nerve shows a **decremental response ($>10\%$ drop)** in compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude.
* **Single-Fiber EMG (SFEMG)**: The most sensitive diagnostic test. Demonstrates increased **"jitter"** (blocking of action potentials due to unstable NMJ transmission).
* **Bedside Ice Pack Test**: Place an ice pack over the ptotic eyelid for 2 minutes. The cold temperature inhibits acetylcholinesterase, increasing acetylcholine availability. A temporary improvement in ptosis ($>2$ mm) is a positive test (highly sensitive/specific).
* **Tensilon (Edrophonium) Test**: Administer IV Edrophonium (short-acting acetylcholinesterase inhibitor). Temporary improvement in strength confirms MG. *Warning: Can cause severe vagal activation (bradycardia, bronchospasm). Keep IV Atropine at the bedside.*

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by clinical fatigable weakness plus either positive serology (AChR/MuSK) OR supportive electrodiagnostics (decrement on RNS or increased jitter on SFEMG).

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Symptomatic Therapy**:
  * **Pyridostigmine** (Mestinon): Oral acetylcholinesterase inhibitor.
  * *Dosing*: Start $30 - 60$ mg PO 3 to 4 times daily.
  * *Side effects (Cholinergic)*: Abdominal cramps, diarrhea, muscle fasciculations, excessive salivation. Managed with oral Glycopyrrolate or Hyoscyamine.
* **Avoid Worsening Medications**:
 > [!WARNING]
  ***Contraindicated Drugs**: Do not prescribe aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), macrolides, beta-blockers, procainamide, or intravenous magnesium to patients with MG, as they impair NMJ transmission and can precipitate a crisis.

## Definitive Management
* **Immunosuppression**:
  * **Corticosteroids** (Prednisolone): Start at low dose and titrate up slowly. (High initial doses can cause a transient, dangerous worsening of weakness).
  * **Steroid-sparing agents**: Azathioprine or Mycophenolate Mofetil.
* **Thymectomy**:
  * Mandatory for all patients with a thymoma.
  * Recommended for patients aged $15-50$ with generalized, AChR-positive MG without thymoma to increase the probability of complete remission.
* **Myasthenic Crisis Management**:
  * *Definition*: Respiratory failure requiring intubation due to respiratory muscle weakness or severe bulbar weakness causing aspiration.
  * *Monitoring*: Follow **Forced Vital Capacity (FVC)** and **Maximal Inspiratory Pressure (MIP)**. Intubate if **FVC $< 15$ mL/kg** or **MIP $< -20$ cm H2O**.
  * *Rescue Therapy*: **Plasmapheresis (Plasma Exchange)** or **Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)** 2 g/kg divided over 5 days.
  * *Note*: **Hold Pyridostigmine** during mechanical ventilation to minimize airway secretions.

# Complications
* **Myasthenic Crisis**: Acute life-threatening respiratory failure.
* **Cholinergic Crisis**: Weakness caused by Pyridostigmine toxicity/overdose (excessive acetylcholine depolarizes the postsynaptic membrane, causing a depolarizing block). Differentiate from Myasthenic Crisis: cholinergic crisis features miosis, salivation, diarrhea, and fasciculations.

# Prognosis
Excellent with modern therapy. Mortality has dropped to $<5\%$. Most patients achieve normal life expectancy with controlled symptoms.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Pupil Sparing**: Unlike third nerve palsy or botulism, the **pupils are never involved in Myasthenia Gravis**. If a patient presents with ptosis and a fixed, dilated pupil, rule out compression (aneurysm) or botulism.
* **The Steroid Worsening Rule**: When starting oral steroids in a patient with severe generalized MG, admit them to the hospital. Steroids cause a transient increase in muscle weakness in the first 7-10 days, which can precipitate respiratory failure.
