## type: disease

# Definition
Facial Nerve Palsy is an acute, unilateral peripheral dysfunction of the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), resulting in weakness or complete paralysis of the muscles of facial expression on the affected side. **Bell's Palsy** is the most common idiopathic form.

# Epidemiology
* Bell's Palsy is the most common cause of unilateral facial paralysis, with an annual incidence of $20 - 30$ cases per 100,000.
* Higher incidence in pregnant women (especially in the third trimester), diabetic patients, and the elderly.

# Etiology
* **Idiopathic (Bell's Palsy)**: The exact cause is unknown, but most cases are believed to be caused by the reactivation of latent **Herpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1)** or Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) in the geniculate ganglion.
* **Secondary Causes**:
  * **Ramsay Hunt Syndrome (Herpes Zoster Oticus)**: Reactivation of latent VZV in the geniculate ganglion, characterized by vesicles in the external auditory canal.
  * **Lyme Disease** (*Borrelia burgdorferi*): A common cause of bilateral facial nerve palsy.
  * **Tumors**: Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma), parotid gland malignancies, or cholesteatoma.
  * **Systemic Inflammatory**: Sarcoidosis (Heerfordt's syndrome: uveitis, parotid enlargement, and facial palsy).
  * **Autoimmune**: [[Guillain-Barre Syndrome]] (often causes bilateral facial palsy).

# Pathophysiology
1. **Inflammation & Edema**: Viral reactivation or immune-mediated injury triggers inflammation and edema of the facial nerve.
2. **Entrapment**: The facial nerve travels through the narrow, rigid **facial canal (fallopian canal)** of the temporal bone. Swelling of the nerve leads to physical compression against the bony walls, causing ischemia, demyelination, and conduction block.
3. **Branch Blockade**: Compression affects the motor fibers (facial expression), sensory fibers (taste to anterior 2/3 of tongue via chorda tympani), parasympathetic fibers (lacrimal and submandibular secretions), and motor fibers to the stapedius muscle.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Rapid Onset**: Weakness develops rapidly, peaking within 72 hours.
* **Unilateral Weakness**: Inability to close the eye, smile, or frown on the affected side.
* **Hyperacusis**: Sounds seem abnormally loud in the ipsilateral ear (due to paralysis of the stapedius muscle, which normally dampens sound).
* **Loss of Taste**: Impaired taste sensation on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue.
* **Dry Eye**: Decreased tear production (parasympathetic fibers involved), combined with inability to blink.
* **Drooling**: From the drooping corner of the mouth.

## Physical Examination
* **Upper vs. Lower Motor Neuron VII Palsy (The Central-Peripheral Differentiator)**:
  * **Lower Motor Neuron (Peripheral - e.g., Bell's Palsy)**: Involves the **entire half of the face**. The patient **cannot wrinkle the forehead** on the affected side, cannot close the eye, has a flattened nasolabial fold, and has a drooping mouth corner.
  * **Upper Motor Neuron (Central - e.g., Stroke)**: **Spares the forehead**. The patient **can wrinkle the forehead** on both sides, but has weakness in the lower half of the contralateral face. This occurs because the frontalis muscle receives bilateral cortical innervation (contralateral and ipsilateral), whereas the lower face only receives contralateral cortical innervation.
* **Bell's Phenomenon**: When the patient attempts to close the paralyzed eyelid, the eyeball rolls upward and outward (a normal physiological reflex that is visible in GBS/Bell's because the eyelid fails to close).
* **Ear Inspection**: Check the external auditory canal and tympanic membrane for erythematous vesicles (confirms **Ramsay Hunt Syndrome**).
* **Parotid Gland Palpation**: Rule out a parotid mass compressing the extracranial branches of CN VII.

```
Facial Nerve Weakness Localization
├── Contralateral Lower Face Weakness (Forehead spared) -> Central UMN (Stroke)
└── Ipsilateral Entire Face Weakness (Forehead involved) -> Peripheral LMN (Bell's Palsy)
```

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Acute Ischemic Stroke / Intracranial Hemorrhage**: Spares the forehead; patient has other neurological signs (hemiparesis, dysarthria, sensory loss).
* **Ramsay Hunt Syndrome**: Features severe otalgia (ear pain) and vesicular eruptions. Worse prognosis than Bell's; requires antivirals.
* **Parotid Gland Malignancy**: Gradual onset, palpable mass, and persistent focal deficits.
* **Middle Ear Infection / Mastoiditis**: Ear discharge, fever, conductive hearing loss.

---

# Investigations
* **Clinical Diagnosis**: In typical cases of Bell's palsy, no investigations are required.
* **Indication for Neuroimaging (Brain MRI or CT Temporal Bone)**:
  * Gradual progression of facial weakness over $>3$ weeks.
  * Absence of any clinical improvement after 3 months.
  * Association with other cranial nerve deficits or long tract signs.
* **Lyme Serology**: Recommended for patients in Lyme-endemic areas, or those presenting with bilateral facial palsy.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by clinical examination showing acute, unilateral lower motor neuron facial nerve weakness without other cranial nerve, cerebellar, or motor tract deficits.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Systemic Corticosteroids (FIRST-LINE)**:
  * Must be initiated **within 72 hours** of symptom onset.
  * **Prednisolone 60 - 80 mg PO daily for 7 days** (reduces dural edema, limits nerve compression, and significantly increases rates of complete recovery).
* **Antiviral Therapy**:
  * Add **Valacyclovir 1000 mg PO tid** or Acyclovir 400 mg 5 times daily for 7 days in patients with **severe paralysis** (House-Brackmann grade V or VI) or suspected Ramsay Hunt.
* **Critical Eye Care**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **Exposure Keratopathy Prevention**: The inability to blink (lagophthalmos) and decreased lacrimation cause the cornea to dry, leading to corneal abrasions, ulceration, and permanent blindness.
  * *Protocols*:
    1. **Preservative-free artificial tears** (eye drops) every 1-2 hours during the day.
    2. **Lubricating eye ointment** (e.g., Lacri-Lube) at bedtime.
    3. **Eye taping or patching at night** to ensure the eyelid remains physically closed during sleep. Protective eyewear/glasses during the day.

## Definitive Management
* Physical therapy/facial muscle exercises post-acute phase.
* If complete paralysis persists with electrodiagnostic evidence of $>90\%$ axonal degeneration, surgical decompression of the facial nerve within the temporal bone may be considered (controversial).

---

# Complications
* **Exposure Keratopathy & Corneal Ulceration**.
* **Synkinesis (Aberrant Regeneration)**:
  * During recovery, regenerating axons misroute to incorrect muscle groups. Examples: smiling causes involuntary winking of the eye, or **"Crocodile Tears"** (gustatolacrimation—secretory fibers destined for salivary glands misroute to the lacrimal gland, causing the patient to cry whenever they eat).

# Prognosis
* *Bell's Palsy*: Excellent; $70-85\%$ recover completely without treatment, rising to $>90\%$ with early steroid therapy. Recovery begins within 2-3 weeks.
* *Ramsay Hunt*: Guarded; lower rate of complete recovery ($50\%$), often leaving permanent mild weakness.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Forehead Check**: The first and most critical step in evaluating any patient with a "crooked face" is asking them to look up and wrinkle their forehead. If they can wrinkle their forehead, they are having a central event (Stroke!) until proven otherwise. If the forehead is smooth and flat on the weak side, it is a peripheral LMN palsy.
* **Acyclovir in Ramsay Hunt**: Ramsay Hunt syndrome is caused by a much higher viral load of VZV than the HSV-1 in Bell's. Combination therapy with high-dose steroids and antivirals is mandatory, as untreated cases have high rates of permanent facial disfigurement.
