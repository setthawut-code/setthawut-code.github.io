## type: disease

# Definition
Delirium is an acute, fluctuating neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by a disturbance in attention, awareness, and cognition. It represents a state of acute organic brain dysfunction ("acute brain failure") and is always secondary to one or more underlying medical conditions, drug toxicities, or withdrawal states.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Extremely common in hospital settings, affecting $15 - 30\%$ of general medical inpatients, up to $50\%$ of post-operative surgical patients, and up to $80\%$ of patients in the intensive care unit (ICU).
* **Mortality**: Delirium is an independent predictor of poor outcomes, associated with a 2- to 3-fold increase in post-discharge mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and accelerated cognitive decline.

# Etiology
Delirium is multifocal and typically occurs when predisposing patient factors interact with precipitating noxious insults:

| Predisposing Factors (Vulnerability) | Precipitating Factors (Noxious Insults - "Tiggers") |
| :--- | :--- |
| • Advanced age ($\ge 65$ years)<br>• Pre-existing cognitive impairment (**[[Alzheimer Disease]]** / dementia)<br>• Chronic sensory deficits (blindness, hearing loss)<br>• Multiple comorbidities / frailty | • **Drugs**: Anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids, corticosteroids<br>• **Infections**: **[[Urinary Tract Infection]]** (urosepsis), pneumonia, meningitis<br>• **Metabolic**: Hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy<br>• **Withdrawal**: Alcohol (**Delirium Tremens**), benzodiazepines<br>• **Other**: Acute **[[Urinary Retention]]**, fecal impaction, sleep deprivation, physical restraints |

# Pathophysiology
The exact pathophysiology of delirium is incomplete, but is widely understood to involve:
1. **Neurotransmitter Imbalances**:
   * **Cholinergic Deficiency**: Acetylcholine is essential for maintaining attention and alertness. Anticholinergic drugs, hypoxia, and thiamine deficiency impair acetylcholine synthesis, triggering delirium.
   * **Dopaminergic Excess**: Dopamine has an inhibitory effect on acetylcholine release. Dopaminergic overactivity (e.g., from drugs, stress, hypoxia) contributes to hyperactive delirium.
2. **Neuroinflammation**:
   * Peripheral infections (e.g., urosepsis) release pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-$\alpha$) into the bloodstream. These cytokines cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) or activate endothelial cells, leading to microglial activation, astrocytic dysfunction, and localized synaptic impairment.
3. **Impaired Cerebral Metabolism**:
   * Global reduction in oxidative metabolism (due to hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoglycemia, or electrolyte shifts) leads to widespread network desynchronization, clinically presenting as inattention and cognitive deficits.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Acute Onset**: Develops rapidly over hours or a few days.
* **Fluctuating Course**: Symptoms vary widely throughout the day, classically worsening in the evening/night ("sundowning").
* **Attention Deficits**: Inability to focus, maintain, or shift attention.
* **Cognitive Changes**: Disorientation to time and place (rarely to person), short-term memory impairment, disorganized thinking, and incoherent speech.
* **Perceptual Disturbances**: Visual illusions and detailed **visual hallucinations** (common in hyperactive delirium).

## Physical Examination & Subtypes
Delirium is classified into three clinical motor subtypes:

1. **Hyperactive Delirium ($25\%$)**:
   * *Presentation*: Agitation, restlessness, hypervigilance, combativeness, hallucinations, and fast speech.
   * *Exam*: Tachycardia, diaphoresis. Patient is alert but highly disorganized.
2. **Hypoactive Delirium ($50\%$)**:
   * *Presentation*: Somnolence, lethargy, apathy, poverty of speech, and psychomotor retardation.
   * *Exam*: Patient appears depressed or withdrawn. **Frequently missed** or misdiagnosed as depression/dementia, but carries a **higher mortality rate** due to delayed diagnostic evaluation.
3. **Mixed Delirium ($25\%$)**:
   * *Presentation*: Alternates between hyperactive and hypoactive features throughout the day.
* **General Examination**:
   * Palpate the suprapubic area for **urinary retention** and perform a digital rectal exam (DRE) for **fecal impaction** (common, underrecognized causes of delirium in the elderly).
   * Check for signs of localized infection (lung crepitations, neck stiffness).

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Dementia (Major Neurocognitive Disorder)**: Distinguished by **insidious onset** and **stable course** with **intact attention** (except in late stages). (Note: Dementia is the strongest predisposing factor for delirium, and patients can have "delirium superimposed on dementia").
* **Primary Psychiatric Disorders (Schizophrenia, Mania)**: Normal orientation, stable course, auditory rather than visual hallucinations, and **absence of physical/systemic triggers or EEG slowing**.
* **Depression**: Mimics hypoactive delirium, but lacks a fluctuating course, acute onset, or inattention on formal screening.

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Initial Screening**:
  * **Fingerstick Glucose**: Rule out hypoglycemia.
  * **[[Urinalysis]] & Culture**: (UTI is a very common trigger in elderly).
  * **Serum Electrolytes & Calcium**: Screen for hyponatremia and hypercalcemia.
  * **Renal & Liver Function**: BUN/Creatinine (uremia), Ammonia (hepatic encephalopathy).
  * **CBC**: Check for leukocytosis.
* **Cardiac Panel**: **ECG** and Troponin to rule out silent myocardial infarction.

## Neurophysiology & Imaging
* **[[EEG]] (Electroencephalography)**:
  * **Highly diagnostic**: Classically shows **diffuse, generalized background slowing** (predominance of theta and delta waves).
  * *Utility*: Differentiates delirium from primary psychiatric disorders (normal EEG) and detects non-convulsive status epilepticus (showing epileptiform discharges).
* **[[CT Brain]] (without contrast)**:
  * Indicated for: head trauma/falls (rule out subdural hematoma), new focal neurological deficits, or unexplained fever/meningismus prior to LP.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria (The CAM Tool)
According to the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM), diagnosis requires:
1. **Acute onset and fluctuating course** (Evidence of acute change from baseline, tending to fluctuate).
2. **Inattention** (Difficulty focusing, keeping track of what is said).
3. **AND Either**:
   * **Disorganized thinking** (Incoherent, rambling, or illogical conversation).
   * **Altered level of consciousness** (Lethargic, hyperalert, stuporous, or comatose).

# Management

## Initial Management (Non-Pharmacological - First-Line)
Non-pharmacological multicomponent protocols (e.g., the Hospital Elder Life Program [HELP]) are the most effective treatments:
* **Orientation Protocols**: Frequent reorientation (clocks, calendars, window beds, staff introduction).
* **Cognitive Stimulation**: Word games, visits from family.
* **Sleep-Wake Cycle Preservation**: Maximize light exposure during the day, minimize noise and light at night, avoid night-time vitals/meds if stable.
* **Avoid Immobilization & Restraints**: Remove physical restraints immediately (restraints prolong delirium and increase injury risk). Mobilize patients early.
* **Sensory Support**: Ensure glasses and working hearing aids are worn.
* **Physiological Optimization**: Correct dehydration, treat pain aggressively (with non-sedating medications), resolve urinary retention or fecal impaction.

## Definitive Management (Pharmacological)
Pharmacotherapy is reserved **exclusively** for patients whose agitation poses an immediate threat to their own safety (e.g., pulling out endotracheal tubes, central lines) or the safety of others, or causes severe distress:
1. **First-line (High-Potency Antipsychotic)**:
   * **Haloperidol**: $0.5 - 1.0$ mg PO/IM/IV. May repeat every $30 - 60$ minutes until settled (maximum $5$ mg/day in elderly).
   * *Monitoring*: Baseline and serial ECGs. **Avoid if $QTc > 500$ ms** (risk of Torsades de Pointes).
2. **Second-line (Atypical Antipsychotics)**:
   * **Quetiapine** ($12.5 - 25$ mg PO daily/bid) - preferred in Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia (haloperidol causes severe, fatal extrapyramidal reactions).
   * **Olanzapine** ($2.5 - 5$ mg PO/IM).
3. **Avoid Benzodiazepines**:
   * > [!CAUTION]
     > **Benzodiazepine Danger**: Benzodiazepines (e.g., Lorazepam) classically cause **paradoxical excitation** in elderly patients, worsen confusion, increase fall risk, and prolong delirium. Avoid them.
   * **Exceptions**: Benzodiazepines are first-line *only* in **Alcohol/Benzodiazepine Withdrawal Delirium (Delirium Tremens)** or if delirium is secondary to seizures.

# Complications
* **Falls and Fractures**: Due to agitation, confusion, and psychomotor changes.
* **Loss of Lines/Intubation**: Accidental removal of critical life support.
* **Persistent Cognitive Decline**: Accelerates transition from mild cognitive impairment to dementia.
* **Aspiration Pneumonia**: Due to dysphagia in hypoactive/somnolent states.

# Prognosis
* Reversible in the majority of patients once the underlying trigger is corrected, but recovery can be slow (weeks to months).
* Hypoactive delirium has a poorer prognosis than hyperactive due to delayed recognition.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Haloperidol Parkinsonism Rule**: Never administer Haloperidol to a patient with known **Parkinson's Disease** or **Dementia with Lewy Bodies**. It can trigger a catastrophic, irreversible worsening of extrapyramidal symptoms or neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Use **Quetiapine** instead.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Delirium Tremens Exception**: While benzodiazepines are avoided in general delirium, they are the **absolute cornerstone of therapy in Delirium Tremens** (alcohol withdrawal). Titrate IV Diazepam or Lorazepam aggressively to control symptoms.
