## type: disease

# Definition
Meningitis is the acute or subacute inflammation of the meninges (pia, arachnoid, and dura mater) surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It is clinically characterized by the classic triad of fever, nuchal rigidity, and altered mental status.

# Epidemiology
* **Bacterial Meningitis**: Incidence has declined significantly due to conjugate vaccines (Hib, Pneumococcal, Meningococcal). However, it remains a severe disease with high mortality ($10-25\%$) and permanent neurological sequelae.
* **Aseptic (Viral) Meningitis**: More common than bacterial, typically self-limiting, with a peak incidence in late summer and autumn.
* **Fungal & Tuberculous Meningitis**: Primarily seen in immunocompromised individuals (e.g., HIV/AIDS, patients on immunosuppressive drugs).

# Etiology
* **Acute Bacterial Meningitis**:
  * *Neonates ($<1$ month)*: **Group B Streptococcus** (*S. agalactiae*), *Escherichia coli*, *Listeria monocytogenes*.
  * *Infants & Children*: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (unvaccinated).
  * *Adults ($18-50$ years)*: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* ($~60\%$), *Neisseria meningitidis*.
  * *Elderly ($>50$ years) & Immunocompromised*: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Listeria monocytogenes* (warrants addition of Ampicillin), aerobic Gram-negative bacilli.
  * *Post-neurosurgery / CSF leak*: *Staphylococcus aureus*, coagulase-negative Staphylococci, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
* **Aseptic / Viral Meningitis**:
  * **Enteroviruses** (Coxsackievirus, Echovirus) - accounts for $>60\%$ of cases.
  * Herpes Simplex Virus type 2 (HSV-2), HIV (during primary seroconversion), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV).
* **Chronic / Subacute Meningitis**:
  * *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.
  * *Cryptococcus neoformans* (opportunistic in HIV, CD4 $< 100$ cells/$\mu$L).

# Pathophysiology
1. **Colonization**: Pathogens colonize the nasopharynx (bacterial) and cross the mucosal barrier into the bloodstream.
2. **Survival & BBB Crossing**: Bacteria survive intravascular host defenses due to polysaccharide capsules and cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) via the choroid plexus or cerebral capillaries.
3. **Meningeal Inflammation**: Rapid bacterial replication in the subarachnoid space triggers release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-$\alpha$). This recruits polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs).
4. **Cerebral Edema & Vasculitis**: PMN accumulation creates purulent exudate in the subarachnoid space, leading to vasogenic, cytotoxic, and interstitial cerebral edema. This elevates intracranial pressure ([[Acute Increased Intracranial Pressure]] / ICP). Inflammation of local cerebral blood vessels (vasculitis) causes arterial thrombosis, focal ischemia, and infarction.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Classic Triad** (present in only $40-50\%$ of cases, but at least 2 signs are present in $95\%$):
  1. **[[Fever]]**
  2. **Nuchal Rigidity** (neck stiffness)
  3. **[[Altered Consciousness]]** (ranging from confusion to [[Coma]])
* **Associated Symptoms**: Severe, generalized, throbbing **[[Headache]]**, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea and projectile vomiting.
* **Focal Neurologic Signs**: Cranial nerve palsies (specifically CN III, IV, VI, VII), hemiparesis, or aphasia ($10-20\%$ of bacterial cases).
* **[[Seizure]]**: Occurs in $20-30\%$ of patients due to focal cortical ischemia or edema.

## Physical Examination
* **Nuchal Rigidity**: Resistance to passive flexion of the neck.
* **Kernig's Sign**: With the patient supine, the hip and knee are flexed to 90 degrees. Passive extension of the knee eliciting severe pain and resistance in the hamstrings is a positive sign.
* **Brudzinski's Sign**: Passive flexion of the neck results in involuntary flexion of the patient's hips and knees.
* **Jolt Accentuation Maneuver**: Exacerbation of the patient's headache with horizontal rotation of the head at a frequency of 2-3 turns per second (high sensitivity for meningeal irritation).
* **Meningococcemia Rash**: Petechial or purpuric rash, primarily on the extremities, highly suggestive of **[[Meningococcemia]]** (*Neisseria meningitidis*).

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Encephalitis]]**: Main differentiator is **parenchymal dysfunction** (altered mental status, severe confusion, behavior changes, or focal seizures occur early in encephalitis, whereas in meningitis, the sensorium is relatively preserved early on, showing signs of meningeal irritation).
* **[[Brain Abscess]]**: Focal neurologic deficits, progressive headache, ring-enhancing mass on neuroimaging.
* **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage**: Sudden-onset "thunderclap" headache, neck stiffness, but no fever.
* **Systemic [[Sepsis]] / Delirium**: Cognitive changes in elderly secondary to UTI or pneumonia without true meningeal irritation.

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Blood Cultures**: **Two sets are mandatory.** Positive in $50-60\%$ of bacterial meningitis cases.
* **CBC**: Marked leukocytosis with a left shift.
* **Coagulation Panel**: Check PT, INR, PTT to screen for early [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation]] (DIC).
* **Inflammatory Markers**: Elevated CRP and Procalcitonin.

## Imaging
* **[[CT Brain]] (without contrast)**:
  >[!IMPORTANT]
   **CT before Lumbar Puncture Criteria**: LP should be performed immediately without CT *unless* the patient has any of the following high-risk features (which indicate risk of brain herniation during LP):
    1. Age $> 60$.
    2. Immunocompromised state (HIV, chemotherapy, immunosuppressants).
    3. History of CNS disease (tumor, stroke, focal infection).
    4. New-onset seizure within 1 week.
    5. Papilledema on fundoscopy.
    6. Focal neurological deficit (pupillary asymmetry, hemiparesis, facial palsy).
    7. Moderate to severe altered consciousness ($GCS < 11$).

## Special Tests
* **[[Lumbar Puncture]] (LP) & [[CSF Analysis]]**: The diagnostic gold standard.

# Diagnostic Criteria
Definitive diagnosis requires CSF analysis demonstrating matching chemistry and identification of the causative pathogen via Gram stain, bacterial culture, acid-fast stain, fungal culture, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Empiric Antibiotics Rule**: Do not delay administering empiric antibiotics for a CT scan or a difficult LP. If LP is delayed, draw blood cultures and **give antibiotics immediately**.
* **Empiric Resuscitative Therapy (Adults $< 50$ years)**:
  * **Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h** (double the standard dose to achieve high blood-brain barrier penetration).
  * **Vancomycin 15 - 20 mg/kg IV q8-12h** (targets penicillin-resistant *S. pneumoniae*).
* **Atypical / Age $> 50$ or Immunocompromised**:
  * Add **Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h** to cover *Listeria monocytogenes*.
* **Dexamethasone Adjunctive Therapy**:
  * Administer **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV** immediately **before or concurrently with the first dose of antibiotics** (do not give if antibiotics have already been administered).
  * *Indication*: Reduces rates of hearing loss, focal neurological deficits, and mortality in adults with *S. pneumoniae* meningitis. Infuse q6h for 4 days; stop if another pathogen is identified.

## Definitive Management
Once CSF cultures or PCR identify the specific pathogen, de-escalate therapy:
* *S. pneumoniae*: Penicillin G (if penicillin-susceptible MIC $<0.1$ $\mu$g/mL) or continue Ceftriaxone.
* *N. meningitidis*: Penicillin G or Ceftriaxone for 7 days. Provide droplet isolation for 24 hours post-initiation of antibiotics. *Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts*: Rifampin 600 mg PO bid for 2 days, or Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO single dose.
* *L. monocytogenes*: Ampicillin or Penicillin G for at least 21 days.
* *Cryptococcus*: Induction with Amphotericin B + Flucytosine for 2 weeks, followed by consolidation with Fluconazole.
* *Tuberculous*: Standard 4-drug anti-TB regimen (HRZE) for 2 months, followed by HR for 7-10 months, combined with adjuvant corticosteroids.

# Complications
* **Neurological**: Permanent sensorineural hearing loss, hydrocephalus (communicating or obstructive), subdural empyema, brain herniation, septic cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.
* **Systemic**: Septic [[Shock]], [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation]] (DIC), adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome in Meningococcemia).

# Prognosis
* *Bacterial*: High mortality ($10-15\%$ in pneumococcal; up to $40\%$ in elderly). $20-30\%$ of survivors have long-term deficits (hearing loss, cognitive decline, epilepsy).
* *Viral*: Favorable; complete recovery in 7-10 days in the majority of patients.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Dexamethasone Timing**: If dexamethasone is given after the first dose of antibiotics, its clinical benefit is lost. The antibiotic-induced bacterial lysis releases cell wall components that trigger a massive inflammatory cascade; steroids must be present first to suppress this response.
* **Listeria Ampicillin Rule**: Listeria is naturally resistant to all cephalosporins. Always add Ampicillin for patients $>50$ years, pregnant women, or transplant recipients presenting with meningitis.
