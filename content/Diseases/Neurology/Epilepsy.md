## type: disease

# Definition
Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures, and by the neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition. An epileptic seizure is a transient occurrence of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.

# Epidemiology
* Affects approximately $1\%$ of the population worldwide.
* Bimodal incidence: peaks in early childhood (genetic, congenital causes) and in the elderly (secondary to stroke, neurodegenerative disease, or brain tumors).

# Etiology
Classified by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) into six categories:
* **Structural**: Stroke, traumatic brain injury, hippocampal sclerosis (mesial temporal sclerosis), focal cortical dysplasia, or brain tumors.
* **Genetic**: Channelopathies (e.g., SCN1A mutation in Dravet syndrome).
* **Infectious**: Neurocysticercosis (most common cause in developing nations), post-meningitis/encephalitis scarring, cerebral malaria, or HIV.
* **Metabolic**: Mitochondrial disorders, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy.
* **Immune**: Autoimmune encephalitis (e.g., anti-LGI1).
* **Unknown**.

# Pathophysiology
Epileptogenesis results from an **imbalance between excitation and inhibition** in the cerebral cortex:
* **Excitatory Drive**: Mediated by **Glutamate** acting on NMDA/AMPA receptors, prompting inward sodium ($Na^+$) and calcium ($Ca^{2+}$) currents.
* **Inhibitory Drive**: Mediated by **GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid) acting on $GABA_A$ receptors, prompting inward chloride ($Cl^-$) and outward potassium ($K^+$) currents.
* **Depolarization Shift**: Dysfunction in ion channels (channelopathies), glial cells (impaired glutamate clearance), or inhibitory interneurons leads to a **paroxysmal depolarizing shift (PDS)** in a group of neurons, propagating to neighboring areas and causing synchronized, high-frequency electrical discharges.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Aura**: A focal seizure without impairment of consciousness, serving as a warning sign.
  * *Temporal lobe*: Epigastric rising sensation, déjà vu, jamais vu, olfactory (foul smell) or gustatory hallucinations.
  * *Occipital lobe*: Flashing lights, visual hallucinations.
* **Ictal Symptoms**:
  * *Focal Aware*: Isolated motor (jerking of one limb) or sensory (paresthesias) symptoms; patient remains fully conscious.
  * *Focal Impaired Awareness*: Lip-smacking, swallowing, hand wringing (automatisms), staring blankly, unable to respond.
  * *Generalized Tonic-Clonic (GTC)*: Abrupt loss of consciousness, tonic extension of limbs (contraction), followed by synchronous clonic jerking (convulsion).
* **Postictal Symptoms**: Severe confusion, fatigue, headache, muscle soreness, and amnesia of the event.

## Physical Examination
* **Interictal Examination**: Usually completely normal, unless there is an underlying focal structural lesion.
* **Ictal / Postictal Signs**:
  * **Tongue Biting**: Typically involves the **lateral border of the tongue** (high specificity for GTC seizures).
  * **Urinary or Fecal Incontinence**: Due to sphincter relaxation post-seizure.
  * **Todd's Paralysis**: Transient, focal neurological deficit (most commonly hemiparesis or aphasia) occurring in the postictal period, lasting from minutes to 36 hours. (Reflects localized postictal exhaust/inhibition of the affected motor cortex).

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Syncope]]**: Transient loss of consciousness, rapid recovery, **no postictal phase** (patient is immediate oriented), brief myoclonic jerks ($<15$ seconds) may occur (convulsive syncope), but tongue biting and prolonged confusion are absent.
* **Psychogenic Non-Epileptic Seizures (PNES)**: Characterized by out-of-phase limb thrashing, side-to-side head shaking, pelvic thrusting, and eyes closed tightly (resisting opening). Postictal lactate is normal.
* **Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)**: Presents with "negative" symptoms (loss of motor/sensory function), whereas focal seizures present with "positive" symptoms (jerking, paresthesias).

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **Routine Labs**: Glucose (exclude hypoglycemia), electrolytes (exclude hyponatremia), renal/hepatic panel.
* **Prolactin**: Elevated 3- to 4-fold within 10-20 minutes of a GTC or complex focal seizure (helps differentiate from PNES).
* **Antiepileptic Drug (AED) Levels**: Monitor compliance or toxicity.

## Imaging
* **[[Brain MRI]] (Epilepsy Protocol)**: Mandatory in all new-onset adult cases to look for structural etiologies. High-resolution coronal FLAIR cuts are needed to identify **mesial temporal sclerosis** (hippocampal atrophy and T2/FLAIR hyperintensity).

## Special Tests
* **[[EEG]] (Electroencephalogram)**:
  * The primary diagnostic test to confirm epileptogenic potential and classify seizure type.
  * Look for **interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs)**: Spikes, sharp waves, spike-wave complexes.
  * *Diagnostic Pearl*: A single routine EEG has a sensitivity of only $30-50\%$. A normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy. Obtain sleep-deprived EEG or continuous video-EEG monitoring to increase yield.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria (ILAE Definition)
Epilepsy is diagnosed if any of the following are met:
1. At least **two unprovoked** (or reflex) seizures occurring **$> 24$ hours apart**.
2. **One unprovoked** (or reflex) seizure and a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk (at least $60\%$) after two unprovoked seizures, occurring over the next 10 years (e.g., one seizure + abnormal MRI/EEG showing epileptiform activity).
3. Diagnosis of an **epilepsy syndrome** (e.g., Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy).

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **First Seizure Decision**: Do not routinely start AEDs after a single, uncomplicated unprovoked seizure unless the patient has risk factors for high recurrence ($>60\%$): abnormal EEG with epileptiform discharges, abnormal brain MRI, or focal neurological deficits.
* **Lifestyle Modification**: Avoid sleep deprivation (most common seizure trigger), limit alcohol, avoid high-risk activities (swimming alone, driving for a specified legal period, working at heights).

## Definitive Management (AED Therapy)
Initiate monotherapy and titrate to therapeutic effect or tolerability.

### 📊 Antiepileptic Drug (AED) Selection Matrix

| Drug Category | Key Medications | Indications | Key Side Effects & Contraindications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Broad-Spectrum** *(Covers focal & generalized)* | **Levetiracetam (Keppra)** | Focal, Generalized, Myoclonic, Status. | **Behavioral changes (Keppra rage)**, depression, somnolence. Safe in liver disease. |
| | **Sodium Valproate (Depakote)** | **First-line for generalized**, Myoclonic, Absence. | Weight gain, alopecia, tremor, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis. **Strictly contraindicated in pregnancy.** |
| | **Lamotrigine** | Focal, Generalized, Absence. | **Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS)** (must titrate slowly!). Safe in pregnancy. |
| **Narrow-Spectrum** *(Focal only; may worsen myoclonic/absence!)* | **Carbamazepine** | **First-line for Focal**, Trigeminal neuralgia. | Hyponatremia (SIADH), leukopenia, SJS (associated with HLA-B*1502 in Asian populations). |
| | **Phenytoin** | Focal, Status epilepticus. | Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, nystagmus, osteomalacia. Drug interactions (inducer). |
| **Absence-Specific** | **Ethosuximide** | **Typical Absence Seizures** only. | Nausea, vomiting, headache, sleep disturbance. |

### 🤰 Epilepsy and Pregnancy:
* **Valproate is strictly contraindicated** due to high risk of major congenital malformations (neural tube defects) and cognitive delay.
* **Lamotrigine** or **Levetiracetam** is preferred.
* **High-Dose Folate (5 mg PO daily)** must be initiated prior to conception to minimize neural tube defects.

---

# Complications
* **Status Epilepticus**: Medical emergency (continuous seizure $>5$ minutes).
* **Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP)**: Most common cause of death in refractory epilepsy. Risk factors: uncontrolled GTC seizures, sleeping prone.
* **Physical Trauma**: Fractures, posterior shoulder dislocation (classic in GTC), aspiration pneumonia, head trauma.

# Prognosis
* $70\%$ of patients achieve complete seizure control with their first or second AED.
* $30\%$ have drug-resistant (refractory) epilepsy (defined as failure of two appropriately chosen and tolerated AED regimens). Warrants referral for epilepsy surgery evaluation.

# Clinical Pearls
* **HLA-B*1502 screening**: In Asian populations, screen for the **HLA-B\*1502** allele before initiating Carbamazepine. Carriage of this gene increases the risk of life-threatening Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) by over 100-fold.
* **Keppra Rage**: Levetiracetam (Keppra) is highly popular due to its lack of drug-drug interactions and rapid onset, but always screen for baseline psychiatric disorders. It can cause severe irritability, anger, and mood swings.
