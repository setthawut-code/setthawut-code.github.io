## type: disease

# Definition
Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive, symmetric, ascending flaccid paralysis and areflexia. It represents a neurological emergency due to the risk of respiratory failure and autonomic instability.

# Epidemiology
* GBS is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis worldwide since the eradication of wild poliovirus.
* Incidence is $1 - 2$ cases per 100,000 annually, slightly higher in males and increasing with age.

# Etiology
* **Post-Infectious Autoimmune Mimicry**: Approximately $60-70\%$ of patients report a preceding respiratory or gastrointestinal illness 1 to 4 weeks prior to neurological onset.
* **Key Associated Pathogens**:
  * **Campylobacter jejuni**: The most common precipitating infection ($30\%$). Strongly associated with severe, axonal forms of GBS.
  * Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Influenza A, Hepatitis E, and Zika virus.

# Pathophysiology
1. **Molecular Mimicry**: Preceding pathogens (especially *Campylobacter jejuni*) carry lipooligosaccharides (LOS) in their outer membrane that share structural similarity with host nerve **gangliosides** (e.g., GM1, GD1a, GD1b, GT1a) in peripheral nerves.
2. **Humoral Response**: The host generates IgG antibodies against the pathogen that cross-react with gangliosides at the nodes of Ranvier and myelin sheaths.
3. **Myelin Stripping & Axonal Loss**:
  * *Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP)*: Complement activation recruits macrophages, which physically strip myelin, leading to conduction block.
  * *Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy (AMAN)*: Antibodies directly target axolemma, leading to macrophages targeting nodes of Ranvier and causing primary axonal degeneration (worse prognosis).

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Symmetric Ascending Weakness**: Begins in the lower extremities (difficulty walking, climbing stairs) and ascends over days to weeks to involve the upper extremities, trunk, facial muscles, and respiratory diaphragm.
* **Sensory Disturbances**: Mild, distal paresthesias (tingling, numbness) in a "stocking-glove" distribution often precede weakness.
* **Neuropathic Pain**: Severe back pain, radicular pain, or muscle aches are present in up to $80\%$ of patients.
* **Autonomic Dysfunction** (Present in $70\%$ of cases): Fluctuating blood pressure (orthostatic hypotension or paroxysmal hypertension), sinus tachycardia, bradyarrhythmias, urinary retention, or paralytic ileus.

## Physical Examination
* **Hyporeflexia or Areflexia**: The hallmark physical finding. Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) are diminished or completely absent early in the course.
* **Symmetric Motor Deficits**: Flaccid, symmetric weakness.
* **Cranial Nerve Palsies**: Bilateral facial nerve palsy (**facial diplegia**) occurs in $50\%$ of patients. Bulbar weakness (CN IX, X) causes dysarthria and dysphagia.
* **Respiratory Compromise**: Monitored via accessory muscle use, shallow breathing, and weak cough.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Myasthenia Gravis]]**: Fatigable weakness, ocular-bulbar predominance, **reflexes are normal**, no sensory involvement, no autonomic instability.
* **[[Poliomyelitis]]**: **Asymmetric** flaccid paralysis, meningeal signs, fever at onset, normal sensory exam, unvaccinated history.
* **Transverse [[Myelitis]]**: Acute spinal cord injury showing a **sharp sensory level**, urinary incontinence (hyperreflexic bladder later), hyperreflexia, and positive Babinski sign (UMN features) after the initial spinal shock phase.
* **Tick Paralysis**: Ascending paralysis; reflexes absent. Differentiated by finding the tick in the hair; immediate recovery on removal.

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **[[CSF Analysis]] (via [[Lumbar Puncture]])**:
  * **Albuminocytologic Dissociation**: The classic CSF finding. Characterized by **elevated protein ($>45 - 200$ mg/dL) with a normal WBC count ($< 5$ cells/$\mu$L)**.
  * *Timing Pearl*: CSF protein may be completely normal in the first week of symptoms ($30-50\%$ of cases). If GBS is strongly suspected but CSF is normal, repeat the LP in 1 week.

## Imaging
* **Spinal MRI (with contrast)**: Shows smooth, continuous enhancement of the cauda equina and anterior nerve roots. Primarily used to rule out mechanical spinal cord compression.

## Special Tests
* **Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS) / Electromyography (EMG)**:
  * Shows demyelinating features: Prolonged or absent F-waves (earliest sign, reflecting proximal nerve root demyelination), reduced conduction velocity, prolonged distal latencies, and temporal dispersion/conduction block.
* **Pulmonary Function Tests**:
  * Bedside monitoring of **Forced Vital Capacity (FVC)** and **Maximal Inspiratory Pressure (MIP)**.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria (Brighton Criteria)
Requires bilateral flaccid weakness + decreased/absent reflexes + monophasic course (progression over 12h to 28 days) + CSF albuminocytologic dissociation + electrodiagnostic features of demyelination.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Respiratory Care (The 20/30/40 Rule)**:
  * Admit the patient to the ICU and prepare for early endotracheal intubation if:
    * **FVC $< 20$ mL/kg**
    * **Maximal Inspiratory Pressure (MIP) $< -30$ cm H2O** (or worsening rapidly)
    * **Maximal Expiratory Pressure (MEP) $< 40$ cm H2O**
  * Do not wait for hypoxemia or hypercapnia to occur; respiratory muscle fatigue is sudden.
* **Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis**:
  * Give subcutaneous LMWH (Enoxaparin) or Unfractionated Heparin + sequential compression devices (due to prolonged immobility and high DVT/PE risk).

## Definitive Management
* **Immunomodulatory Therapy (Initiate immediately if non-ambulatory or rapidly progressive)**:
  * **Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)**: $0.4$ g/kg/day IV for 5 days ($2$ g/kg total). Preferred due to ease of administration. OR
  * **Plasmapheresis (Plasma Exchange)**: 5 exchanges over 1 to 2 weeks. Removes circulating autoantibodies and complement factors.
  * *Note*: IVIG and Plasmapheresis are equally effective. **Do not combine them** (does not improve efficacy, increases adverse events).

  > [!WARNING]
  Corticosteroids are strictly ineffective in GBS and should not be administered, as they do not speed recovery and may delay nerve regeneration.

---

# Complications
* **Respiratory Failure**: Requires prolonged mechanical ventilation ($20-30\%$ of cases).
* **Autonomic Storm**: Fatal arrhythmias, sudden asystole, or severe orthostatic collapse.
* **Immobility-Related**: DVT, pulmonary embolism, pressure ulcers, contractures.

# Prognosis
* $80 - 85\%$ of patients make a full or near-full recovery.
* Mortality is $3 - 5\%$, primarily due to autonomic instability, cardiac arrest, or ventilator-associated pneumonia.
* $10\%$ are left with severe permanent disability (wheelchair dependency or severe drop foot).

# Clinical Pearls
* **Reflexes are Key**: If a patient presents with symmetric, ascending weakness but has hyperactive reflexes or a positive Babinski sign, GBS is ruled out. Investigate for a central spinal cord lesion.
* **Campylobacter and Axonal GBS**: *C. jejuni* infection predisposes to the AMAN variant, which features primary axonal loss, slower recovery, and higher rates of permanent motor disability.
