## type: disease

# Definition
Intracranial Hemorrhage (ICH) refers to bleeding within the cranial vault. It is classified by its anatomical location into four major types: Epidural Hematoma (EDH), Subdural Hematoma (SDH), Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH), and Intracerebral (Intraparenchymal) Hemorrhage (ICH).

# Epidemiology
* **Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)**: Accounts for $10-15\%$ of all strokes, but carries a significantly higher morbidity and 30-day mortality ($40\%$) than ischemic stroke.
* **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)**: Accounts for $5\%$ of strokes; typically occurs in younger patients (peak age $40-60$ years).
* **EDH & SDH**: Predominantly traumatic. SDH is common in the elderly and chronic alcoholics.

# Etiology & Pathophysiology
The vascular pathology and anatomical location differ for each hemorrhage type:

```
Intracranial Hemorrhage Anatomy
└── 1. Epidural Hematoma (EDH)
    ├── Middle meningeal artery rupture (temporal bone fracture)
    └── Location: Between skull inner table and dura mater
└── 2. Subdural Hematoma (SDH)
    ├── Bridging veins rupture (shearing trauma, brain atrophy)
    └── Location: Between dura mater and arachnoid mater
└── 3. Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)
    ├── Saccular (berry) aneurysm rupture (Circle of Willis)
    └── Location: Between arachnoid and pia mater (CSF space)
└── 4. Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)
    ├── Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms (hypertension) or Amyloid Angiopathy (lobar)
    └── Location: Within the brain parenchyma
```

* **Epidural Hematoma (EDH)**: Skull fracture lacerates the **middle meningeal artery**. High-pressure arterial bleeding strips the dura from the skull, forming a localized hematoma.
* **Subdural Hematoma (SDH)**: Acceleration-deceleration forces tear the fragile **bridging veins** as they span from the cortex to the dural sinuses. Slow venous bleeding accumulates. Brain atrophy (elderly, alcoholics) stretches these veins, predisposing to chronic SDH from minor trauma.
* **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)**: Rupture of a **saccular (berry) aneurysm** (85% of non-traumatic SAH) located at Circle of Willis branch points (most commonly Anterior Communicating Artery, followed by Posterior Communicating and Middle Cerebral Arteries).
* **Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)**:
  * *Deep (Hypertensive)*: Chronic systemic hypertension damages deep penetrating arterioles (Lenticulostriate arteries), producing **Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms** that rupture in the basal ganglia ($50\%$), thalamus ($15\%$), pons ($10\%$), or cerebellum ($10\%$).
  * *Lobar (Amyloid)*: **Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA)** in the elderly. Deposition of amyloid-$\beta$ peptide in the walls of cortical capillaries and arterioles causes recurrent lobar hemorrhages.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **EDH**: Head trauma followed by a classic **"lucid interval"** (temporary recovery of consciousness lasting minutes to hours), followed by rapid neurological deterioration (somnolence, hemiparesis, blown pupil) due to expanding hematoma.
* **SDH**:
  * *Acute*: Coma or immediate severe headache and focal deficits post-trauma.
  * *Chronic*: Weeks to months after minor trauma. Progressive headache, cognitive decline (apathy, confusion - mimics dementia), gait instability, and fluctuating consciousness.
* **SAH**: Abrupt onset of an excruciating, **"worst headache of my life" (Thunderclap Headache)**, reaching peak intensity within seconds. Accompanied by photophobia, neck stiffness, vomiting, and transient syncope.
* **ICH**: Sudden onset of focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, aphasia) that progress over minutes to hours, accompanied by headache, vomiting, elevated BP, and early altered consciousness.

## Physical Examination
* **Meningismus**: Nuchal rigidity, Kernig's, and Brudzinski's signs (prominent in SAH and intraventricular extension of ICH).
* **Herniation Signs**:
  * **Ipsilateral Dilated, Fixed Pupil** ("blown pupil" - CN III compression, classic in EDH or temporal lobar hemorrhages).
  * **Cushing's Triad** (Hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing - indicates critically high ICP).
* **Focal deficits**: Contralateral hemiparesis, sensory loss, or gaze deviation (pointing toward the side of the lesion).

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Acute Ischemic Stroke**: Presents with identical sudden focal deficits. However, ischemic stroke features normal or mildly elevated BP, slower loss of consciousness, and absence of headache/vomiting in the early stages.
* **[[Meningitis]]**: Fever, headache, neck stiffness. CSF shows high WBCs; head CT shows no hemorrhage.
* **Hypertensive Encephalopathy**: Severe hypertension and headache, but *no focal mass lesion or hematoma* on CT scan.

# Investigations

## Imaging
* **[[CT Brain]] (without contrast)**: The primary diagnostic standard.

### 📊 Head CT Hemorrhage Identification Table

| Hemorrhage Type | Classical CT Appearance | Anatomical Boundary | Suture Line Crossing |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Epidural (EDH)** | **Biconvex (lenticular)**, hyperdense, sharp margins. | Does **not** cross suture lines (dura is firmly attached at sutures). | No |
| **Subdural (SDH)** | **Crescent-shaped**, hyperdense (acute) to hypodense (chronic). | **Can** cross suture lines (runs beneath the dura). | **Yes** (stops at dural reflections) |
| **Subarachnoid (SAH)**| **Hyperdensity in basal cisterns**, sylvian fissures, and sulci ("star" pattern). | Diffuse within the subarachnoid space. | N/A |
| **Intracerebral (ICH)**| **Hyperdense intraparenchymal mass**, often surrounded by hypodense vasogenic edema. | Confined within brain parenchyma. | N/A |

* **CT Angiography (CTA) of the Head**: Indicated immediately in non-traumatic SAH to identify the bleeding aneurysm.

## Special Tests
* **[[Lumbar Puncture]] (LP)**:
  * **Indication**: Mandatory if SAH is strongly suspected clinically but the Head CT is normal (CT can be false-negative in $<5\%$ of cases, especially if $>24$h post-onset).
  * **CSF Findings**: **Xanthochromia** (yellow/pink CSF supernatant due to hemoglobin breakdown to bilirubin; requires centrifugation, peaks 12h post-bleed) and **persistent RBC counts** from tube 1 to tube 4 (does not clear, unlike a traumatic tap).

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by clinical history matching the vascular deficit, combined with neuroimaging demonstrating blood in the epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, or intraparenchymal spaces.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Airway Protection**: Secure airway (intubation) if GCS $\le 8$ or rapidly deteriorating.
* **Blood Pressure Optimization**:
  * **Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)**:
    * For patients presenting with systolic BP $150-220$ mmHg, acutely lower systolic BP to **$140$ mmHg** using IV Nicardipine or Labetalol. (Limits hematoma expansion without inducing cerebral ischemia).
  * **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)**:
    * Maintain systolic BP **$< 140$ mmHg** prior to aneurysm securing to prevent rebleeding.
* **Reversal of Anticoagulation**:
  * *Warfarin*: Administer **Four-Factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC)** + IV Vitamin K 10 mg.
  * *Dabigatran*: Give Idarucizumab.
  * *Apixaban/Rivaroxaban*: Give Andexanet Alfa or PCC.
  * *Heparin*: Give Protamine Sulfate.
* **ICP Control**: Elevate HOB to 30 degrees, administer IV Mannitol or 3% Hypertonic Saline if herniation is suspected.

## Definitive Management
* **Aortic / Aneurysmal Securing (SAH)**:
  * Perform **endovascular coiling** or **surgical clipping** of the ruptured aneurysm within 24 hours of presentation to prevent catastrophic rebleeding.
* **Surgical Evacuation (EDH/SDH)**:
  * Emergent craniotomy and hematoma evacuation if:
    * *EDH*: Volume $>30$ mL, or midline shift $>5$ mm.
    * *Acute SDH*: Thickness $>10$ mm, or midline shift $>5$ mm.
    * *Cerebellar Hemorrhage*: Hematoma $>3$ cm in diameter, or causing brainstem compression/hydrocephalus.
* **Vasospasm Prevention (SAH)**:
  * Administer **Nimodipine 60 mg PO every 4 hours for 21 days** to all patients with aneurysmal SAH (reduces delayed cerebral ischemia/vasospasm).

---

# Complications
* **Rebleeding**: Highest risk in the first 24 hours in SAH ($15\%$).
* **Delayed Cerebral Ischemia (Vasospasm)**: Occurs **3 to 14 days** post-SAH. Managed with Nimodipine and maintaining euvolemia.
* **Acute Hydrocephalus**: Obstruction of CSF flow by blood clots; requires external ventricular drain (EVD) placement.
* **Hyponatremia**: Due to Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) or Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW) (common in SAH).

# Prognosis
* *EDH*: Excellent ($>90\%$ recovery) if surgically evacuated before pupil dilation or coma.
* *ICH & SAH*: Poor; $30-40\%$ mortality at 30 days. Half of survivors are left with permanent neurological deficits.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Xanthochromia Timing**: Centrifuged CSF for xanthochromia should be checked only if the tap is performed **$> 12$ hours** after the onset of the headache, as it takes time for RBC lysis and enzymatic conversion of heme to bilirubin to occur.
* **Avoid Steroids in ICH/Stroke**: Unlike brain tumors, corticosteroids (Dexamethasone) are ineffective for the edema surrounding an intracranial hemorrhage and are associated with increased infective complications.
