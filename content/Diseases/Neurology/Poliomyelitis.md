## type: disease

# Definition
Poliomyelitis (Polio) is a highly infectious viral disease that primarily targets and destroys the motor neurons in the anterior horn of the spinal cord and brainstem, leading to acute flaccid paralysis (AFP), muscle wasting, and potentially fatal respiratory failure.

# Epidemiology
* Near-total global eradication has been achieved through systematic immunization using the **Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV / Salk)** and **Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV / Sabin)**.
* Wild Poliovirus (WPV) transmission remains endemic in only a few restricted areas globally. However, Vaccine-Derived Poliovirus (VDPV) outbreaks occasionally occur in areas with low vaccine coverage.

# Etiology
* **Poliovirus**: An enterovirus belonging to the *Picornaviridae* family. There are three antigenically distinct serotypes (PV1, PV2, PV3). PV1 is the only remaining active wild serotype.
* **Transmission**: **Fecal-oral route** via ingestion of water or food contaminated with feces containing the virus.

# Pathophysiology
1. **Replication**: The virus enters via the mouth, replicates in the pharynx and Peyer's patches of the small intestine.
2. **Viremia**: It spreads to the regional lymph nodes and enters the bloodstream (primary and secondary viremia).
3. **Neurotropism**: The virus crosses the blood-brain barrier and demonstrates specific tropism for **motor neurons in the anterior horn of the spinal cord** and the motor nuclei of the brainstem.
4. **Neuronal Destruction**: Poliovirus replicates inside motor neurons, causing cell lysis and inflammatory microglial infiltration. This leads to denervation of the corresponding motor units, presenting as flaccid muscle weakness and atrophy. Sensory neurons and pathways are entirely spared.

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Asymptomatic / Subclinical Infection** ($90 - 95\%$ of cases): No symptoms, but patients shed the virus in their stool and transmit infection.
* **Abortive Poliomyelitis** ($4 - 8\%$): A mild, self-limiting illness featuring [[Fever]], headache, sore throat, vomiting, and malaise. Resolves within 2-3 days.
* **Non-Paralytic Poliomyelitis** ($1 - 2\%$): Presents as aseptic [[Meningitis]] (fever, headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, back pain) without motor deficits.
* **Paralytic Poliomyelitis** ($< 1\%$): The classic, dreaded presentation. Begins with aseptic meningitis features, followed by the rapid onset of severe muscle pain and flaccid weakness.

## Physical Examination
* **Asymmetric Flaccid Paralysis**: Typically involves the lower extremities more than the upper extremities, and **proximal muscles** (thigh, hip) more than distal muscles (foot). The paralysis is characteristically asymmetric (e.g., one leg severely affected, the other normal).
* **Hyporeflexia or Areflexia**: Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) are diminished or completely absent in the affected limbs.
* **Sparing of Sensation**: **Sensory examination is completely normal.** The patient has full touch, pain, and temperature sensation, differentiating it from spinal cord transection or transverse myelitis.
* **Bulbar Paralysis**: Involves cranial nerves IX, X, XI, and XII. Presents with dysphagia, nasal regurgitation, dysarthria, and respiratory failure due to medullary motor nucleus damage.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Guillain-Barre Syndrome]] (GBS)**: **Symmetric** ascending paralysis, early sensory paresthesias (numbness), and CSF albuminocytologic dissociation.
* **Transverse [[Myelitis]]**: Presents with a **sharp sensory level** and hyperreflexia/Babinski sign (upper motor neuron signs) after the initial spinal shock phase.
* **Acute Flaccid Myelitis (AFM)**: Caused by other non-polio enteroviruses (e.g., EV-D68, EV-A71). Presents identically to paralytic polio; differentiated by CSF PCR or viral stool isolation.

# Investigations

## Laboratory & Surveillance
* **WHO Surveillance Stool Protocol**:
  * > [!IMPORTANT]
  * **The Stool Surveillance Rule**: To confirm or rule out poliomyelitis in any case of acute flaccid paralysis (AFP), **two stool specimens must be collected 24 to 48 hours apart, within 14 days of paralysis onset**. This is the gold standard for WHO polio surveillance, as the virus is shed in high concentrations in the stool.
* **CSF Analysis**:
  * Demonstrates aseptic meningitis profile: Lymphocytic pleocytosis ($10-500$ cells/$\mu$L), normal glucose, and mildly elevated protein.
* **Viral PCR and Culture**:
  * From stool, throat swab, or CSF (though CSF PCR for poliovirus is frequently negative).

## Special Tests
* **Electromyography (EMG) / NCS**:
  * Demonstrates decreased compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes with **normal sensory nerve conduction studies**. EMG shows signs of acute denervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves) in affected muscles.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Confirmed by the clinical presentation of asymmetric acute flaccid paralysis with preserved sensation, combined with isolation of wild or vaccine-derived poliovirus from stool samples.

---

# Management

## Initial & Supportive Management
* **No specific antiviral therapy exists.** Management is entirely supportive.
* **Airway & Respiratory Support**:
  * Close monitoring of vital capacity. If bulbar or diaphragmatic paralysis occurs, mechanical ventilation is required (historically managed using the negative-pressure "Iron Lung").
* **Pain Management**: Warm moist packs (hot packs) to relieve severe muscle spasms and analgesics.
* **Strict Enteric Isolation**: Place the patient on contact isolation. The virus can be shed in stool for up to 6-8 weeks.

## Definitive Management
* **Physical Therapy**: Initiated early after the acute febrile stage to prevent joint contractures and muscle atrophy.
* **Reconstructive Orthotics**: Bracing, splinting, or orthopedic surgery to manage permanent flaccid limb deformities.

---

# Complications
* **Respiratory Failure**: Due to diaphragmatic paralysis or bulbar dysfunction.
* **Joint Deformities & Skeletal Contractures**: Leading to leg-length discrepancy or scoliosis.
* **Post-Polio Syndrome (PPS)**:
  * Characterized by the **onset of new, progressive muscle weakness, muscle pain, and severe fatigue** occurring **$15 - 40$ years** after recovery from paralytic poliomyelitis.
  * *Pathophysiology*: Not a reactivation of the virus. During recovery from acute polio, surviving motor neurons sprout new axon collaterals to reinnervate denervated muscle fibers (giant motor units). Decades later, these overworked axonal sprouts undergo premature metabolic exhaustion and die, leading to new weakness.

# Prognosis
* Abortive and non-paralytic polio have an excellent prognosis.
* Paralytic polio: $5-10\%$ mortality in spinal forms due to respiratory muscle paralysis; up to $20-60\%$ mortality in bulbar forms. Survivors are left with permanent flaccid weakness.

# Clinical Pearls
* **Sensation Check**: A patient presenting with a floppy, flaccid leg and absent reflexes who has **intact sensation** has a lower motor neuron lesion. If there is a sensory boundary or loss of sensation, investigate for a spinal cord lesion (e.g., transverse myelitis or compression).
* **OPV vs. IPV**: The Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV/Sabin) is a live-attenuated vaccine that provides mucosal IgA immunity (preventing wild virus transmission) but carries a rare risk of causing vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP) or mutating into VDPV. The Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV/Salk) prevents systemic disease but does not prevent gut colonization or transmission.
