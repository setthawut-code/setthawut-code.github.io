# 🩸 G6PD Deficiency (ภาวะพร่องเอนไซม์จีซิกพีดี)

> **Definition**: An X-linked recessive hereditary enzymatic disorder characterized by red blood cell susceptibility to oxidative stress, leading to episodic intravascular and extravascular hemolytic anemia. It is the most common disease-producing enzymopathy in the world.

---

## 📊 Triggers of Oxidative Stress

* **Infections** (Most common trigger overall): Viral or bacterial infections (e.g., pneumonia, hepatitis).
* **Fava Beans** (Favism): Ingestion of fava/broad beans.
* **Oxidative Medications**:
  * **Antimalarials**: **Primaquine**, Tafenoquine.
  * **Antibiotics**: **Dapsone**, Sulfamethoxazole (in Co-trimoxazole/TMP-SMX), Nitrofurantoin.
  * **Urate-Lowering**: **Rasburicase**.
  * **Others**: Methylene blue, high-dose Vitamin C.

---

## 🦠 Pathophysiology
1. **HMP Shunt Defect**: Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the rate-limiting enzyme of the Hexose Monophosphate (HMP) shunt, which is the sole source of **NADPH** in mature RBCs.
2. **Glutathione Depletion**: NADPH is required by glutathione reductase to regenerate **reduced glutathione**. Reduced glutathione is the cell's primary defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydrogen peroxide.
3. **Heinz Body Formation**: In the presence of oxidative triggers, ROS accumulate and oxidize sulfhydryl groups on globin chains, causing hemoglobin to denature and precipitate as **Heinz bodies** against the RBC membrane.
4. **Splenic Bite Cells**: As RBCs pass through the spleen, macrophages pluck out the Heinz bodies, leaving **bite cells (degmacytes)** or blister cells. Severely damaged RBCs undergo intravascular lysis or extravascular phagocytosis in the spleen.

```
                  OXIDATIVE STRESS (Primaquine, Fava Beans, Infection)
                                         │
                                         ▼
                   Accumulation of Hydrogen Peroxide (ROS)
                                         │
                 Glutathione remains oxidized (Lack of NADPH)
                                         │
                                         ▼
                    Denatured Globin Chains (HEINZ BODIES)
                                         │
          ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
          ▼                                                             ▼
  Intravascular Lysis                                         Splenic Macrophages
 (Hemoglobinuria/Tea urine)                                 (Pluck Heinz bodies → BITE CELLS)
```

## 🔍 Clinical Manifestations
* **Between Flares**: Patient is completely asymptomatic with normal hemoglobin levels.
* **During Acute Flare** (onset 1-3 days after trigger exposure):
  * Sudden onset of severe back pain, abdominal pain, or joint pain.
  * **Hemoglobinuria**: Dark, **cola- or tea-colored urine**.
  * Scleral icterus, pallor, fatigue.
  * Splenomegaly (transient).
* **Neonatal Jaundice**: Elevated indirect bilirubin in the first week of life, can lead to kernicterus if severe.

---

## 🔬 Investigations

### 1. CBC & Blood Smear
* **Hb/Hct**: Rapidly drops during a flare.
* **Reticulocyte Count**: High (compensatory erythropoiesis, peaks 7-10 days).
* **Peripheral Smear**:
  * **Bite cells** (degmacytes) and blister cells.
  * **Heinz Bodies**: Visible **only with supravital stains** (Brilliant Cresyl Blue or Crystal Violet). *Not visible on standard Wright-Giemsa stained smears.*

### 2. Urine Studies
* **Dipstick**: Positive for blood (detects free hemoglobin).
* **Microscopy**: **No RBCs** or minimal RBCs (confirms hemoglobinuria rather than hematuria).

### 3. G6PD Enzyme Assays
* **Fluorescent Spot Test / Spectrophotometric Assay**:
  * > [!WARNING]
    > **The Acute Flare Diagnostic Trap**: During or immediately after an acute hemolytic flare, the G6PD assay **can return a falsely normal level**. This occurs because the oldest, most G6PD-deficient RBCs have already hemolyzed, and the remaining circulating blood contains a high percentage of young reticulocytes, which have normal G6PD levels. **Always retest the patient 2 to 3 months after the acute episode has completely resolved.**

---

## 🏥 Management

### 1. Acute Phase Care
* **Avoid Triggers**: Discontinue any suspected medication immediately.
* **Hydration**: Maintain aggressive oral or IV hydration to prevent acute kidney injury (AKI) from intratubular precipitation of free hemoglobin.
* **Blood Transfusion**: Indicated only if severe, symptomatic anemia compromises cardiopulmonary status (usually Hb $<7\text{ g/dL}$).

### 2. Chronic Phase
* Provide the patient and family with a written list of drugs, foods, and chemicals to avoid.
* Screen newborn males in high-prevalence areas.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Bite Cells vs. Spherocytes**: Bite cells signify oxidative membrane damage (G6PD), while spherocytes signify antibody-mediated membrane loss ([[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia]]) or structural protein deficiency ([[Hereditary Spherocytosis]]).
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Rasburicase Contraindication**: Before starting Rasburicase for Tumor Lysis Syndrome prophylaxis in leukemia/lymphoma patients, **always screen for G6PD deficiency**. Rasburicase generates hydrogen peroxide during uric acid breakdown, triggering catastrophic intravascular hemolysis in G6PD-deficient patients.
* > [!TIP]
  > **The Urine Clue**: If a patient presents with sudden severe pallor and dark red-brown urine, ask: "Did you recently take antimalarials, sulfa drugs, or eat fava beans?"
