# 🦴 Vasculitis / Vasculitis Syndrome (กลุ่มอาการหลอดเลือดอักเสบ)

> **Definition**: Systemic Vasculitides are a heterogeneous group of immunological disorders characterized by inflammatory destruction of blood vessel walls. This leads to endothelial damage, lumen occlusion (causing tissue ischemia/infarction), or vessel wall weakening (causing aneurysm, stenosis, or hemorrhage). They are classified based on the size of the dominant vessels affected: large, medium, or small.

---

## 📊 Overview of Vessel Size Classification

| Vessel Size | Primary Diseases | Key Pathological Features | Common Clinical Manifestations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Large Vessel** (Aorta & main branches) | • [[Giant Cell Arteritis]] (GCA)<br>• **Takayasu Arteritis** | Granulomatous vasculitis, giant cells | Limb claudication, asymmetric pulses, unequal BP, headache, blindness |
| **Medium Vessel** (Main visceral arteries) | • **Polyarteritis Nodosa** (PAN)<br>• **Kawasaki Disease** | Necrotizing vasculitis, microaneurysms | Abdominal angina, renovascular HTN, mononeuritis multiplex, coronary aneurysms |
| **Small Vessel** (Arterioles, capillaries, venules) | • **ANCA-Associated**: GPA, MPA, EGPA<br>• **Immune Complex**: IgA Vasculitis (HSP), Cryoglobulinemia | Necrotizing vasculitis, pauci-immune or immune-complex deposits | **Palpable purpura**, glomerulonephritis (hematuria, RBC casts), pulmonary hemorrhage |

## 🔍 Specific Vasculitis Syndromes

### 1. Large Vessel Vasculitis (LVV)

#### A. Takayasu Arteritis ("Pulseless Disease")
* **Epidemiology**: Typically affects **young females $<40$ years** old (most commonly of Asian descent).
* **Pathology**: Granulomatous inflammation of the aorta and its primary branches (subclavian, carotid, renal arteries).
* **Clinical Manifestations**:
  * **Asymmetric/absent peripheral pulses** and **blood pressure discrepancy** ($>10\text{ mmHg}$ difference between upper extremities).
  * Arterial bruits (subclavian, carotid, abdominal).
  * Upper or lower limb claudication.
  * Hypertension secondary to **renal artery stenosis** (renovascular HTN).
  * Systemic symptoms: Fever, arthralgias, weight loss.
* **Diagnosis**: Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) or CT Angiography (CTA) showing vessel wall thickening, stenosis, occlusion, or aneurysms of the aorta. Biopsy is rarely done due to vessel size.

#### B. Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)
* *Note: See detailed notes in [[Giant Cell Arteritis]].*
* **Key difference from Takayasu**: Affects patients **$>50$ years** old, branches of the external carotid artery (temporal artery), associated with polymyalgia rheumatica, and carries a high risk of permanent blindness.

---

### 2. Medium Vessel Vasculitis (MVV)

#### A. Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)
* **Epidemiology**: Commonly presents in middle-aged adults. **Strongly associated with Hepatitis B virus (HBV)** infection in $10-30\%$ of cases.
* **Pathology**: Systemic necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized muscular arteries. **Characteristically spares the pulmonary vessels (no lung involvement).**
* **Clinical Manifestations**:
  * **Renal**: Vasculitis of renal arteries leading to ischemia, renovascular hypertension, and microaneurysms (no glomerulonephritis, as it is a medium-vessel disease).
  * **Gastrointestinal**: Mesenteric ischemia causing postprandial abdominal pain ("abdominal angina") and gastrointestinal bleeding.
  * **Neurological**: **Mononeuritis multiplex** (asymmetric painful sensorimotor neuropathy, e.g., wrist drop or foot drop) due to vasculitis of the vasa nervorum.
  * **Cutaneous**: Livedo reticularis, subcutaneous nodules, deep skin ulcers.
  * **Constitutional**: Fever, weight loss, myalgias, arthralgias.
* **Diagnosis**:
  * **Tissue Biopsy**: Biopsy of symptomatic organ (skin nodule, sural nerve, muscle) showing necrotizing inflammation of medium-sized arteries.
  * **Angiography**: Renal or mesenteric angiography showing focal stenosis, occlusion, and **multiple microaneurysms** ("string of pearls" appearance).
  * **Labs**: Negative for ANCA. Check Hepatitis B serology (HBsAg, anti-HBc).

#### B. Kawasaki Disease (Mucocutaneous Lymph Node Syndrome)
* **Epidemiology**: Children $<5$ years old (highest in Asian populations).
* **Clinical Tetrad + Criteria**: Fever for $\ge 5$ days + at least 4 of:
  1. Bilateral non-exudative conjunctivitis.
  2. Mucous membrane changes (strawberry tongue, dry/cracked lips).
  3. Polymorphous rash.
  4. Extremity changes (desquamation of fingers/toes, erythema/edema of palms/soles).
  5. Cervical lymphadenopathy ($>1.5$ cm, usually unilateral).
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Kawasaki Coronary Risk**: The most critical complication of Kawasaki disease is **Coronary Artery Aneurysms** (occurs in up to 25% of untreated children). Prevent by administering **High-dose IVIG (2 g/kg)** over 10-12 hours + **Aspirin** (high-dose initially for anti-inflammatory, then low-dose for anti-platelet). Monitor with echocardiography.

### 3. Small Vessel Vasculitis (SVV)

#### A. ANCA-Associated Vasculitides (AAV)
* *Note: See detailed notes in [[ANCA-associated Vasculitis]] and [[Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis]].*
* **Key Features**: Pauci-immune (lack of immune complexes on biopsy).
  * **GPA (Wegener's)**: **c-ANCA (PR3)** positive. Triad of **upper respiratory** (sinusitis, saddle-nose), **lower respiratory** (cavitary nodules/hemoptysis), and **renal** (pauci-immune crescentic GN).
  * **MPA**: **p-ANCA (MPO)** positive. Similar to GPA but **no granulomas** and **no upper respiratory tract involvement**.
  * **EGPA (Churg-Strauss)**: **p-ANCA (MPO)** positive. **Severe asthma**, eosinophilia, and transient pulmonary infiltrates.

#### B. Immune Complex-Mediated Small Vessel Vasculitis

##### 1. IgA Vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura / HSP)
* **Epidemiology**: Most common vasculitis in children (usually aged 3-15), but can occur in adults (who have worse renal prognosis). Often preceded by an **Upper Respiratory Infection (URI)**.
* **Pathophysiology**: Deposition of **IgA-containing immune complexes** in vessel walls.
* **Clinical Tetrad**:
  1. **Palpable Purpura**: Symmetrical, raised, non-blanching purpura on the lower extremities and buttocks (dependent areas).
  2. **Arthralgias / Arthritis**: Transient, non-deforming, affecting knees and ankles.
  3. **Abdominal Pain**: Colicky abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting. Risk of **intussusception** (ileocecal).
  4. **Renal Disease**: Hematuria, proteinuria (pathologically identical to IgA Nephropathy / Berger's disease).
* **Diagnosis**: Clinically diagnosed. Skin biopsy of purpura reveals **Leukocytoclastic Vasculitis (LCV) with IgA and complement deposition** on immunofluorescence.
* **Management**: Supportive (hydration, pain control). Systemic steroids for severe abdominal pain or progressive nephritis.

##### 2. Cryoglobulinemic Vasculitis
* **Pathophysiology**: Cryoglobulins are immunoglobulins that precipitate at cold temperatures.
* **Etiology**: **Strongly associated with Hepatitis C virus (HCV)** infection ($>90\%$ of cases).
* **Clinical Manifestations**: Meltzer's triad: **Palpable purpura**, **arthralgias**, and **weakness**. Also peripheral neuropathy, glomerulonephritis, and low complement levels (classically **extremely low C4**).
* **Diagnosis**: Detection of serum cryoglobulins. Positive HCV RNA.
* **Management**: Treatment of underlying Hep C infection (Direct-Acting Antivirals / DAAs) + plasmapheresis/steroids for severe flares.

## 🔬 Diagnostic Workup for Suspected Vasculitis
1. **Screening Labs**: CBC, ESR, CRP, Renal function (BUN/Cr), Liver function, Urinalysis (screen for hematuria, proteinuria, **RBC casts**).
2. **Serology**: ANA, RF, ANCA (c-ANCA/p-ANCA), Complement (C3, C4), Hepatitis B (PAN) and Hepatitis C (Cryoglobulinemia) serologies.
3. **Tissue Biopsy (Gold Standard for confirmation)**:
   * Skin biopsy of palpable purpura (reveals leukocytoclastic vasculitis).
   * Renal biopsy (reveals crescentic glomerulonephritis; pauci-immune in AAV, IgA-dominant in HSP).
   * Temporal artery biopsy (reveals giant cells and granulomatous inflammation in GCA).
4. **Angiography**: MRA/CTA of aorta/primary branches for Takayasu. Visceral angiography for PAN.

## 🏥 Management Principles

### 1. Remission Induction (Active, Organ- or Life-Threatening Disease)
* **Corticosteroids**: High-dose oral Prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) or **IV Methylprednisolone pulse** ($500 - 1000\text{ mg IV daily}$ for 3 days) for rapid control.
* **Immunosuppressants**: Combined with **IV Cyclophosphamide** OR **Rituximab** (anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody) to induce remission.
* **Plasmapheresis**: Indicated in severe pulmonary hemorrhage or rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.

### 2. Remission Maintenance (Once disease is controlled)
* Taper corticosteroids to lowest effective dose.
* Switch from Cyclophosphamide to less toxic agents: **Azathioprine** (1.5 - 2.5 mg/kg/day) or **Methotrexate** (15 - 25 mg/week) or **Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF)**.

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Pulmonary Sparing Rule**: In medical exams, a patient presenting with systemic necrotizing vasculitis, abdominal angina, hypertension, and foot drop, but with **completely normal lungs/chest X-ray**, has **Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)**. Check for Hepatitis B.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Palpable Purpura Hallmark**: Non-palpable purpura is a sign of platelet disorders (ITP) or vascular fragility (senile purpura). **Palpable purpura is the clinical hallmark of cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis.** Do not diagnose vasculitis based on flat ecchymoses.
* > [!TIP]
  > **C4 vs. C3 in Cryoglobulinemia**: In HCV-associated cryoglobulinemia, **C4 is characteristically extremely low (often undetectable)** while C3 is normal or only mildly reduced. This is due to selective activation of the classical pathway by the cryoprecipitates.
