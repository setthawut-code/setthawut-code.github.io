## type: disease

# Definition
Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) is a heterogeneous group of chronic inflammatory arthritides of unknown etiology presenting in children **under the age of 16** and persisting for **at least 6 weeks**. It is the most common pediatric chronic rheumatologic condition.

# Classification & Subtypes
JIA is classified into distinct categories based on clinical and laboratory features during the first 6 months of disease onset:

### 1. Oligoarticular JIA (Most Common, ~50–60%)
* **Definition**: Affects **$\le 4$ joints** during the first 6 months.
* **Characteristics**: Asymmetric involvement of large joints (knees, ankles).
* **Uveitis Risk**: Highest risk of chronic, asymptomatic, bilateral **anterior uveitis** (iridocyclitis), particularly in patients who are **ANA-positive**.
* **Subtypes**: *Persistent* (remains $\le 4$ joints) vs. *Extended* (involves $>4$ joints after the first 6 months).

### 2. Systemic JIA (sJIA / Still's Disease, ~10–15%)
* **Definition**: Arthritis in $\ge 1$ joint accompanied by or preceded by a documented **spiking fever** for $\ge 2$ weeks.
* **Fever Pattern**: Characteristically **quotidian** (spikes once or twice daily, typically in the evening, and rapidly returns to baseline or subnormal).
* **Evanescent Rash**: A **salmon-pink, macular, non-pruritic rash** that appears during fever spikes and fades as the temperature normalizes, primarily on the trunk and proximal extremities.
* **Other Features**: Hepatosplenomegaly, generalized lymphadenopathy, and serositis (pericarditis, pleuritis). Not strongly linked to HLA or autoantibodies (mediated by innate immunity/autoinflammatory).

### 3. Polyarticular JIA (~15–20%)
* **Definition**: Affects **$\ge 5$ joints** in the first 6 months.
* **RF-Negative**: Variable onset, asymmetric or symmetric, moderate severity.
* **RF-Positive**: Occurs predominantly in adolescent girls; behaves similarly to adult Rheumatoid Arthritis (symmetric small-joint involvement of MCPs, PIPs, aggressive, erosive, and high risk of rheumatoid nodules).

### 4. Enthesitis-Related JIA (ERA, ~5–10%)
* **Definition**: Arthritis and/or enthesitis, plus $\ge 2$ features (sacroiliac joint tenderness, inflammatory spinal pain, positive HLA-B27, family history of HLA-B27 disease).
* **Characteristics**: Most common in boys $>6$ years. Affects lower extremities (Achilles tendonitis, plantar fasciitis) and axial skeleton. Acts like juvenile spondyloarthritis.

### 5. Psoriatic JIA (~5%)
* **Definition**: Arthritis + psoriasis, or arthritis + $\ge 2$ features (dactylitis, nail pitting, family history of psoriasis).

# Etiology & Pathophysiology
* **Oligoarticular / Polyarticular JIA**: Classical autoimmune pathogenesis driven by autoreactive CD4+ T-helper cells (Th1, Th17) that infiltrate the synovium, causing chronic synovitis, hyperplasia, and bone erosion.
* **Systemic JIA**: Autoinflammatory pathogenesis driven by the innate immune system. Overproduction of IL-1 and IL-6 plays a central role, driving systemic inflammation, leukocytosis, and spiking fevers.

---

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Joint Dysfunction**: Limping, morning stiffness (the child is slow to get out of bed or refuses to walk in the morning, which improves as the day progresses), and irritability.
* **Pain**: Children often do not complain of pain directly but show regression in developmental milestones or avoidance of physical play.

## Physical Examination
* **Joint Swelling & Warmth**: Active synovitis, often with loss of full range of motion.
* **Leg Length Discrepancy**: Chronic hyperemia in a single joint (e.g., knee) stimulates adjacent growth plates (epiphyseal acceleration), leading to overgrowth and leg length discrepancy.
* **Micrognathia**: Chronic bilateral temporomandibular joint (TMJ) arthritis arrests mandible growth, leading to a retrognathic jaw ("bird-face" deformity).

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **Transient Synovitis (Toxic Synovitis)**: The most common cause of acute hip pain in children. Post-viral, self-limiting within 1-2 weeks. Normal inflammatory markers.
* **Septic Arthritis**: Acute monoarthritis, high fever, refusal to bear weight, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, and synovial fluid WBC $>50,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)**: Can mimic JIA with bone pain and joint swelling. Differentiated by out-of-proportion bone pain (wakes child at night), severe night sweats, cytopenias (leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia), and blast cells on peripheral smear.
* **Acute Rheumatic Fever**: Migratory polyarthritis following Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis, accompanied by carditis, chorea, erythema marginatum, and elevated ASO titer.

# Investigations

## Laboratory
* **ANA (Antinuclear Antibody)**: Positive in up to $70\%$ of oligoarticular JIA patients. **Critical marker for chronic anterior uveitis risk.**
* **RF & Anti-CCP**: Positive only in the polyarticular RF+ subtype.
* **Inflammatory Markers**: ESR and CRP are elevated, particularly in systemic JIA (where they are characteristically extremely high).
* **CBC**: Leukocytosis and thrombocytosis (highly elevated in active systemic JIA); microcytic anemia of chronic disease.

## 👁️ Ophthalmology Screening (Critical Step)
> [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Asymptomatic Uveitis Screening**: Chronic anterior uveitis in JIA is **painless, white, and asymptomatic** (no red eye or photophobia). If undetected, it leads to band keratopathy, cataracts, glaucoma, and permanent blindness.
  > **Slit-lamp examinations must be scheduled regularly** (every 3 months for ANA-positive oligoarticular JIA).

## Imaging
* **Plain Radiography**: Early stages show soft tissue swelling and periarticular osteopenia. Late stages show joint space narrowing, erosions, and growth anomalies.

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
Clinically diagnosed based on persistent arthritis in one or more joints in a child $<16$ years of age lasting for $\ge 6$ weeks, after excluding other pediatric bone, joint, infectious, or neoplastic conditions.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **First-line Symptomatic (NSAIDs)**: Naproxen (10-20 mg/kg/day divided bid) or Ibuprofen, Meloxicam to control pain and stiffness. NSAIDs do not prevent joint damage.
* **Intra-articular Corticosteroid Injections**: Highly effective first-line therapy for oligoarticular JIA, providing rapid local control. Triamcinolone hexacetonide is preferred.

## Systemic DMARD Therapy
* Indicated for patients with polyarticular JIA, extended oligoarticular JIA, or those unresponsive to NSAIDs and local injections.
* **Methotrexate (MTX)**: First-line conventional synthetic DMARD (dosed weekly, PO or SC, with Folic acid supplementation).

## Biologic Therapy
* **TNF-$\alpha$ Inhibitors** (e.g., **Adalimumab**, **Etanercept**): First-line biologic for polyarticular JIA refractory to Methotrexate. Highly effective for treating co-existing uveitis.
* **IL-1 & IL-6 Inhibitors** (First-line for Systemic JIA):
  * **Anakinra** (IL-1 receptor antagonist) or **Canakinumab** (monoclonal anti-IL-1$\beta$).
  * **Tocilizumab** (IL-6 receptor antagonist).
  * *Rationale*: Because sJIA is an autoinflammatory disease driven by IL-1 and IL-6, these biologics provide rapid resolution of systemic fevers, rash, and arthritis.

# Complications
* **Macrophage Activation Syndrome (MAS)**:
  * A life-threatening complication occurring in up to $10\%$ of **systemic JIA** patients.
  * *Pathophysiology*: Uncontrolled activation of T-cells and macrophages leading to a **cytokine storm** and hemophagocytosis in the bone marrow.
  * *Clinical Features*: **Persistent high fever** (losing its quotidian pattern), hepatosplenomegaly, bleeding/purpura, and encephalopathy.
  * *Lab Features*: **Pancytopenia**, **exceedingly high Ferritin ($>10,000\text{ ng/mL}$)**, low fibrinogen, elevated liver enzymes, elevated triglycerides, and prolonged PT/INR.
  * *Management*: High-dose intravenous Methylprednisolone, Cyclosporine, or Anakinra.
* **Blindness**: Secondary to undetected chronic uveitis.
* **Growth Retardation**: Due to chronic inflammation and prolonged corticosteroid use.

# Prognosis
* Oligoarticular JIA has the best prognosis, though extended oligoarticular JIA has a higher risk of joint damage.
* RF-positive polyarticular JIA carries the highest risk of chronic, progressive joint destruction extending into adulthood.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Silent Eye**: Never wait for a child with JIA to complain of eye pain or redness. The uveitis in oligoarticular JIA is **completely asymptomatic**. Slit-lamp monitoring is the only way to detect it.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **MAS vs. Flare**: If a patient with systemic JIA suddenly develops a **persistent, non-spiking fever** associated with a drop in WBC and platelet counts, suspect **Macrophage Activation Syndrome**. Although active inflammation usually raises platelet counts (as an acute phase reactant), a *sudden drop* in platelets indicates consumption via hemophagocytosis. This is a medical emergency.
