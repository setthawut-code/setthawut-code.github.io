## type: disease

# Definition
Gout is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by the deposition of **monosodium urate (MSU) crystals** in joints and soft tissues, secondary to persistent hyperuricemia. It is the most common cause of inflammatory arthritis in adult males, presenting as recurrent episodes of acute monoarthritis, which can progress to chronic deforming tophaceous arthropathy, nephrolithiasis, and renal impairment.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Affects approximately $1-4\%$ of the global population.
* **Gender Ratio**: Significantly more common in men ($4-9:1$ male-to-female ratio). In women, onset is typically post-menopausal (estrogen is uricosuric).
* **Age of Onset**: Peaks in the 4th to 6th decades in men.
* **Risk Factors**: Obesity, high-purine diet (red meat, seafood, beer, spirits), high-fructose corn syrup, renal insufficiency, hypertension, and medications (diuretics, low-dose aspirin).

# Etiology & Pathophysiology
Hyperuricemia is defined as a serum urate level **$>6.8\text{ mg/dL}$** (the physiological solubility limit of MSU at $37^\circ\text{C}$). It is caused by:

### 1. Renal Urate Underexcretion ($~90\%$ of cases)
* Impaired clearance of uric acid by the kidneys.
* *Causes*: Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), volume depletion, and drugs that inhibit renal urate transport:
  * **Thiazide and Loop Diuretics**.
  * **Low-dose Aspirin** ($<2\text{ g/day}$).
  * **Cyclosporine**, Tacrolimus.
  * **Pyrazinamide**, Ethambutol.

### 2. Uric Acid Overproduction ($~10\%$ of cases)
* Increased synthesis of purines or high tissue turnover.
* *Causes*: Myeloproliferative and lymphoproliferative disorders, psoriasis, chemotherapy (Tumor Lysis Syndrome), high dietary purine intake, and rare genetic enzyme defects (e.g., Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, PRPP synthetase overactivity).

### 3. Joint Inflammation Pathway
1. **MSU Precipitation**: Supersaturated uric acid precipitates as needle-like crystals in the avascular joint tissues.
2. **Macrophage Phagocytosis**: Resident macrophages phagocytose the crystals, activating the **NLRP3 inflammasome**.
3. **IL-1$\beta$ Release**: Activated inflammasomes cleave pro-IL-1$\beta$ into active **IL-1$\beta$**, which is secreted into the joint space.
4. **Neutrophil Recruitment**: IL-1$\beta$ stimulates endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules, recruiting neutrophils that release oxygen radicals, lysosomal enzymes, and cytokines, producing intense local inflammation.

# Clinical Manifestations

## 1. Acute Gouty Arthritis
* **Onset**: Abrupt onset, often overnight, reaching peak intensity within **12 to 24 hours**.
* **Pain**: Excruciating, throbbing pain. The joint is exquisitely tender, warm, swollen, and erythematous, mimicking cellulitis.
* **Localization**: **Monoarticular in $85-90\%$ of initial attacks**. The **first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint** is involved in $50\%$ of cases (**Podagra**). Other sites: ankle, midfoot, knee, and wrist.
* **Triggers**: Alcohol consumption (beer and spirits are worse than wine), heavy red meat/seafood meal, dehydration, trauma, surgery, or starting urate-lowering therapy.

## 2. Intercritical Gout
* Complete resolution of symptoms between acute attacks. If untreated, intervals between flares shorten and attacks become polyarticular.

## 3. Chronic Tophaceous Gout
* **Tophi**: Painless, firm, yellow-white nodules containing large aggregates of MSU crystals surrounded by granulomatous inflammation. Located on extensor surfaces of elbows, Achilles tendons, finger pads, and the helix of the ear. Can ulcerate and discharge a chalky, paste-like material.
* **Arthropathy**: Chronic progressive joint stiffness and deformity, mimicking RA.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Infectious Arthritis]] (Septic Arthritis)**: The most critical differential. A septic joint can present with identical pain, fever, and redness. Must be ruled out by synovial fluid Gram stain and culture.
* **[[Pseudogout]] (CPPD)**: Presents with acute monoarthritis, but affects larger joints (knees, wrists) and synovial fluid contains positively birefringent rhomboid crystals.
* **Cellulitis**: Superficial skin infection. Unlike gout, the pain in cellulitis is not sharply localized to the joint capsule, and passive range of motion of the joint is relatively preserved.
* **Palindromic Rheumatism**: Recurrent, self-limiting episodes of acute arthritis, but typically resolves in 24-48 hours and is associated with positive Anti-CCP.

# Investigations

## 🔬 Synovial Fluid Analysis (Diagnostic Gold Standard)
**Must be performed during an acute flare to confirm diagnosis.**
* **Polarized Light Microscopy**: Demonstrates **needle-shaped, negatively birefringent** monosodium urate (MSU) crystals.
* **Birefringence Rule**: Under a red compensator filter, crystals oriented **parallel** to the slow ray of the compensator appear **yellow**, while those **perpendicular** appear **blue**. ("**Y**ellow is **P**arallel" $\rightarrow$ **Y.P.**).
* **WBC Count**: Typically $2,000 - 50,000/\mu\text{L}$ with $>75\%$ PMNs (inflammatory profile).

## Laboratory
* **Serum Uric Acid**:
  > [!WARNING]
    > **The Normal Uric Acid Trap**: Serum uric acid levels **can be completely normal in up to 30% of patients during an acute gouty flare**. This occurs because uric acid is an acute phase reactant excreted by the kidneys during stress, and it precipitates out of the blood into the joint. Do not use a normal serum uric acid level to rule out acute gout.
* **ESR & CRP**: Elevated during flares.

## Imaging
* **Plain Radiography**:
  * Acute gout: Normal or soft tissue swelling.
  * Chronic tophaceous gout: **Punched-out bony erosions with overhanging margins** ("rat-bite" erosions). Joint space is characteristically preserved until very late stages, and there is an absence of periarticular osteopenia (unlike RA).
* **Dual-Energy CT (DECT)**: Color-codes urate deposits green, highly sensitive for detecting subclinical tophi.

# Management

## 1. Treatment of Acute Flare (Anti-inflammatory)
**Goal is rapid relief. Initiate within 24 hours of onset. Do not modify existing Urate-Lowering Therapy.**
* **First-line Options** (based on patient comorbidities):
  1. **NSAIDs** (e.g., **Indomethacin 50 mg PO tid**, **Naproxen 500 mg PO bid**, or **Celecoxib 200 mg PO bid**). Avoid in severe CKD, active PUD, or heart failure.
  2. **Colchicine**:
     * *Dose*: **1.2 mg PO at onset, followed by 0.6 mg 1 hour later** (total 1.8 mg on Day 1), then 0.6 mg daily or bid until resolution.
     * *Side Effects*: Diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal cramps. Renally cleared; reduce dose in CKD.
  3. **Corticosteroids**: Oral Prednisolone 30-40 mg daily for 5 days (preferred in patients with renal failure, where NSAIDs/Colchicine are contraindicated). Alternatively, intra-articular Triamcinolone injection for monoarticular gout.

## 2. Chronic Management: Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)
* **Indications to Start**: $\ge 2$ flares per year, presence of tophi, CKD stage $\ge 2$, or history of uric acid kidney stones.
* **Target**: Lower serum uric acid to **$<6.0\text{ mg/dL}$** ($<5.0\text{ mg/dL}$ in patients with tophaceous gout to drive tophi dissolution).
* **First-line (Allopurinol)**:
  * Xanthine oxidase inhibitor (blocks conversion of hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to uric acid).
  > [!IMPORTANT]
    > **HLA-B\*5801 Screening Rule**: In patients of Han Chinese, Thai, or Korean descent, **screen for the HLA-B\*5801 allele prior to starting Allopurinol**. The presence of this allele is strongly associated with **Allopurinol Hypersensitivity Syndrome (AHS)**, presenting as Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS), Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN), and DRESS syndrome, which carries a 20-30% mortality rate. If positive, do not use Allopurinol.
  * *Dose*: Start low (**100 mg PO daily**; 50 mg in CKD) and titrate up by 100 mg every 2-4 weeks until the uric acid target is achieved (max 800 mg/day).
* **Alternative ULT**:
  * **Febuxostat**: Non-purine xanthine oxidase inhibitor. Use if Allopurinol is not tolerated or contraindicated. (Black box warning: caution in patients with high risk of cardiovascular disease).
  * **Benzbromarone** or **Probenecid**: Uricosurics (block renal URAT1 transporter to increase excretion). Contraindicated in patients with CKD or a history of nephrolithiasis.

## 3. Flare Prophylaxis during ULT Initiation
> [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Mobilization Flare**: Starting or adjusting ULT causes a sudden drop in serum uric acid, which leads to micro-fractures and dissolution of joint tophi, shedding crystals into the joint cavity and triggering acute flares.
  > **Always co-prescribe low-dose Colchicine (0.6 mg PO daily or bid)** or low-dose NSAID as prophylaxis when starting Allopurinol, and continue for **3 to 6 months**.

# Complications
* **Uric Acid Urolithiasis**: Kidney stones (radiolucent on plain X-rays).
* **Urate Nephropathy**: Chronic interstitial nephritis from crystal deposition in the renal parenchyma.
* **Tophaceous Joint Destruction**: Severe bony erosion and functional deformity.

# Clinical Pearls
> [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Don't Stop, Don't Start**:
  > * If a patient is already taking Allopurinol when an acute flare starts, **do not stop Allopurinol**. Rapidly changing urate levels will prolong and worsen the flare.
  > * If the patient is not on Allopurinol when a flare starts, **do not start Allopurinol** during the acute flare. Wait 2 weeks after the flare has completely resolved before initiating Allopurinol.

> [!TIP]
  > **Estrogen Protection**: Estrogen stimulates the renal excretion of uric acid. This is why pre-menopausal women rarely develop gout. If a young woman presents with symptoms of gout, strongly investigate for secondary causes like severe renal insufficiency, genetic defects, or drug abuse.
