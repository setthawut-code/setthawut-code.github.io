# 🦴 Giant Cell Arteritis / Temporal Arteritis (โรคหลอดเลือดขมับอักเสบ)

> **Definition**: Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA), also known as Temporal Arteritis, is a systemic, granulomatous vasculitis of medium and large-sized arteries, primarily affecting the branches of the external carotid artery (specifically the temporal, ophthalmic, posterior ciliary, and occipital arteries). It is a critical **medical and ophthalmic emergency** because delay in treatment can lead to irreversible blindness.

---

## 📊 Epidemiology
* **Age Limit**: Almost **exclusively affects patients age $>50$ years**, peaking between 70 and 80 years.
* **Gender Ratio**: Significantly more common in females ($2-3:1$ female-to-male ratio).
* **Connection to PMR**: Up to **$40 - 60\%$** of patients with GCA have co-existing Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR). Conversely, $15-20\%$ of PMR patients develop GCA.

---

## 🦠 Pathophysiology
GCA is an immune-mediated antigen-driven inflammatory disease targeting the arterial wall structure:
1. **Antigen Presentation**: T-cells are recruited to the adventitia of medium/large arteries, where they are activated by resident dendritic cells.
2. **Granulomatous Influx**: Infiltrating CD4+ T-helper cells (Th1, Th17) release **IFN-$\gamma$** and **IL-17**, recruiting macrophages.
3. **Giant Cell Formation**: Macrophages fuse to form **multinucleated giant cells** at the junction of the media and intima.
4. **Vascular Destruction**: Giant cells and macrophages release metalloproteinases and reactive oxygen species, leading to fragmentation of the **internal elastic lamina**.
5. **Occlusion (Ischemia)**: Intimal smooth muscle cells proliferate (intimal hyperplasia) in response to growth factors (PDGF, VEGF), causing lumen narrowing, thrombosis, and tissue ischemia.

## 🔍 Clinical Manifestations

### 1. Classical Symptoms
* **New-Onset Headache**: Present in $>70\%$ of cases. Typically localized to the temporal or occipital region, constant, throbbing, and severe.
* **Jaw Claudication**: Pain, fatigue, or cramping of the masseter or temporalis muscles during chewing, which resolves with rest. **This symptom has the highest specificity ($>90\%$) for GCA.**
* **Visual Manifestations (The Ophthalmic Emergency)**:
  * **Amaurosis Fugax**: Transient, painless, monocular vision loss (often described as a curtain coming down). **This is a warning sign of impending permanent blindness.**
  * **Permanent Blindness**: Caused by **Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (AION)** due to occlusion of the posterior ciliary arteries (branches of the ophthalmic artery supplying the optic nerve). Fundoscopy reveals a pale, swollen optic disc with blurred margins.
* **Scalp Tenderness**: Pain when combing hair, washing the face, or resting the head on a pillow.
* **Systemic / Fever of Unknown Origin**: High fevers, night sweats, fatigue, and weight loss. GCA is a leading cause of FUO in patients $>65$ years.

### 2. Physical Examination
* **Temporal Arteries**: May be thickened, tender, nodular, erythematous, or pulseless upon palpation.
* **Large Vessel Signs**: Bruits over the subclavian, axillary, or carotid arteries. Decreased or asymmetric pulses in the upper extremities.

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis
* **Primary Headaches (Migraine, Tension)**: Chronic history, younger age of onset, normal inflammatory markers.
* **Non-Arteritic Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (NAION)**: Optic nerve stroke in patients with hypertension/diabetes; normal ESR/CRP and no systemic symptoms.
* **Takayasu Arteritis**: Large vessel granulomatous vasculitis, but **occurs in young females (age $<40$ years)**, affecting the aorta and its main branches (pulseless disease).
* **Amyloidosis** or systemic necrotizing vasculitis.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

### Laboratory
* **Inflammatory Markers**: Characteristically **markedly elevated ESR (often $>50-100\text{ mm/hour}$)** and elevated CRP. A normal ESR/CRP makes GCA highly unlikely.
* **CBC**: Normocytic normochromic anemia (anemia of chronic disease) and reactive thrombocytosis.
* **LFTs**: Mild elevation of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in $30\%$ of cases.

### Imaging
* **Temporal Artery Ultrasound**: Shows a **"Halo Sign"** (a hypoechoic, dark ring surrounding the patent arterial lumen, representing vessel wall edema).
* **CT/MRI/PET-CT**: Indicated to evaluate for large vessel involvement (aortic aneurysm or dissection of the thoracic aorta).

### 🚨 Temporal Artery Biopsy (TAB - Diagnostic Gold Standard)
* Must be performed to confirm diagnosis, but **steroid therapy must NOT be delayed while waiting for the biopsy**.
* **Procedure Rule**: A biopsy specimen of **at least $1.5 - 2.0\text{ cm}$** in length must be obtained. This is because GCA features **"skip lesions"** (areas of active vasculitis interspersed with normal segments); a short biopsy can lead to a false negative.
* **Biopsy Timing**: TAB remains histologically diagnostic for up to 1-2 weeks after starting steroid therapy.

---

## Diagnostic Criteria
### 1990 ACR Classification Criteria (Requires $\ge 3 / 5$ criteria)
1. Age at disease onset $\ge 50$ years.
2. New headache or new type of localized head pain.
3. Temporal artery tenderness or decreased pulsation.
4. Elevated ESR $\ge 50$ mm/hour.
5. Abnormal temporal artery biopsy (showing granulomatous inflammation or giant cells).

---

## 🏥 Management

### 🚨 Emergency Corticosteroid Protocol
If GCA is clinically suspected, **start corticosteroids immediately**. Do **not** wait for a temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound. A single day of delayed therapy can result in permanent, irreversible blindness.

```
GCA Steroid Decision Path
├── 1. Suspicion of GCA WITHOUT Visual Loss
│   └── Action: Start PREDNISONE 40 - 60 mg PO daily immediately
├── 2. Suspicion of GCA WITH Visual Loss or Amaurosis Fugax
│   ├── Action: Admit for IV METHYLPREDNISOLONE 500 - 1000 mg daily for 3 days
│   └── Goal: Stabilize vision in the affected eye and protect the contralateral eye
└── 3. Biopsy Scheduling
    └── Action: Perform Temporal Artery Biopsy (TAB) within 7 - 14 days of starting steroids
```

### Steroid-Sparing & Maintenance Therapy
* **Taper Protocol**: Once symptoms resolve and ESR/CRP normalize (usually within 2-4 weeks), begin tapering Prednisone slowly over **12 to 18 months**. 
* **Tocilizumab (Anti-IL-6 receptor)**: Given subcutaneously once weekly. Approved to reduce steroid exposure and prevent relapses.
* **Aspirin (Low-Dose)**: **81 mg PO daily** is co-prescribed to reduce the risk of cranial ischemic complications (stroke, visual loss).
* **Bone Protection**: Co-prescribe Calcium + Vitamin D + Bisphosphonates due to prolonged high-dose steroid use.

---

## 🫁 Complications
* **Irreversible Blindness**: Due to AION or central retinal artery occlusion.
* **Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm & Dissection**: GCA can affect the aorta; patients have an increased risk of aortic rupture years after the cranial symptoms resolve. Annual screening with chest X-ray or CT is recommended.
* **Ischemic Stroke**.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Ophthalmic Emergency Rule**: Visual loss in GCA is almost always permanent; steroids **cannot** restore vision that has already been lost. The primary goal of emergency steroid therapy (especially IV methylprednisolone pulse) is to **prevent bilateral blindness** by protecting the healthy contralateral eye from ischemic occlusion.
* > [!TIP]
  > **Biopsy Length Warning**: Because of "skip lesions," a small biopsy segment ($< 1$ cm) has a high false-negative rate. Ensure the surgeon excises at least $1.5 - 2$ cm of the temporal artery. If the biopsy is negative but clinical suspicion remains high, consider performing a biopsy on the contralateral temporal artery.
