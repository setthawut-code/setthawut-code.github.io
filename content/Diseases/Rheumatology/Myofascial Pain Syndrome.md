## type: disease

# Definition
Myofascial Pain Syndrome (MPS) is a common chronic regional pain disorder characterized by the presence of hyperirritable spots called **myofascial trigger points (MTrPs)** located within taut bands of skeletal muscle or fascia. Palpation of these trigger points reproduces local pain and a characteristic referred pain pattern.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Extremely common in clinical practice, accounting for up to 30% of patients presenting with musculoskeletal pain in general outpatient clinics, and up to 85% in specialized pain management centers.
* **Demographics**: More common in females than males. Most frequently diagnosed between ages 30 and 50.
* **Risk Factors**: Sedentary lifestyle, poor posture, repetitive occupational microtrauma, structural discrepancies (e.g., leg length discrepancy), chronic stress, and sleep deprivation.

# Etiology
* **Primary (Direct) Muscle Trauma**: Acute muscle strain, repetitive microtrauma, or chronic muscle overload (e.g., poor ergonomics, typing, heavy lifting).
* **Secondary Muscle Dysfunction**: Protective muscle guarding secondary to adjacent joint disorders (e.g., cervical/lumbar spondylosis, osteoarthritis) or radiculopathies.
* **Systemic/Predisposing Factors**: Chronic fatigue, emotional stress, anxiety, nutritional deficiencies (specifically Vitamin D, Vitamin B12, Iron), and hypothyroidism.

# Pathophysiology
The most widely accepted model is **Simons' Integrated Hypothesis**:
1. **Acetylcholine (ACh) Excess**: Abnormal and continuous release of ACh from the motor nerve terminal at the neuromuscular junction (motor endplate), even at rest.
2. **Sustained Contraction**: Excessive ACh leads to continuous depolarization and calcium release, causing localized, persistent sarcomere shortening (forming the **taut band**).
3. **Ischemia & Energy Crisis**: The sustained contraction compresses local capillaries, reducing local blood flow and leading to muscle ischemia and hypoxia. The reduction in oxygen and nutrients combined with high metabolic demand creates an **energy crisis**.
4. **Sensitizing Substance Release**: Ischemia and cell distress cause the release of nociceptive substances (protons, bradykinin, serotonin, calcitonin gene-related peptide [CGRP], TNF-$\alpha$, and interleukins).
5. **Sensitization**: These substances lower the activation threshold of muscle nociceptors, causing **peripheral sensitization**. Over time, continuous nociceptive input to the spinal cord leads to **central sensitization** and referred pain.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           ABNORMAL / EXCESSIVE ACh RELEASE             │
└───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                            ▼
          Sustained Local Sarcomere Contraction
                            │ (Taut Band Formation)
                            ▼
             Capillary Compression & Ischemia
                            │
                            ▼
           Local Hypoxia & Cellular Energy Crisis
                            │ (Depletion of ATP)
                            ▼
         Release of Inflammatory & Nociceptive Agents
              (H+, Bradykinin, CGRP, Cytokines)
                            │
                            ▼
      Nociceptor Activation & Peripheral/Central Sensitization
                  (Local & Referred Pain)
```

# Clinical Manifestations

## Symptoms
* **Regional Pain**: Dull, deep, aching pain restricted to a specific region (e.g., neck, shoulder girdle, lower back, buttock) rather than widespread.
* **Referred Pain**: Pain that projects from the trigger point to a distant site in a predictable, non-dermatomal pattern (e.g., trigger point in upper trapezius referring pain to the temple, mimicking a tension headache).
* **Stiffness & Weakness**: Decreased range of motion in the affected joint and mild muscle weakness (due to pain inhibition) without muscle atrophy.

## Physical Examination
* **Palpable Taut Band**: A cord-like band of contracted muscle fibers felt on palpation.
* **Myofascial Trigger Point (MTrP)**: An exquisitely tender spot within the taut band.
  * **Active MTrP**: Causes spontaneous regional pain and reproduces the patient's familiar pain when palpated.
  * **Latent MTrP**: Painless at rest but causes local tenderness and referred pain only upon manual compression.
* **Jump Sign**: An involuntary physical withdrawal or vocalization by the patient in response to firm pressure applied over a trigger point.
* **Local Twitch Response (LTR)**: A transient visual or palpable contraction of the taut band elicited by snapping palpation perpendicular to the muscle fibers or during needle penetration.

---

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Fibromyalgia]]**: Differentiated by **widespread** bilateral, upper/lower body pain (rather than regional) lasting $>3$ months, systemic symptoms (severe fatigue, sleep disturbance, cognitive dysfunction), and the absence of taut bands or local twitch responses.
* **Cervical / Lumbar Radiculopathy**: Presents with dermatomal pain, paresthesias, numbness, diminished reflexes, and motor deficits (MPS can coexist as secondary muscle spasm).
* **Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)**: Seen in patients $>50$ years; characterized by bilateral shoulder and hip girdle stiffness and pain, associated with markedly elevated ESR/CRP.
* **Tendinopathy / Bursitis**: Pain localized directly over the tendon insertion or bursa, exacerbated by active or resisted tendon movement, without muscular taut bands.

---

# Investigations
* **Diagnostic Workup**: MPS is a **clinical diagnosis**. There are no laboratory, imaging, or electrodiagnostic markers.
* **Exclusionary Testing**:
  * **Lab**: CBC, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), ESR, CRP, Vitamin D, and Vitamin B12 are normal. Elicited to rule out hypothyroidism, polymyalgia rheumatica, or severe nutritional deficiencies.
  * **Imaging / EMG**: Plain X-rays, MRI, and electromyography (EMG) are normal (except to rule out underlying radiculopathy or arthritis).

---

# Diagnostic Criteria
MPS is diagnosed based on clinical findings. Commonly used criteria require the presence of:
1. A palpable taut band in skeletal muscle.
2. An exquisitely tender spot within the taut band.
3. Reproduction of the patient's familiar pain upon pressure.
4. (Optional but supportive) A local twitch response and a referred pain pattern.

---

# Management

## Initial Management
* **Patient Education & Ergonomics**: Correct posture, modify repetitive occupational movements, optimize workstation setup, and address sleep hygiene.
* **Pharmacotherapy**:
  * **NSAIDs** (e.g., Naproxen, Celecoxib): Provide mild, short-term relief of pain but do not treat the underlying taut band.
  * **Muscle Relaxants** (e.g., Eperisone, Baclofen, Tizanidine): Useful in the acute phase to reduce muscle spasms.
* **Physical Therapy**:
  * **"Stretch and Spray" Technique**: Passive stretching of the affected muscle while spraying a vapocoolant spray (e.g., ethyl chloride) over the muscle and referred pain zone.
  * **Manual Therapy**: Ischemic compression (applying deep pressure over the MTrP to disrupt the sustained contraction), myofascial release, and heat therapy.

## Definitive Management
* **Trigger Point Injections (TPI)**:
  * Injection of a local anesthetic (e.g., 1–2 mL of 1% lidocaine) directly into the hyperirritable spot.
  * *Mechanism*: Mechanical disruption of the dysfunctional motor endplates by the needle (most critical factor) + anesthetic relief of local pain.
* **Dry Needling**: Insertion of a fine acupuncture needle into the trigger point without injecting fluid, targeting a local twitch response. Highly effective, comparable to lidocaine injection.
* **Chronic Management**: Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., **Amitriptyline 10 mg PO at bedtime**) to improve sleep quality and decrease central sensitization.

---

# Complications
* **Chronic Pain Syndrome**: Longstanding untreated MPS can lead to central sensitization, making the pain harder to manage.
* **Impaired Range of Motion**: Chronic muscle shortening can lead to joint stiffness and contractures.
* **Sleep Disturbance & Anxiety**: Chronic pain impairs sleep quality, contributing to mood disorders.

# Prognosis
* Generally favorable with early diagnosis, ergonomic corrections, and treatment of trigger points.
* High rate of recurrence if underlying predisposing factors (poor posture, repetitive strain, stress, or secondary pathology like osteoarthritis) are not fully corrected.

# Clinical Pearls
* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **MPS vs. Fibromyalgia**: Remember that MPS is **regional** (one or two areas, e.g., shoulder/neck) and associated with localized **taut bands** and **referred pain**. Fibromyalgia is **widespread** (all four quadrants of the body) and characterized by central sensitization without taut bands.
* > [!TIP]
  > **The Needle is Key**: In trigger point injection, the mechanical disruption of the tight sarcomeres by the needle itself is the primary therapeutic action (evidenced by eliciting a local twitch response). The choice of local anesthetic is secondary and mainly serves to reduce post-injection soreness.
