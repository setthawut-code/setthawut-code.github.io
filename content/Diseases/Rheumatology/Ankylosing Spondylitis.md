# 🦴 Ankylosing Spondylitis (โรคข้อสันหลังอักเสบยึดติด)

> **Definition**: Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS) is a chronic, progressive, systemic inflammatory arthritis characterized by sacroiliitis, enthesitis, and a propensity for bony ankylosis (fusion) of the axial skeleton (spine and sacroiliac joints). It is the prototype of the **seronegative spondyloarthropathies**.

---

## 📊 Epidemiology
* **Age of Onset**: Typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, peaking between ages 15 and 30. Onset after age 40 is rare.
* **Gender Ratio**: Historically reported as $3:1$ male-to-female ratio, but newer studies indicate a more equal distribution, though males tend to have more severe radiographic progression.
* **Genetic Association**: Strongest known HLA association in medicine: **$>90\%$ of patients with AS are HLA-B27 positive**. (However, only $2-8\%$ of HLA-B27 positive individuals in the general population will develop AS).

---

## 🦠 Etiology & Pathophysiology
AS is an immune-mediated disorder with a strong polygenic genetic predisposition. HLA-B27 plays a primary role, likely by presenting arthritogenic peptides to CD8+ T-cells, or through misfolding of the HLA-B27 heavy chain causing endoplasmic reticulum stress and subsequent inflammatory cytokine release.

### Pathological Mechanisms
1. **Enthesitis**: The primary pathological lesion is inflammation at the **entheses** (the sites where tendons, ligaments, or joint capsules insert into bone), particularly the annulus fibrosus of intervertebral discs and the sacroiliac ligaments.
2. **Erosion & Inflammation**: T-cells, macrophages, and cytokines (predominantly **TNF-$\alpha$, IL-17, and IL-23**) infiltrate these sites, causing localized bone resorption and erosion.
3. **Syndesmophyte Formation**: During the healing phase, granulation tissue is replaced by fibrocartilage which undergoes ossification. This forms **syndesmophytes**—vertical bony outgrowths that project from the margins of one vertebral body to the next.
4. **Ankylosis**: Progressive syndesmophyte growth and ossification of spinal ligaments (anterior/posterior longitudinal ligaments) leads to complete bony fusion of the spine (**"Bamboo Spine"**).

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│        ENTHESITIS (Ligament-to-Bone Insertion)         │
└───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                            ▼
      Infiltration of IL-23/IL-17 Producing T-Cells
                            │
                            ▼
          Osteoclast Activation & Bone Erosion
                            │
                            ▼
     Healing phase: Fibrocartilage Repair & Ossification
                            │ (Syndesmophyte Growth)
                            ▼
      Progressive Bony Fusion of Spine (Ankylosis)
```

## 🔍 Clinical Manifestations

### Symptoms
* **Inflammatory Back Pain** (The hallmark presentation):
  * **Insidious onset** (developing over months) in patients **$<40$ years old**.
  * Pain and stiffness are **worse in the morning** (lasting $>30-60$ minutes) or after periods of inactivity.
  * Pain **improves with physical activity** or exercise and is **not relieved by rest**.
  * Pain typically awakens the patient during the second half of the night, prompting them to walk around to relieve stiffness.
* **Peripheral Arthritis**: Occurs in $30-50\%$ of patients, typically asymmetric oligoarthritis of the lower limbs (hip and shoulder involvement can be severe and disabling).

### Physical Examination
* **Sacroiliac Joint Tenderness**: Pain elicited by direct pressure over the SI joints or by provocative maneuvers (e.g., Gaenslen's test, FABER test).
* **Limited Lumbar Flexion (Modified Schober's Test)**:
  * Measures the range of motion of the lumbar spine.
  * *Method*: Mark L5 spinous process and a point 10 cm above. With the patient bending forward maximally, the distance should increase from 10 cm to **$>15\text{ cm}$** (an increase of $>5\text{ cm}$ is normal). An increase of $<5\text{ cm}$ indicates restricted lumbar mobility.
* **Chest Expansion Limitation**: Measurement of chest expansion at the 4th intercostal space during maximal inspiration and expiration. Normal is $>5\text{ cm}$. Restricted expansion ($<2.5\text{ cm}$) indicates costovertebral joint involvement.
* **Postural Changes**: Loss of lumbar lordosis, flattening of the buttocks, and progressive thoracic kyphosis ("stooped" or **"question mark" posture**).
* **Tragus-to-Wall Distance**: In advanced disease, thoracic kyphosis prevents the patient from touching the back of their head to the wall when standing straight.

### Extra-articular Manifestations
* **Acute Anterior Uveitis**: The most common extra-articular feature ($25-30\%$). Presents as acute, unilateral, painful red eye with photophobia and ciliary flush.
* **Cardiovascular**: Aortitis leading to **aortic regurgitation** (insufficiency) and cardiac conduction abnormalities (e.g., first-degree AV block).
* **Pulmonary**: Restrictive lung disease secondary to costovertebral joint fusion. In late stages, **apical fibrotic lung disease** (can be colonized by *Aspergillus*).
* **Renal**: Secondary **AA amyloidosis** (rare) and IgA nephropathy.

---

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis
* **Mechanical Back Pain**: Acute onset, worsens with activity, **improves with rest**, no morning stiffness, no systemic features.
* **Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH)**: Occurs in older males (typically $>50-60$). Characterized by flowing ossification of the anterior longitudinal ligament of at least 4 contiguous vertebrae. **Spares the sacroiliac joints** and does not have HLA-B27 association.
* **Osteitis Condensans Ilii**: Benign, asymptomatic sclerosis of the lower iliac side of the sacroiliac joint, typically seen in multiparous females. Normal joint space.
* **Cervical / Lumbar Spondylosis**: Age-related degenerative disc disease, worse with activity.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

### Imaging (Diagnostic Cornerstone)
* **Plain Radiography of Sacroiliac Joints**:
  * **Sacroiliitis**: Bilateral and symmetric (hallmark of AS). Findings include joint space widening (early erosions), subchondral sclerosis, and eventual complete joint fusion (ankylosis).
* **Plain Radiography of Spine**:
  * **Romanus Lesions**: "Shiny corners" of vertebral bodies due to early entheseal inflammation and erosion.
  * **Squaring of Vertebrae**: Loss of normal anterior concavity of the vertebral bodies.
  * **Syndesmophytes**: Vertical bony bridging between adjacent vertebrae.
  * **Bamboo Spine**: Late finding showing diffuse spinal ankylosis and ossification of spinal ligaments.
* **MRI (SI Joints / Spine)**:
  * The most sensitive imaging modality for early disease. Detects **active sacroiliitis** (evidenced by **bone marrow edema** on T2/STIR sequences) years before structural changes are visible on plain X-rays (non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis).

### Laboratory
* **HLA-B27 testing**: Highly supportive if positive, but not diagnostic on its own.
* **Inflammatory Markers**: ESR and CRP are elevated in only $50-70\%$ of patients with active disease.
* **Serology**: Rheumatoid Factor (RF) and Anti-CCP are **negative**.

---

## Diagnostic Criteria
### Modified New York Criteria (Requires radiographic sacroiliitis + $\ge 1$ clinical criteria)
* **Clinical Criteria**:
  1. Low back pain and stiffness for $>3$ months, improving with exercise but not relieved by rest.
  2. Limitation of lumbar spine motion in both sagittal and frontal planes.
  3. Limitation of chest expansion relative to normal values for age and sex.
* **Radiographic Criteria**:
  * Bilateral sacroiliitis grade $\ge 2$ or unilateral sacroiliitis grade $3-4$.

---

## 🏥 Management

### Initial Management
* **Physical Therapy & Exercise (Critical)**: Daily spinal extension exercises, swimming, and posture training to maintain spinal mobility and chest expansion.
* **Pharmacotherapy (First-line)**:
  * **Continuous NSAID therapy** (e.g., **Naproxen 500 mg PO bid**, **Celecoxib 200 mg PO daily/bid**, or **Indomethacin 50 mg PO tid**). NSAIDs not only control symptoms but also slow radiographic spinal progression.

### Definitive Management (For patients refractory to NSAIDs)
* **Biologic Therapy (First-line for axial disease)**:
  * **TNF-$\alpha$ Inhibitors** (e.g., **Adalimumab**, **Etanercept**, **Infliximab**): Highly effective for spinal pain, stiffness, and extra-articular manifestations like uveitis.
  * **IL-17 Inhibitors** (e.g., **Secukinumab**): Alternative first-line biologic if TNF inhibitors are contraindicated or ineffective.
* **Conventional DMARDs** (e.g., **Sulfasalazine**):
  * **Ineffective for axial spinal disease.** Only used for patients with active *peripheral* arthritis (e.g., knees, ankles) associated with AS.
* **Surgery**: Total hip arthroplasty for severe hip disease.

---

## 🫁 Complications
* **Spinal Fractures**: The fused spine acts like a rigid "long bone." Minor trauma can lead to unstable fractures (most commonly cervical C5-C7), risking spinal cord transection.
* **Cauda Equina Syndrome**: Chronic arachnoiditis leading to bladder/bowel incontinence and saddle anesthesia.
* **Restrictive Lung Disease**: Secondary to chest wall fusion.
* **Aortic Valve Disease**: Progressing to heart failure.

---

## Prognosis
* Variable course; most patients maintain good functional status and can continue working.
* Poorer prognosis is associated with early hip disease, cigarette smoking, and elevated CRP.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

* > [!IMPORTANT]
  > **Schober's Test Calculation**: Make sure to mark exactly L5 (at the level of the dimples of Venus) and 10 cm above it while the patient is standing. When bending forward, the distance MUST increase by **at least 5 cm** (to a total of $\ge 15\text{ cm}$). Any increase $< 5\text{ cm}$ is abnormal and indicates lumbar spine rigidity.
* > [!WARNING]
  > **Spinal Trauma Alert**: In any patient with advanced Ankylosing Spondylitis who presents after even a minor fall or trauma (e.g., sudden deceleration in a car), **always obtain a CT or MRI of the spine**. Normal X-rays can easily miss unstable fractures in a fused spine, which can lead to fatal cervical cord compression if not immobilized.
