## type: disease

# Definition
Pseudogout, clinically referred to as **Acute Calcium Pyrophosphate (CPP) Crystal Arthritis**, is an inflammatory arthritis caused by the deposition of **calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals** in articular cartilage and surrounding joint tissues. It belongs to the spectrum of CPPD diseases, which also includes asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis and chronic CPPD arthropathy.

# Epidemiology
* **Prevalence**: Strongly associated with aging. Extremely rare before age 50; prevalence rises to over $30\%$ in individuals $>80$ years of age.
* **Gender Ratio**: Equal distribution ($1:1$ male-to-female ratio).
* **Risk Factors**: Advanced age, joint trauma, osteoarthritis, joint instability, and genetic mutations (e.g., *ANKH* gene).

# Etiology & Associated Metabolic Disorders
Most cases in elderly patients are idiopathic. However, in patients presenting with CPPD **under 50 years of age**, a secondary metabolic etiology must be suspected:
* **Primary Hyperparathyroidism**: Chronic hypercalcemia promotes CPP crystal formation.
* **Hemochromatosis**: Iron deposits inhibit pyrophosphatase, reducing CPP breakdown. Typically presents with CPPD in the 2nd and 3rd MCP joints ("bronze diabetes" hand).
* **Hypomagnesemia**: Magnesium is a cofactor for alkaline phosphatase, which breaks down pyrophosphate. Low magnesium increases CPP precipitation (e.g., Gitelman's syndrome, chronic diuretic use).
* **Hypothyroidism**.
* **Hypophosphatasia** / Wilson's Disease.

# Pathophysiology
1. **Pyrophosphate Accumulation**: Aging or metabolic changes alter chondrocyte metabolism, leading to increased extracellular levels of **inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi)**.
2. **Crystal Formation**: PPi combines with calcium in the extracellular matrix of cartilage to form **calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals** (this cartilage calcification is called **chondrocalcinosis**).
3. **Crystal Shedding**: Physical trauma, joint stress, or systemic medical illness triggers shedding of CPPD crystals from the cartilage into the joint space.
4. **Inflammatory Activation**: Synovial macrophages phagocytose the free crystals, activating the **NLRP3 inflammasome** and releasing **IL-1$\beta$**, which recruits neutrophils and generates acute inflammatory synovitis.

# Clinical Manifestations

## 1. Acute CPP Crystal Arthritis ("Pseudogout")
* **Onset**: Acute, inflammatory flare mimicking gout, but typically less rapid, taking $24-48$ hours to reach peak intensity.
* **Localization**: **Mono- or oligoarticular**. The **knee** is the most commonly involved joint ($>50\%$ of cases), followed by the **wrist**, shoulder, ankle, and elbow.
* **Features**: Warmth, swelling, erythema, and severe pain. Flares are self-limiting but can last longer than gout (typically 1 to 3 weeks if untreated).
* **Triggers**: Parathyroidectomy or joint surgery, acute medical illness (e.g., pneumonia, myocardial infarction), or joint trauma.

## 2. Chronic CPPD Arthropathy
* **Osteoarthritis with CPPD**: Mimics standard OA but exhibits an **atypical joint distribution**, such as involvement of the **radiocarpal joint** (wrist), **glenohumeral joint** (shoulder), or **2nd and 3rd MCP joints** (hands).
* **Pseudo-Rheumatoid Arthritis**: About $5\%$ of cases present with chronic, symmetric polyarthritis, morning stiffness, and elevated inflammatory markers, mimicking RA.

# Differential Diagnosis
* **[[Gout]]**: Differentiated by podagra (1st MTP involvement), needle-shaped, negatively birefringent crystals, and erosive "rat-bite" X-ray changes.
* **[[Infectious Arthritis]] (Septic Arthritis)**: The most critical diagnosis to rule out in any acute monoarthritis. Joint aspiration is mandatory; septic joints will show positive cultures/Gram stains and higher WBC counts.
* **[[Rheumatoid Arthritis]]**: Differentiated by symmetric small joint involvement (MCP, PIP), RF/Anti-CCP seropositivity, and lack of chondrocalcinosis.
* **Standard Osteoarthritis**: Involves weight-bearing joints (knees, hips) and DIPs (Heberden's nodes). Radiocarpal or isolated MCP involvement is highly atypical for standard OA and suggests CPPD.

# Investigations

## 🔬 Synovial Fluid Analysis (Diagnostic Gold Standard)
**Must be performed to confirm CPPD crystals and rule out septic arthritis.**
* **Polarized Light Microscopy**: Demonstrates **rhomboid- or rod-shaped, weakly positively birefringent** crystals.
* **Birefringence Rule**: Under a red compensator filter, crystals oriented **parallel** to the slow ray appear **blue**, while those **perpendicular** appear **yellow**.
* **WBC Count**: Typically $2,000 - 50,000/\mu\text{L}$ with $>75\%$ PMNs (inflammatory profile).

## Imaging
* **Plain Radiography**:
  * **Chondrocalcinosis**: The hallmark radiographic finding. Appears as **thin, linear, radiodense calcifications** within fibrocartilage or hyaline articular cartilage.
  * *Classic sites*: **Knee meniscus**, **triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) of the wrist**, and the **pubic symphysis**.
  * Advanced disease shows joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, and large subchondral cysts.

## Laboratory
* **Routine Labs**: ESR and CRP are elevated during acute flares.
* **Metabolic Screening**: **Mandatory for patients $<50$ years old** presenting with CPPD:
  * **Serum Calcium & Parathyroid Hormone (PTH)** (to rule out hyperparathyroidism).
  * **Serum Ferritin & Iron Saturation** (to rule out hemochromatosis).
  * **Serum Magnesium** (to rule out hypomagnesemia).
  * **TSH** (to rule out hypothyroidism).
  * **Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)** (to rule out hypophosphatasia).

# Management

## 1. Treatment of Acute Flare (Anti-inflammatory)
* **First-line Therapy (NSAIDs)**: High-dose oral NSAIDs (e.g., Naproxen, Indomethacin, Celecoxib) provide rapid relief.
* **Colchicine**: Oral Colchicine (1.2 mg followed by 0.6 mg 1 hour later, then 0.6 mg daily or bid) is effective, similar to its use in acute gout.
* **Corticosteroids**:
  * **Intra-articular Triamcinolone Injection**: Highly effective and preferred if the flare is limited to a single large joint (e.g., knee) once septic arthritis is excluded.
  * **Oral Prednisolone**: 30 mg daily tapered over 5-7 days. Indicated for patients with multiple active joints or those who cannot take NSAIDs or Colchicine (e.g., severe CKD).

## 2. Chronic Prophylaxis (For recurrent flares $\ge 3$ per year)
* **Low-dose Colchicine**: Colchicine 0.6 mg PO once or twice daily reduces the frequency of recurrent acute flares.
* **No Crystal-Dissolving Therapy**: Unlike gout, there are no pharmacological agents capable of dissolving or clearing CPPD crystals from cartilage.

## 3. Manage Underlying Disorders
* Treating hyperparathyroidism (e.g., parathyroidectomy) or hemochromatosis (e.g., phlebotomy) is critical to prevent systemic complications, although it does not reverse existing chondrocalcinosis or joint damage.

---

# Complications
* **Destructive Arthropathy**: Severe osteolysis, subchondral cyst formation, and rapid joint space narrowing, mimicking neuropathic Charcot joints.
* **Spinal Cord Compression**: Rare cases of CPP deposition in the ligamentum flavum or transverse ligament of the atlas (Crowned Dens Syndrome) can cause severe neck pain and cervical myelopathy.

# Clinical Pearls
> [!IMPORTANT]
  > **The Under-50 Rule**: While CPPD is a normal age-related degenerative change in an 80-year-old, finding chondrocalcinosis or acute pseudogout in a **40-year-old patient is highly abnormal**. You must perform a full metabolic screening for hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, and hypothyroidism.

>[!TIP]
  > **The TFCC Sign**: In patients presenting with acute wrist pain, a simple wrist X-ray showing calcification of the **triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC)** (a linear density in the space between the ulna and carpal bones) is highly diagnostic of CPPD and immediately differentiates it from gout.
