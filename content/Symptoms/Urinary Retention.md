# 🚽 Urinary Retention (ปัสสาวะคั่ง)

> "Urinary retention is the inability to voluntarily void urine. It is classified as acute (sudden, painful inability to pass urine with bladder volume $>500$ mL) or chronic (painless, gradual retention associated with high post-void residuals and overflow incontinence)."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Action:**
> 1. **Confirm Retention & Bladder Volume**: Palpate the suprapubic area. Perform a bedside bladder scan (if volume $>300-500$ mL, proceed to catheterization).
> 2. **Emergency Catheterization**:
>    - Perform sterile urethral catheterization with a standard Foley catheter.
>    - *If resistance is met (BPH)*: Do not force. Try a **Coudé tip catheter** or consult urology.
>    - *If catheterization fails*: Urology consult for suprapubic cystostomy.
> 3. **Monitor Post-Obstructive Diuresis (POD)**:
>    - If initial drainage is $>1000$ mL, check urine output hourly.
>    - Look for polyuria ($>200$ mL/h for 2 consecutive hours) and monitor electrolytes.

---

## 🔍 Pathophysiology & Etiology

Urinary retention results from either **outflow obstruction** (increased resistance), **detrusor muscle underactivity** (impaired contractility), or **neurological disruption** of the micturition reflex.

```
Urinary Retention Pathophysiology
└── 1. Outflow Obstruction (Mechanical)
    ├── Prostatic: BPH, Prostate Cancer
    ├── Urethral: Strictures, Blood Clots, Stones
    └── Extra-urinary: Fecal Impaction, Pelvic Masses
└── 2. Detrusor Underactivity (Neurological/Myogenic)
    ├── Spinal Cord Injury / Cauda Equina
    ├── Diabetic Neurogenic Bladder
    └── Anticholinergic Medication Blockade
```

## 📊 Acute vs. Chronic Urinary Retention

| Feature | Acute Urinary Retention (AUR) | Chronic Urinary Retention (CUR) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Onset** | Sudden, agonizingly painful | Gradual, painless, or mild discomfort |
| **Bladder Palpation** | Palpable, tender, suprapubic mass | Palpable, non-tender suprapubic mass |
| **Bladder Volume** | Typically $500 - 1000$ mL | Often $> 1000 - 1500$ mL |
| **Associated Symptoms** | Severe suprapubic distress, agitation | **Overflow Incontinence** (dribbling), frequency |
| **Renal Impairment** | Rare, unless superimposed on CKD | Common (obstructive uropathy, hydronephrosis) |
| **Immediate Risk** | Bladder rupture (rare), autonomic response | Post-obstructive diuresis, renal failure, uremia |

---

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

* **Onset and Pain**: Is there sudden agonizing pain (AUR) or chronic painless overflow (CUR)?
* **LUTS (Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms)**: Hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, straining to void (suggests mechanical obstruction).
* **Medication History**:
  * *Anticholinergics* (atropine, ipratropium) and *Antihistamines* (diphenhydramine).
  * *Tricyclic Antidepressants* (amitriptyline) and *Opioids* (morphine, fentanyl).
  * *Sympathomimetics* (pseudoephedrine - increases bladder outlet tone).
* **Neurological Cues**: History of spinal trauma, back pain, limb weakness, or chronic diabetes mellitus.
* **Red Flags for Spinal Compression**: History of malignancy, lower extremity weakness, numbness, or fecal incontinence.

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Abdominal Examination**: Palpate and percuss for a **dull, dome-shaped suprapubic mass** rising from the pelvis.
* **Digital Rectal Examination (DRE)**:
  * In men: Assess prostate size, consistency (smooth/rubbery in BPH vs. hard/nodular in prostate cancer), and check rectal sphincter tone.
  * In both sexes: Rule out severe fecal impaction compressing the bladder neck.
* **Neurological Examination**:
  * Assess sacral dermatomes (**S2-S4**) for saddle anesthesia.
  * Check **Anal Wink Reflex** and **Bulbocavernosus Reflex** (loss indicates neurogenic bladder).
  * Evaluate lower extremity reflexes, motor strength, and tone.

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Common Causes | Dangerous / Can't Miss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mechanical Obstruction** | • **Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)**<br>• Fecal impaction<br>• Pelvic organ prolapse (cystocele) | • **Prostate Malignancy**<br>• Bladder neck / Urethral cancer<br>• **Clot Retention** (hematuria)<br>• Impacted [[Urinary Stone Symptoms]] |
| **Neurological** | • Diabetic neuropathy (atonic bladder)<br>• Post-operative (spinal anesthesia) | • **Cauda Equina Syndrome**<br>• **Acute Spinal Cord Compression** (trauma, epidural abscess) |
| **Pharmacological** | • Anticholinergics, Antihistamines<br>• Opioids, NSAIDs, CCBs | • Anticholinergic toxicity / overdose |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Immediate neurological decompression or emergency surgery is required if:**
> * Retention is accompanied by **saddle anesthesia** or new **bilateral lower limb weakness** (suggests [[Cauda Equina Syndrome]] or spinal cord compression).
> * Retention in the setting of trauma or suspected spinal epidural abscess (fever, localized spine tenderness).
> * Retaining urine with severe hematuria and passage of blood clots (leads to bladder tamponade; requires three-way Foley for continuous bladder irrigation).

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Bedside Evaluation**:
  * **Bladder Ultrasound / Scan**: Quick estimation of urinary volume ($>300$ mL confirms retention).
* **Laboratory Investigations**:
  * **[[Urinalysis]] & Urine Culture**: Screen for infection (UTI) or hematuria.
  * **Renal Function Tests (BUN, Creatinine)**: Assess for post-renal [[Acute Kidney Injury]] (AKI).
  * **Serum Electrolytes**: Check for hyperkalemia and hyponatremia, especially if post-obstructive diuresis is suspected.
* **Imaging**:
  * **Renal Ultrasound**: Indicated in chronic retention to evaluate for bilateral hydronephrosis and thin renal cortex (signs of chronic obstructive uropathy).

---

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Immediate Decompression**:
   - Insert a standard 14F-16F Foley catheter.
   - If obstruction from BPH prevents entry, try a **Coudé catheter** (angled tip to slide over the prostatic lobe).
   - *Decompression Speed Warning*: Rapid decompression is safe. Historically, clamping at every 500 mL was recommended to avoid hematuria *ex vacuo* or transient hypotension, but evidence shows complete rapid emptying is safe and reduces infection risk.
2. **Post-Obstructive Diuresis (POD) Monitoring**:
   - Defined as urine output **$> 200$ mL/h for 2 consecutive hours** or **$> 3$ L/day** after decompression.
   - *Management*:
     - Measure vitals, intake/output, and electrolytes every 4-6 hours.
     - Provide IV fluids only if the patient is hypotensive, tachycardic, or has severe electrolyte disturbances. Use **0.45% NaCl** (since post-obstructive urine is hypotonic) to replace **50% of the hourly output**. Avoid over-replacement, which prolongs the diuresis.
3. **Medical Therapy**:
   - Start an **alpha-1 blocker** (e.g., Tamsulosin 0.4$ mg PO daily) to relax the prostatic smooth muscle, optimizing success for a Trial Without Catheter (TWOC) in 3-5 days.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **The Trial Without Catheter (TWOC) Rule**: Before removing the Foley catheter in a patient with AUR secondary to BPH, make sure they have been on an alpha-blocker (Tamsulosin) for at least 3 days. This significantly increases the success rate of voiding independently on catheter removal.
> **Ex Vacuo Hematuria**: Sudden decompression of a chronically distended bladder can cause transient bleeding (hematuria *ex vacuo*) due to the sudden relief of transmural pressure on mucosal capillaries. It is usually self-limiting and does not require active intervention unless blood clots form.
