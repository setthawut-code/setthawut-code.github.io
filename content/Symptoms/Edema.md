# 💧 Edema (บวม)

> "Edema represents an expansion of the interstitial fluid volume. The clinical approach hinges on distinguishing localized/unilateral from generalized/bilateral edema."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Approach:**
> 1. **Unilateral vs. Bilateral**:
>    - *Unilateral*: Local vascular or lymphatic problem. **Rule out DVT immediately.**
>    - *Bilateral*: Systemic fluid retention. Evaluate the "Big 3" organs: **Heart**, **Kidney**, **Liver**.
> 2. **Pitting vs. Non-pitting**: Pitting is typical for systemic failures; non-pitting suggests lymphedema or myxedema.
> 3. **Rule Out Pulmonary Congestion**: Auscultate the lungs for crackles, check $SpO_2$.

---

## 🔍 Definition & Physiology

Edema is the accumulation of fluid in the interstitial space. It becomes clinically apparent when the interstitial volume increases by **$>2.5-3.0$ liters**.

### Starling Forces Framework
Fluid movement between intravascular and interstitial spaces is governed by Starling forces:
1. **Increased Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure**:
   - Systemic volume overload (e.g., [[Heart Failure]], CKD/AKI).
   - Local venous obstruction (e.g., DVT, chronic venous insufficiency).
2. **Decreased Capillary Oncotic Pressure** (Hypoalbuminemia):
   - Decreased synthesis (e.g., [[Cirrhosis]], malnutrition).
   - Increased loss (e.g., [[Nephrotic Syndrome]], protein-losing enteropathy).
3. **Increased Capillary Permeability**:
   - Inflammation, infection ([[Sepsis]]), burns, anaphylaxis, ARDS.
4. **Lymphatic Obstruction**:
   - Lymphedema (malignancy, surgery/radiation, filariasis).

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

### ⏳ Clinical Clues & Drug Causes
* **Onset & Location**:
  * *Sudden onset, unilateral leg swelling* $\rightarrow$ Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or cellulitis.
  * *Gradual bilateral swelling, worse at end of day* $\rightarrow$ Chronic venous insufficiency or early heart failure.
  * *Facial/Periorbital edema (prominent in morning)* $\rightarrow$ Renal disease ([[Nephrotic Syndrome]]).
* **Systemic Symptoms**:
  * Dyspnea, orthopnea, PND $\rightarrow$ [[Heart Failure]].
  * Abdominal distension $\rightarrow$ [[Cirrhosis]] (with [[Ascites]]) or nephrotic syndrome.
  * Foamy urine $\rightarrow$ Significant proteinuria ([[Nephrotic Syndrome]]).
  * Cold intolerance, dry skin, weight gain $\rightarrow$ Hypothyroidism (myxedema).
* **Medication History (High-Yield)**:
  * **Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)** (especially dihydropyridines: Amlodipine): Induces precapillary vasodilation, increasing hydrostatic pressure.
  * **NSAIDs**: Promotes renal afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction $\rightarrow$ sodium and water retention.
  * **Systemic Corticosteroids**: Direct mineralocorticoid effect causing sodium retention.
  * **Thiazolidinediones (TZDs)** (Pioglitazone): Increases sodium reabsorption in renal collecting ducts.

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Pitting vs. Non-pitting**:
  * Apply firm pressure with thumb over a bony prominence (e.g., medial malleolus, anterior tibia) for 5 seconds.
  * *Pitting*: Indent remains. Typical of systemic heart, liver, or kidney dysfunction.
  * *Non-pitting*: Skin bounces back. Suggests lymphedema or myxedema (deposition of mucopolysaccharides).
* **Edema Distribution**:
  * Check **sacral area** in bedridden/supine patients.
  * *Bilateral pedal edema*: Heart, kidney, liver disease.
  * *Anasarca* (generalized severe edema): Nephrotic syndrome, end-stage cirrhosis, severe heart failure.
* **Organ-Specific Signs**:
  * **Jugular Venous Distension (JVD) & S3 gallop** $\rightarrow$ Right-sided or biventricular [[Heart Failure]].
  * **Pulmonary Crackles** $\rightarrow$ Left-sided heart failure / pulmonary edema.
  * **Ascites, Spider Angiomas, Palmar Erythema** $\rightarrow$ Portal hypertension/[[Cirrhosis]].
  * **Periorbital Edema** $\rightarrow$ Highly suggestive of renal etiology, especially in children/young adults.
  * **Unilateral Leg Signs**: Warmth, erythema, tenderness $\rightarrow$ Cellulitis or DVT. Homan's sign is unreliable.

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

### 📊 Localized vs. Generalized Matrix

| Type | Pathophysiology | Key Etiologies | Diagnostic Clues |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Unilateral** | Local venous or lymphatic pathology | • DVT<br>• Cellulitis<br>• Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI)<br>• Lymphedema | Asymmetric calf diameter ($>3$ cm difference), local erythema/warmth, history of pelvic surgery/lymphadenectomy. |
| **Bilateral** | Systemic fluid retention or hypoalbuminemia | • [[Heart Failure]]<br>• CKD / AKI<br>• [[Nephrotic Syndrome]]<br>• [[Cirrhosis]]<br>• Medication-induced | Symmetrical pitting edema, JVD, lung crackles, ascites, elevated creatinine, hypoalbuminemia ($<3.0$ g/dL), proteinuria. |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Immediate emergency intervention required if present:**
> * Sudden onset of dyspnea, tachypnea, or chest pain in a patient with edema ([[Acute Pulmonary Edema]] or [[Pulmonary Embolism]]).
> * Sudden unilateral leg pain and swelling extending to thigh (massive DVT/phlegmasia cerulea dolens).
> * Facial/airway swelling, stridor, or wheezing (angioedema/anaphylaxis).
> * Anuria (no urine output for $>12$ hours) or profound oliguria.
> * Signs of sepsis (fever, hypotension, altered mental status) in a patient with severe cellulitis.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **First-line Labs**:
  * **[[Urinalysis]]**: Look for proteinuria (proteinuria $>3.5$ g/day suggests [[Nephrotic Syndrome]]) and active casts.
  * **Renal Function Tests**: Serum Creatinine, BUN (evaluates for AKI/CKD).
  * **Serum Albumin**: Low level ($<3.0$ g/dL) suggests hepatic failure, nephrosis, or malnutrition.
  * **Liver Function Tests**: Check for chronic liver disease markers.
  * **Thyroid Function Tests (TSH/Free T4)**: To rule out myxedema in hypothyroidism.
  * **BNP or NT-proBNP**: If heart failure is clinically suspected.
* **Imaging & Diagnostic Modalities**:
  * **[[Chest X-Ray]]**: Assess for cardiomegaly, cephalization of pulmonary vessels, pleural effusion, or Kerley B lines (pulmonary congestion).
  * **Venous Duplex Ultrasound**: Mandatory if unilateral leg swelling to confirm/rule out DVT.
  * **Echocardiography**: Evaluate left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), diastolic function, and right ventricular pressure.

---

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Dietary Modification**:
   - Limit sodium intake ($<2$ g/day) and fluid intake ($<1.5-2.0$ L/day) in hypervolemic states (HF, renal failure, cirrhosis).
2. **Pharmacological Therapy (Diuretics)**:
   - **Loop Diuretics** (e.g., Furosemide $20-80$ mg IV/PO): First-line therapy to promote natriuresis and fluid clearance.
   - **Thiazide Diuretics** (e.g., Metolazone or Hydrochlorothiazide): Can be added synergistically to loop diuretics in refractory edema (sequential nephron blockade).
   - **Aldosterone Antagonists** (e.g., Spironolactone $25-100$ mg PO): Key agent in [[Cirrhosis]] with ascites (target ratio 100 mg Spironolactone to 40 mg Furosemide).
3. **Compression & Position**:
   - Leg elevation (above heart level) and compression stockings (20-30 mmHg) are primary treatments for chronic venous insufficiency. *Avoid compression stockings in active DVT or severe peripheral arterial disease.*

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> * **Diuretic Resistance in Nephrotic Syndrome**: In nephrotic syndrome, loop diuretics (which are highly protein-bound) cannot be delivered effectively to the loop of Henle because they bind to intratubular albumin. Higher doses of loop diuretics or co-administration with IV albumin may be required.
> * **CCB-Induced Edema Management**: CCB-induced edema is **not** caused by volume overload, but rather precapillary dilation. Therefore, it does **not** respond well to loop diuretics. The best management is to reduce the dose, switch to another antihypertensive class, or add an ACE inhibitor/ARB (which causes postcapillary venodilation, balancing hydrostatic pressures).
> * **The Sacral Edema Trap**: In bedbound or paralyzed patients, edema does not collect in the legs due to lack of gravity. Always palpate the sacral and presacral areas to detect edema.
