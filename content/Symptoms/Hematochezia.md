# 🩸 Hematochezia (ถ่ายเป็นเลือด)

> "Hematochezia is the passage of bright red or maroon blood per rectum. It usually represents Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding (LGIB), but a massive Upper GI bleed must be ruled out in hemodynamically unstable patients."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Approach:**
> 1. **Rule Out Massive Upper GI Bleeding**: If the patient has hematochezia **AND** hemodynamic instability (shock), perform an **EGD** or pass an **NG tube** immediately. A rapid transit of massive UGIB can present as red blood per rectum.
> 2. **Establish Hemodynamics**: LGIB is often self-limiting, but requires immediate IV access and volume resuscitation if unstable.
> 3. **Characterize the Bleeding**:
>    - *Bright red blood coating stool or dripping into bowl*: Think anorectal (Hemorrhoids, Fissures).
>    - *Blood mixed with stool + weight loss / bowel habit change*: Think **Colorectal Cancer** or IBD.
>    - *Painless massive maroon/red blood*: Think **Diverticular bleeding** or Angiodysplasia.

---

## 🔍 Definition & Physiology

* **Hematochezia**: Emission of bright red blood, maroon blood, or blood clots per rectum.
* **Anatomical Origin**: Typically originates from the lower GI tract (colon, rectum, anus), distal to the ligament of Treitz.
* **The Transit Exception**: In $\sim 10-15\%$ of patients presenting with hematochezia, the source is actually a massive upper GI bleed (PUD or varices) with rapid transit ($<1-2$ hours) through the intestines.

---

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

### ⏳ Stool Blood Presentation & Localization
> [!TIP]
> **The appearance of blood and its relationship to stool localizes the lesion:**

| Presentation | Suspected Source | Common Etiologies |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bright red, separate from stool** (streaks on paper, dripping into toilet bowl) | Anal canal / Distal rectum | Internal/external hemorrhoids, anal fissure. |
| **Bright red/maroon, mixed with stool** | Colon / Rectum | Colorectal cancer, IBD, infectious colitis, polyps. |
| **Maroon or dark red, large volume** | Right colon / Small bowel | Diverticular bleeding, angiodysplasia. |
| **Maroon / hematochezia + Shock** | Upper GI tract (massive) | Ruptured varices, bleeding duodenal ulcer. |

* **Pain vs. Painless**:
  * *Sharp pain during defecation* $\rightarrow$ Anal fissure.
  * *Painless large-volume bleeding* $\rightarrow$ Diverticular bleeding, angiodysplasia, or internal hemorrhoids.
  * *Abdominal pain, cramping, fever* $\rightarrow$ Infectious colitis, ischemic colitis, or IBD.
* **Associated Symptoms**: Weight loss, change in stool caliber (pencil-thin) $\rightarrow$ Colorectal cancer.

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Vital Signs**: Assess for orthostatic changes or resting hypotension/tachycardia ([[Shock]]). *A SBP $<90$ mmHg in hematochezia suggests a massive bleed (either massive diverticular/variceal or massive UGIB).*
* **Abdominal Examination**: Look for surgical scars. Palpate for tenderness (colitis, diverticulitis) or masses.
* **Anorectal & Digital Rectal Examination (DRE) (Mandatory)**:
  * *Inspection*: Part the buttocks to look for external hemorrhoids, anal fissures (most common at posterior midline), skin tags, fistulas, or rectal prolapse.
  * *Palpation*: Assess for rectal masses, internal hemorrhoids (often soft and difficult to palpate unless thrombosed), and sphincter tone.
  * *Glove inspection*: Verify color of blood (bright red vs. maroon).

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Origin | Common / Self-Limiting | Dangerous / Can't Miss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Anorectal** | • Internal / External Hemorrhoids<br>• Anal Fissure | • Rectal cancer / Anal cancer |
| **Colonic** | • Diverticular bleeding (usually resolves spontaneously)<br>• Angiodysplasia (vascular ectasia)<br>• Colonic polyps | • **Colorectal Cancer (CRC)**<br>• **Ischemic Colitis** (presents with pain)<br>• Severe IBD flare (Toxic megacolon)<br>• Infectious colitis / pseudomembranous |
| **Upper GI** | None | • **Massive Bleeding Peptic Ulcer** (duodenal)<br>• **Aortoenteric Fistula** |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Urgent resuscitation and emergency investigation required if present:**
> * Hemodynamic instability (shock) or orthostatic syncope.
> * Hematochezia in a patient with a history of abdominal aortic graft (aortoenteric fistula).
> * Severe abdominal pain, guarding, or rebound tenderness (ischemic bowel or perforation).
> * Hematochezia accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, and signs of sepsis.
> * Documented weight loss, cachexia, or change in bowel habits (suggests malignancy).

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **First-line Screening**:
  * **[[CBC]]**: Monitor hemoglobin levels. (Note: initial Hb can be falsely normal in hyperacute bleeding).
  * **Coagulation Profile (PT/INR, aPTT)**: Crucial in patients on anticoagulants or with liver disease.
  * **BUN-to-Creatinine Ratio**: If ratio is elevated ($>30:1$), suspect massive **Upper GI bleed** rather than lower GI bleed.
  * **Type & Screen / Crossmatch**.
* **Diagnostic & Therapeutic Modalities**:
  * **Colonoscopy**: Diagnostic standard. Ideally performed within 24 hours of admission after rapid bowel purge (PEG lavage). Allows for therapy (coagulation, epinephrine, clips).
  * **Anoscopy / Flexible Sigmoidoscopy**: If history suggests benign distal anorectal pathology (hemorrhoids, fissure) in a young patient ($<45$ years) without red flags.
  * **CT Angiography (CTA) / Catheter Angiography**: Indicated in hemodynamically unstable patients with active bleeding where colonoscopy cannot be performed due to poor visualization. Can localize bleed and perform embolization.
  * **EGD (Esophagogastroduodenoscopy)**: Perform first if the patient has hematochezia with hemodynamic instability (suspected massive UGIB).

---

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Hemodynamic Resuscitation**:
   - Establish two large-bore IVs. Aggressive crystalloid fluid resuscitation.
   - Transfusion target: **$Hb \ge 7$ g/dL** (restrictive strategy).
2. **Diverticular Bleeding**:
   - $70-80\%$ of cases resolve spontaneously. Monitor hemodynamics.
   - If bleeding continues: Endoscopic clip application, epinephrine injection, or angiographic embolization. Segmental colectomy is reserved for refractory bleeding.
3. **Anorectal Bleeding**:
   - Hemorrhoids: Fiber supplementation, stool softeners, topical steroids. Refractory: rubber band ligation, hemorrhoidectomy.
   - Anal Fissure: Stool softeners, topical nitroglycerin or diltiazem ointment (to relax internal sphincter).
4. **Ischemic Colitis**:
   - Bowel rest (NPO), IV fluids to maintain bowel perfusion. Avoid vasopressors if possible. Most cases resolve with supportive care unless gangrene/perforation develops (requires surgery).

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> * **The Hematochezia Shock Rule**: If a patient presents with hematochezia and is in shock (tachycardia, SBP $<90$ mmHg), assume it is a **massive upper GI bleed** (e.g., bleeding duodenal ulcer) until proven otherwise. Pass an NG tube or perform an emergent EGD before colonoscopy.
> * **Diverticula Bleed is Painless**: Diverticular bleeding is caused by erosion of the vasa recta over a diverticular dome. It is **painless** and presents as sudden, large-volume red/maroon blood. In contrast, **Diverticulitis** (inflammation of diverticula) causes severe LLQ pain and fever, but rarely causes bleeding.
> * **Ischemic Colitis Location**: Ischemic colitis classically affects the **"watershed areas"** of the colon: the splenic flexure (Griffiths' point - SMA/IMA border) and the rectosigmoid junction (Sudek's point - IMA/internal iliac border). It presents as sudden abdominal pain followed by self-limiting bloody diarrhea.
