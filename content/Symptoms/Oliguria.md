# 💧 Oliguria (ปัสสาวะน้อย)

> "Oliguria is defined as urine output $< 400$ mL/day (or $< 0.5$ mL/kg/h for adults for $> 6$ hours). Anuria is defined as $< 100$ mL/day. It is a critical warning sign of impending [[Acute Kidney Injury]] (AKI) and requires rapid systemic evaluation."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Action:**
> 1. **Confirm Oliguria is Real**: Double-check fluid intake/output charts. If the patient has a Foley catheter, **flush the catheter** first to rule out mechanical obstruction or kink.
> 2. **Rule Out Postrenal Obstruction**: Perform a bedside bladder scan or renal ultrasound. If retention is present, insert or replace Foley catheter.
> 3. **Assess Hemodynamics**:
>    - *Hypovolemic/Shock*: Give fluid resuscitation (crystalloids).
>    - *Fluid Overloaded (JVP elevated, lung crackles)*: Hold fluids, consider loop diuretics (Furosemide).
> 4. **Review Meds (Stop Nephrotoxins)**: Discontinue NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, aminoglycosides, and hold contrast studies.

---

## 🔍 Pathophysiology & Etiology

The approach to oliguria is divided into three classic categories: **Prerenal** (renal hypoperfusion), **Intrinsic** (parenchymal damage), and **Postrenal** (obstruction).

```
Oliguria Classification
└── 1. Prerenal (Hypoperfusion)
    ├── True Volume Depletion
    └── Effective Volume Depletion
└── 2. Intrinsic (Renal Parenchyma)
    ├── Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)
    ├── Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN)
    └── Glomerulonephritis (GN)
└── 3. Postrenal (Obstruction)
    ├── Bladder Outlet Obstruction
    └── Bilateral Ureter Obstruction
```

## 📊 Diagnostic Indices: Prerenal vs. Intrinsic (ATN)

When postrenal obstruction is ruled out, use urinary indices to differentiate prerenal azotemia from intrinsic AKI (ATN):

| Diagnostic Index | Prerenal Azotemia | Intrinsic AKI (ATN) | Clinical Pearl |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fractional Excretion of Sodium ($FE_{Na}$)** | **$< 1\%$** | **$> 2\%$** | Invalid if patient is on diuretics. |
| **Fractional Excretion of Urea ($FE_{Urea}$)** | **$< 35\%$** | **$> 50\%$** | Use this if patient is taking diuretics. |
| **Urine Sodium ($U_{Na}$)** | $< 20$ mEq/L | $> 40$ mEq/L | Low sodium reflects intact tubular reabsorption. |
| **Urine Osmolality ($U_{Osm}$)** | $> 500$ mOsm/kg | $< 350$ mOsm/kg | Concentrated urine in prerenal; dilute in ATN. |
| **BUN / Creatinine Ratio** | $> 20:1$ | $10-15:1$ | Urea reabsorption is enhanced in hypoperfusion. |
| **Urine Sediment** | Hyaline casts, normal | Muddy brown granular casts, epithelial cells | Muddy brown casts are pathognomonic for ATN. |

$$FE_{Na} = \frac{U_{Na} \times P_{Cr}}{P_{Na} \times U_{Cr}} \times 100\%$$

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

* **Fluid Status Cues**: History of vomiting, [[Diarrhea]], excessive sweating, hemorrhage, or decreased oral intake.
* **Cardiac/Hepatic Disease**: History of congestive heart failure (cardiorenal syndrome) or decompensated liver cirrhosis (hepatorenal syndrome).
* **Nephrotoxic Exposure**: Recent use of NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, or recent IV contrast administration.
* **Obstructive Symptoms**: Hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia, sensation of incomplete emptying (suggests BPH or prostate cancer).
* **Systemic Symptoms**: Fever, rash, arthralgia (suggests drug-induced Acute Interstitial Nephritis or systemic lupus erythematosus).

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Hemodynamic Assessment**: Check blood pressure (look for orthostatic hypotension), heart rate, capillary refill time, and skin turgor.
* **Volume Overload Assessment**: Look for elevated Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP), gallop (S3), pulmonary crackles, and bilateral peripheral [[Edema]].
* **Abdominal/Pelvic Exam**: Palpate the suprapubic area for a distended bladder (dullness to percussion). Check costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness.
* **DRE / Pelvic Exam**: Assess prostate size (BPH) or check for pelvic masses compressing the ureters.

| Category | Common Causes | Dangerous / Can't Miss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Intrinsic** | • Nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides)<br>• Pigment nephropathy (Rhabdomyolysis) | • **Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis (RPGN)**<br>• **Atheroembolic renal disease** (post-catheterization) |
| **Postrenal** | • **Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)**<br>• Bilateral kidney stones | • **Bilateral Ureteral Obstruction** by pelvic malignancy<br>• Obstructed solitary kidney |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Immediate complications of oliguria requiring urgent action:**
> * **Hyperkalemia** (potassium $> 6.5$ mEq/L or EKG changes: peaked T waves, PR prolongation, QRS widening) $\rightarrow$ Requires calcium gluconate, insulin/D5W, and emergency dialysis.
> * **Refractory Volume Overload** causing acute pulmonary edema (severe dyspnea, hypoxia).
> * **Uremic Encephalopathy** (asterixis, altered consciousness) or **Uremic Pericarditis** (friction rub, pleuritic chest pain).
> * Severe metabolic acidosis ($pH < 7.1$ or bicarbonate $< 12$ mEq/L).

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Urgent Biochemistry**:
  * **Serum Electrolytes**: Monitor Potassium, Sodium, Calcium, Phosphate.
  * **BUN and Creatinine**: Assess baseline renal function.
  * **Venous Blood Gas (VBG)**: Check pH, bicarbonate, and lactate.
* **Urine Studies**:
  * **[[Urinalysis]] and Microscopy**: Look for hematuria, proteinuria, and cast types.
    * *RBC casts*: [[Glomerulonephritis]].
    * *WBC casts & Eosinophils*: Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN) or Pyelonephritis.
    * *Muddy brown granular casts*: Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN).
  * **Urine Electrolytes**: Calculate $FE_{Na}$ and $FE_{Urea}$.
* **Imaging**:
  * **Renal Ultrasound**: Look for hydronephrosis (indicates postrenal obstruction), kidney size, and parenchymal echogenicity.

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Address Reversible Causes**:
   - Stop all nephrotoxic agents.
   - If prerenal and volume depleted: Give **fluid challenge** (0.9% NaCl or Acetate/Ringer's lactate).
   - If postrenal: Insert **Foley catheter** or contact urology for nephrostomy tubes/ureteral stents.
2. **Fluid Balance**:
   - If volume-overloaded: Give IV Furosemide (e.g., Furosemide Stress Test: $1.0 - 1.5$ mg/kg IV). If no response within 2 hours, suspect established ATN and prepare for conservative dialysis management.
   - *Do not push fluids if the patient is already fluid-overloaded.*
3. **Indications for Urgent Renal Replacement Therapy (Dialysis Mnemonic: AEIOU)**:
   - **A** - Refractory Metabolic **A**cidosis ($pH < 7.1-7.2$).
   - **E** - Refractory **E**lectrolyte abnormality (Hyperkalemia $> 6.5$ mEq/L or rising rapidly).
   - **I** - **I**ntoxication (toxic alcohols, lithium, salicylates, theophylline).
   - **O** - Volume **O**verload refractory to diuretics.
   - **U** - **U**remia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, neuropathy).

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **Furosemide Stress Test (FST)**: A standardized tool in early AKI to predict progression. In patients with early AKI, administering $1.0$ mg/kg (loop diuretic naive) or $1.5$ mg/kg (prior loop exposure) of IV Furosemide should yield $> 200$ mL of urine output within 2 hours. Failure to meet this threshold indicates severe tubular injury (ATN) and predicts high likelihood of requiring renal replacement therapy.
> **Avoid "Oliguria Fluid Overload"**: Pushing IVF in an anuric/oliguric patient with established ATN who is hemodynamically stable does not "restart" the kidneys. Instead, it leads to pulmonary edema, hypoxia, and increased mortality. If the patient is euvolemic/hypervolemic, restrict fluids.
