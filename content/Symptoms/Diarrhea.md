# 💩 Diarrhea (ท้องเดิน)

> "Diarrhea is classified temporally as acute (<14 days) or chronic (>30 days). The pathophysiological hallmark is a mismatch between fluid absorption and secretion in the intestine."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Approach:**
> 1. **Assess Hydration**: Check for signs of hypovolemia or [[Shock]]. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) is preferred; use IV fluids if severe.
> 2. **Inflammatory (Dysentery) vs. Non-inflammatory**:
>    - *Inflammatory*: High fever, bloody/mucoid stool, severe abdominal pain. Think *Shigella*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, or IBD. **Avoid anti-motility agents (Loperamide).**
>    - *Non-inflammatory*: Watery, large-volume stool, mild pain. Think viral or enterotoxigenic bacteria.
> 3. **Identify Chronic Mechanism**: Differentiate Osmotic (stops with fasting) from Secretory (continues despite fasting).

---

## 🔍 Definition & Physiology

* **Acute Diarrhea**: $<14$ days. Most commonly infectious.
* **Persistent Diarrhea**: $14-30$ days.
* **Chronic Diarrhea**: $>30$ days. Often non-infectious (IBD, IBS, malabsorption).

### 📊 Pathophysiological Classification Table

| Feature | Osmotic Diarrhea | Secretory Diarrhea | Inflammatory Diarrhea |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pathophysiology** | Unabsorbed solutes draw water into bowel lumen. | Active ion secretion ($Cl^-$ or $HCO_3^-$) or impaired absorption. | Mucosal destruction and exudation of blood, protein, pus. |
| **Response to Fasting**| **Diarrhea stops** or decreases significantly. | **Diarrhea continues** unchanged. | Diarrhea continues. |
| **Stool Osmotic Gap**  | **High** ($>50-100$ mOsm/kg) | **Low** ($<50$ mOsm/kg) | Variable |
| **Common Causes** | Lactose intolerance, magnesium ingestion, celiac disease. | Cholera, VIPoma, carcinoid, bile acid malabsorption, laxatives. | Shigella, Salmonella, C. difficile, Crohn's, Ulcerative Colitis. |

$$\text{Stool Osmotic Gap} = 290 - 2 \times (\text{Stool } Na^+ + \text{Stool } K^+)$$

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

* **Onset & Duration**: Sudden onset after eating $\rightarrow$ Food poisoning (*S. aureus* if onset within $1-6$ hrs; *B. cereus* in fried rice).
* **Stool Characteristics**: Watery vs. Bloody/Mucoid (Dysentery) vs. Steatorrhea (greasy, foul-smelling, floats $\rightarrow$ pancreatic insufficiency, celiac).
* **Nocturnal Symptoms**: Diarrhea waking the patient up at night $\rightarrow$ **Organic cause** (secretory or inflammatory).
* **Exposures & History**:
  * Recent antibiotics or hospitalization $\rightarrow$ *Clostridioides difficile* infection.
  * Travel to endemic areas $\rightarrow$ Enterotoxigenic *E. coli* (ETEC), *Giardia* (freshwater streams).
  * High-risk sexual behavior $\rightarrow$ Proctitis (*Neisseria*, Chlamydia), HIV enteropathy.

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Hydration Status**: Assess mucous membranes, skin turgor, blood pressure, and pulse (look for orthostatic changes or shock).
* **Abdomen**:
  * Hyperactive bowel sounds.
  * Tenderness: Diffuse mild tenderness is common. Localized severe tenderness or peritoneal signs $\rightarrow$ Toxic megacolon or bowel perforation (dangerous complications of C. diff or severe colitis).
* **Skin**: Poor turgor, dry skin. Look for dermatitis herpetiformis (Celiac disease), erythema nodosum (IBD).

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Acute Diarrhea (<14 days) | Chronic Diarrhea (>30 days) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Infectious** | • **Viral**: Rotavirus, Norovirus<br>• **Bacterial**: *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Shigella*, *E. coli* (EHEC/ETEC)<br>• **Parasitic**: *Giardia*, *Entamoeba histolytica* | • Parasitic (Giardiasis, Cryptosporidium in HIV)<br>• Tuberculous enteritis |
| **Inflammatory** | • Acute IBD flare | • **Ulcerative Colitis** / **Crohn's Disease** (IBD)<br>• Radiation enteritis |
| **Malabsorptive** | None | • Celiac disease<br>• Chronic pancreatitis (steatorrhea)<br>• Lactose intolerance |
| **Functional / Meds**| • Antibiotic-associated diarrhea<br>• Medication-induced (Metformin, laxatives) | • **Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS-D)**<br>• Diabetic enteropathy |
| **Dangerous / Can't Miss**| • **C. difficile Infection** (pseudomembranous)<br>• **Toxic Megacolon** | • **Colorectal Cancer** (presenting as diarrhea)<br>• Neuroendocrine tumors (VIPoma, Gastrinoma) |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Admit or investigate urgently if any red flags are present:**
> * Signs of severe dehydration or hypovolemic [[Shock]] (SBP $<90$, anuria).
> * High fever ($>38.5^\circ\text{C}$) with severe abdominal pain.
> * Grossly bloody stools (dysentery).
> * Age $\ge 70$ years or severely immunocompromised status (HIV, chemotherapy).
> * Recent antibiotic use or hospitalization (suspect C. diff).
> * Chronic diarrhea with unexplained weight loss and night sweats.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **First-line Screen**:
  * **[[Electrolyte]] Panel**: Look for hypokalemia and metabolic acidosis (due to loss of bicarbonate in stool).
  * **Renal Function (BUN/Cr)**: Assess for AKI secondary to volume loss.
  * **Inflammatory Markers**: CRP, ESR, or Fecal Calprotectin.
* **Stool Studies (Crucial for Acute/Bloody Diarrhea)**:
  * **Stool Microscopy**: Check for RBCs, WBCs (indicates inflammatory diarrhea), and Ova & Parasites (O&P).
  * **Stool Culture**: Identify *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*.
  * **C. difficile Toxin PCR / ELISA**: If antibiotic history.
  * **Stool Shiga Toxin**: If E. coli O157:H7 suspected (bloody diarrhea without fever).
* **Diagnostic Gold Standard (Chronic)**:
  * **Colonoscopy with mucosal biopsies**: Indicated to rule out IBD, microscopic colitis, or malignancy in chronic unexplained diarrhea.

---

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Fluid and Electrolyte Replacement**:
   - Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) contain glucose and sodium, utilizing the sodium-glucose cotransporter (SGLT-1) in the gut to enhance absorption.
   - IV fluids (Ringer's Lactate or Acetar are preferred over Normal Saline to correct accompanying acidosis) if the patient cannot tolerate oral intake or is in shock.
2. **Antidiarrheal Agents**:
   - **Loperamide** ($2-4$ mg): Useful in mild-to-moderate watery diarrhea.
   - **MANDATORY CONTRAINDICATION**: Do **not** give Loperamide to patients with **bloody diarrhea, high fever, or suspected C. diff**. Inhibiting motility traps toxins and bacteria, risking **Toxic Megacolon** and perforation.
3. **Antibiotic Therapy**:
   - *Acute watery*: Usually viral; antibiotics are contraindicated.
   - *Empiric bacterial* (if indicated: severe dysentery, traveler's diarrhea, or immunocompromised): **Ciprofloxacin** $500$ mg BID or **Azithromycin** $500$ mg daily for $3$ days.
   - *C. difficile Infection*: **Oral Vancomycin** $125$ mg QID or **Fidaxomicin** $200$ mg BID for 10 days. *Avoid IV Vancomycin (does not enter bowel lumen).*

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> * **EHEC and HUS Danger**: In patients presenting with bloody diarrhea without fever (suspect Enterohemorrhagic E. coli / EHEC O157:H7), **avoid antibiotics**. Giving antibiotics to EHEC patients triggers release of Shiga toxins, which significantly increases the risk of **Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS)** (thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, AKI).
> * **Hypokalemic Acidosis**: Diarrheal fluid is rich in potassium and bicarbonate. Severe diarrhea characteristically leads to **hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis** with hypokalemia, whereas vomiting leads to metabolic alkalosis.
> * **C. Difficile Resuscitation Trap**: Alcohol-based hand sanitizers do **not** kill C. difficile spores. You must wash hands with soap and water to physically remove the spores from your skin after examining a C. diff patient.
