# 🥚 Proteinuria (โปรตีนในปัสสาวะ)

> "Proteinuria is the abnormal excretion of protein in the urine ($> 150$ mg/day in adults). It is a hallmark of renal disease and an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease and progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD)."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Action:**
> 1. **Identify the Clues**: Look for "frothy" or "foamy" urine on history and bilateral pitting leg [[Edema]] or periorbital puffiness on exam.
> 2. **Confirm and Quantify**:
>    - Perform a spot **Urine Protein-to-Creatinine Ratio (UPCR)** or Urine Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (UACR) on a random urine sample (preferred over cumbersome 24-hour urine collection).
>    - *Nephrotic-range*: UPCR $> 3.5$ g/g (equivalent to $> 3.5$ g/day).
> 3. **Rule out Mimics & Transient Causes**: Assess for fever, strenuous exercise, or acute illness, which can cause transient, benign proteinuria.
> 4. **Check renal function & Albumin**: Assess serum Creatinine, Albumin, and Lipid profile.

---

## 🔍 Pathophysiology & Mechanisms

Proteinuria is divided into four pathophysiological types based on the mechanism of protein entry into the urine:

```
Proteinuria Mechanisms
└── 1. Glomerular Proteinuria
    ├── Increased permeability of glomerular basement membrane
    └── Predominantly Albumin (e.g., Diabetic Nephropathy)
└── 2. Tubular Proteinuria
    ├── Impaired reabsorption of low-molecular-weight proteins
    └── Beta-2 microglobulin, light chains (e.g., AIN)
└── 3. Overflow Proteinuria
    ├── Overproduction of small proteins filters through glomerulus
    └── Exceeds tubular reabsorptive capacity (e.g., Myeloma)
└── 4. Functional / Transient Proteinuria
    ├── Hemodynamic changes altering glomerular blood flow
    └── Benign, self-limiting (e.g., Fever, Exercise)
```

---

## 📊 Comparison of Proteinuria Types

| Feature | Glomerular Proteinuria | Tubular Proteinuria | Overflow Proteinuria |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pathology** | Damage to glomerular filtration barrier (podocytes, GBM). | Proximal tubular injury prevents reabsorption of filtered proteins. | Excess plasma protein filters through normal glomeruli, saturating tubules. |
| **Primary Protein** | **Albumin** | Low-molecular-weight proteins (e.g., $\beta_2$-microglobulin). | Immunoglobulin light chains (Bence-Jones), Myoglobin, Hemoglobin. |
| **Urine Dipstick** | **Highly Sensitive** (detects negatively charged albumin). | **Poorly Sensitive** / Negative. | **Negative** (dipstick does *not* detect light chains or myoglobin). |
| **Etiology** | • Diabetic Nephropathy<br>• Glomerulonephritis<br>• Lupus Nephritis | • Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN)<br>• Fanconi Syndrome<br>• Heavy metal poisoning | • **Multiple Myeloma** (light chains)<br>• Rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin)<br>• Intravascular Hemolysis (hemoglobin) |

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

* **Urine Character**: Has the patient noticed persistent foaming or frothiness in the toilet bowl?
* **Volume Status**: Sudden weight gain, swelling of the eyelids in the morning, or swollen ankles.
* **Secondary Causes & Comorbidities**:
  * Long-standing Diabetes Mellitus or [[Hypertension]].
  * Joint pain, malar rash, photosensitivity (suggests Systemic Lupus Erythematosus / Lupus Nephritis).
  * Bone pain, fatigue, recurrent infections in an elderly patient (suggests Multiple Myeloma).
* **Medication & Toxin Exposure**: Use of NSAIDs, gold salts, penicillamine, or recent exposure to heavy metals.

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **Fluid Overload**: Assess for bilateral, symmetric pitting lower extremity [[Edema]], sacral edema (if bedridden), ascites, and periorbital edema.
* **Cardiovascular**: Measure blood pressure (look for secondary or uncontrolled [[Hypertension]]). Check JVP and lung auscultation for signs of pulmonary edema.
* **Systemic Signs**: Check for malar rash, discoid rash, or oral ulcers (SLE), purpura (vasculitis), or pale conjunctiva ([[Anemia]] secondary to renal failure or myeloma).

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Common Causes | Dangerous / Can't Miss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nephrotic Syndrome**<br>(Protein $>3.5$ g/day,<br>Albumin $<3.0$ g/dL,<br>Edema, Hyperlipidemia) | • Minimal Change Disease (MCD)<br>• Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)<br>• Membranous Nephropathy (MN)<br>• Diabetic Nephropathy | • **Renal Vein Thrombosis** (hypercoagulability complication)<br>• Amyloidosis |
| **Nephritic Syndrome**<br>(Protein $<3.5$ g/day,<br>Hematuria, HTN, Oliguria) | • IgA Nephropathy<br>• Post-infectious Glomerulonephritis | • **Lupus Nephritis**<br>• **Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis (RPGN)** (crescentic) |
| **Non-Glomerular** | • Orthostatic proteinuria (adolescents)<br>• Tubular injury (AIN) | • **Multiple Myeloma** (light chain cast nephropathy) |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Critical presentations of nephrotic/nephritic state requiring immediate admission and specialist workup:**
> * **Nephrotic-range proteinuria** combined with **acute dyspnea, calf swelling, or pleuritic chest pain** (suggests thromboembolism: DVT, PE, or renal vein thrombosis due to antithrombin III loss in urine).
> * **Rapidly rising Creatinine** with macroscopic hematuria ("coca-cola" urine) and oliguria (suggests RPGN; requires urgent pulse methylprednisolone).
> * Severe hypertension refractory to therapy, associated with encephalopathy or heart failure.
> * Severe hypoalbuminemia ($< 2.0$ g/dL) predisposing to spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or severe infections.

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Urine Studies**:
  * **[[Urinalysis]] (Dipstick)**: Semi-quantitative assessment of albumin. Note: Alkaline urine ($pH > 8$) or contrast media can cause false positives.
  * **Urine Sulfosalicylic Acid (SSA) Test**: Detects *all* proteins (including Bence-Jones). An SSA-positive, dipstick-negative urine indicates **Multiple Myeloma**.
  * **Spot Urine Protein-to-Creatinine Ratio (UPCR)**:
    $$\text{UPCR (g/g)} \approx \text{24-hour protein excretion (g/day)}$$
  * **Urine Electrophoresis (UPEP)**: Check for monoclonal light chains (M-spike).
* **Serum Chemistry**:
  * **Renal Function (BUN, Creatinine)** & **Electrolytes**.
  * **Albumin and Total Protein**: Confirm hypoalbuminemia.
  * **Lipid Profile**: Check for hypercholesterolemia (classic in nephrotic syndrome).
* **Immunological Screening** (to identify secondary glomerular disease):
  * ANA, anti-dsDNA (SLE), C3 and C4 levels (depleted in post-streptococcal GN, lupus nephritis, membranoproliferative GN).
  * Hepatitis B, C, and HIV serologies (associated with membranous, membranoproliferative, and FSGS respectively).
* **Renal Biopsy**:
  * **Indications**: Unexplained nephrotic syndrome in adults, rapidly progressive nephritic syndrome, or suspected lupus nephritis. (Typically avoided in children with suspected Minimal Change Disease as they are treated empirically with steroids).

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **The Dipstick Fallacy**: A standard urine dipstick is highly sensitive only to albumin. If an elderly patient presents with severe renal failure, bone pain, and a negative urine dipstick, do *not* assume they do not have proteinuria. Order an SSA test or a **Urine Protein Electrophoresis (UPEP)** to look for Bence-Jones proteins (light chains), which the dipstick will completely miss.
> **Orthostatic Proteinuria**: A benign condition common in children and young adults. Proteinuria is present only when the patient is upright. Diagnosis is confirmed by comparing a split urine collection: a first-morning void (collected immediately upon waking, representing recumbent posture, which should be normal $< 50$ mg) and a daytime void (upright posture, which shows elevated protein).
