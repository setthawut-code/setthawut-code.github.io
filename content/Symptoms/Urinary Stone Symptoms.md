# 🪨 Urinary Stone Symptoms (นิ่วในทางเดินปัสสาวะ)

> "Urinary stone symptoms (nephrolithiasis and ureterolithiasis) typically present as acute, excruciating, unilateral flank pain (renal colic) that radiates to the groin, often accompanied by microscopic or macroscopic [[Hematuria]] and autonomic symptoms like nausea and vomiting."

---

## 🎯 30-Second Rounding Guide
> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Immediate Ward Action:**
> 1. **Rule Out Obstruction + Infection**: If the patient has a stone AND fever, chills, or hypotension, this is a **urological emergency (obstructed pyelonephritis)**. Arrange urgent decompression (Double-J stent or percutaneous nephrostomy).
> 2. **Optimize Pain Control**:
>    - **NSAIDs (IV Ketorolac / Diclofenac)** are first-line. They reduce ureteral spasm and are superior to opioids.
>    - Use Opioids (Morphine) if NSAIDs are contraindicated (e.g., in [[Acute Kidney Injury]] or gastrointestinal bleeding).
> 3. **Order Diagnostic Imaging**:
>    - **Non-contrast CT Abdomen & Pelvis (CT KUB)** is the gold standard.
>    - Use renal ultrasound if the patient is pregnant or a child.
> 4. **Assess Stone Size**: Stones $< 5$ mm pass spontaneously in $> 80\%$ of cases; stones $> 10$ mm rarely pass without intervention.

---

## 🔍 Pathophysiology & Pain Migration

The location and character of renal colic pain correlate directly with the migration of the stone down the urinary tract:

```
Ureteral Stone Migration & Pain Sites
└── 1. Ureteropelvic Junction (UPJ) / Renal Pelvis
    └── Dull, constant flank pain; radiates forward; CVA tenderness
└── 2. Mid-ureter (Crossing Pelvic Brim)
    └── Flank pain radiating to lower abdomen; mimics appendicitis/diverticulitis
└── 3. Ureterovesical Junction (UVJ)
    └── Pain radiates to groin, scrotum, or labia; causes dysuria and urgency
```

## 📊 Comparison of Urinary Stone Types

Understanding stone composition is key for long-term prevention and immediate management:

| Stone Type | Frequency | Radiopacity | Urine pH | Crystal Morphology | Key Risk Factors & Treatment |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Calcium Oxalate** | **$75-80\%$** | Radiopaque | Variable / Acidic | Envelope / Dumbbell | • Hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria (vitamin C excess).<br>• *Tx*: Hydration, thiazide diuretics, low sodium diet. |
| **Calcium Phosphate** | $10-15\%$ | Radiopaque | **Alkaline** ($>6.5$) | Wedge-shaped prisms | • Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA) Type 1, hyperparathyroidism.<br>• *Tx*: Address underlying cause. |
| **Struvite** (Triple Phos) | $10-15\%$ | Radiopaque (Staghorn) | **Alkaline** ($>7.2$) | Coffin-lid | • Infection by urease-producing bacteria (*Proteus, Klebsiella*).<br>• *Tx*: Complete surgical removal + antibiotics. |
| **Uric Acid** | $5-10\%$ | **Radiolucent** (on X-ray) | **Acidic** ($<5.5$) | Rhomboid / Rosette | • Gout, high purine diet, tumor lysis syndrome.<br>• *Tx*: **Urine alkalinization** with potassium citrate. |
| **Cystine** | $<1\%$ | Poorly radiopaque | Acidic | **Hexagonal** (highly specific) | • Autosomal recessive cystinuria defect.<br>• *Tx*: High hydration, urine alkalinization, thiol agents. |

## 🗣️ Key History Taking

* **Pain Characteristics**: Acute onset of severe, colicky, unilateral flank pain. Does the pain radiate to the lower abdomen, groin, testicle, or labia?
* **Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (LUTS)**: Dysuria, frequency, urgency, or straining (suggests UVJ stone irritating bladder wall).
* **Autonomic Symptoms**: Significant nausea, vomiting, or diaphoresis (common due to shared splanchnic innervation of the kidney and stomach).
* **Prior History**: Personal history of nephrolithiasis, gout, recurrent UTIs, or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD increases oxalate absorption $\rightarrow$ calcium oxalate stones).
* **Diet and Medications**: Inadequate fluid intake, high sodium diet, high oxalate diet (spinach, tea, nuts), or medications like loop diuretics or protease inhibitors (Indinavir).

---

## 🩺 Physical Examination

* **General Appearance**: Patients with renal colic are typically **agitated, pacing, and unable to find a comfortable position** (contrasting with peritonitis, where patients lie completely still).
* **Abdomen**: Generally soft and non-tender, or with mild deep tenderness on the affected side. *Absence of peritoneal signs* (guarding, rebound).
* **Costovertebral Angle (CVA)**: Striking tenderness to percussion (Lloyd's sign) on the affected side.
* **Vitals**: Assess for fever and tachycardia (warning signs of secondary pyelonephritis/urosepsis).

## 📂 Differential Diagnosis

| Category | Renal / Ureteral Pathology | Extra-Renal Mimics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common** | • Acute Pyelonephritis (fever, pyuria)<br>• Papillary necrosis | • Musculoskeletal back pain (positional)<br>• Herpetic neuralgia (pre-eruptive zoster) |
| **Gastrointestinal** | None | • **Acute Appendicitis** (mimics right mid-ureteral stone)<br>• **Acute Diverticulitis** (mimics left mid-ureteral stone)<br>• Biliary colic |
| **Dangerous / Can't Miss**| • **Obstructed Pyelonephritis** (urosepsis)<br>• Renal infarction | • **Ruptured Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)**<br>• Ectopic pregnancy / Ovarian torsion |

## 🚨 Red Flags

> [!CAUTION]
> **Immediate Urological Consultation and Intervention (Decompression) is indicated if:**
> * **Urosepsis**: Flank pain + fever ($>38.5^\circ\text{C}$), chills, tachycardia, or hypotension in the presence of an obstructing stone.
> * **Acute Kidney Injury** or **Anuria** (occurs in bilateral stone obstruction or a stone obstructing a solitary kidney).
> * Intractable pain or persistent vomiting failing oral analgesics/antiemetics.
> * Pediatric or pregnant patients with severe symptoms.

---

## 🧪 Investigations

* **Imaging Studies**:
  * **Non-contrast CT Abdomen and Pelvis (CT KUB)**: Gold standard. High sensitivity ($>95\%$) and specificity. Detects stone size, location, and secondary signs of obstruction (hydronephrosis, perinephric stranding).
  * **Renal Ultrasound**: Preferred in pregnant patients and children. Demonstrates hydronephrosis and shadows of large stones, but poor for small ureteral stones ($< 5$ mm).
  * **KUB X-ray**: Insensitive for small stones, radiolucent uric acid stones, or stones overlying bony structures.
* **Urine Studies**:
  * **[[Urinalysis]] and Microscopy**: Look for microscopic hematuria (present in $85\%$ of patients), pyuria, urine pH, and specific crystals (e.g., hexagonal cystine, coffin-lid struvite).
* **Laboratory Panels**:
  * **BUN, Creatinine, Electrolytes**: Evaluate for acute kidney injury.
  * **CBC**: Leukocytosis suggests infection.
  * **Blood Cultures**: If fever or septic features are present.

---

## 🏥 Management Principles

1. **Analgesia**:
   - **First-line**: NSAIDs (e.g., Ketorolac 15-30 mg IV or Diclofenac 75 mg IM). They decrease ureteral smooth muscle tone and reduce glomerular filtration rate, lowering intracapsular pressure and relieving colic.
   - **Second-line**: Opioids (Morphine 2-5 mg IV or Fentanyl) if NSAIDs are contraindicated (AKI, GI bleed, third-trimester pregnancy).
2. **Medical Expulsive Therapy (MET)**:
   - For stones **$5 - 10$ mm** without complications.
   - Prescribe **Tamsulosin 0.4 mg PO daily** for up to 4 weeks. It relaxes distal ureteral smooth muscle, accelerating stone passage.
3. **Urological Intervention Indications**:
   - **Urgent Decompression**: Double-J (JJ) stent placement or Percutaneous Nephrostomy (PCN) for obstructed pyelonephritis/sepsis or anuric AKI. *Do not attempt lithotripsy in an active infection.*
   - **Elective / Semi-urgent Intervention**: For stones $> 10$ mm or those failing to pass after 4-6 weeks of conservative therapy. Options include Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL), Ureteroscopy (URS) with laser lithotripsy, or Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL) for large staghorn calculi.

---

## 💡 Clinical Pearls

> [!TIP]
> **AAA Mimicry**: In an elderly patient ($> 60$ years old) presenting with new-onset severe flank/back pain and hematuria, do *not* assume it is a simple kidney stone. A leaking or **ruptured Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)** can present identically due to retroperitoneal blood tracking. Perform immediate bedside ultrasound or CT scan to rule out AAA.
> **Staghorn Calculus Infection**: Struvite stones are caused by urease-producing bacteria like *Proteus mirabilis*. Urease splits urea into ammonia, raising urine pH ($> 7.2$) and causing precipitation of magnesium ammonium phosphate. These stones can grow to fill the entire renal pelvis (Staghorn). They cannot be sterilized with antibiotics alone; complete surgical removal is mandatory to prevent recurrent sepsis and renal destruction.
